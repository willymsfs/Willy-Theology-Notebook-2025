# 01 — What a Council Is and Why Vatican II Happened

> [!info] Assumed knowledge: none
> This note starts from zero. If you already know what an ecumenical council is, skip to §3.

## 1. What is an ecumenical council?

An **ecumenical council** is a meeting of *all* the bishops of the world, called and confirmed by the Pope, to settle matters of faith, morals, or Church order. "Ecumenical" here means *worldwide* (Greek *oikoumene*, the inhabited earth) — not "about Christian unity", which is what the word usually means today.

Three things make a council ecumenical:
1. **The Pope calls it** (or confirms it afterwards).
2. **All bishops are invited** — not a region, not a religious order.
3. **The Pope approves its decisions.** Without this, the documents have no binding force.

There have been **21** in two thousand years. That is roughly one per century — they are rare, and each is called to solve a specific crisis.

| Council | Year | Called because |
|---------|------|----------------|
| Nicaea I | 325 | Arius denied Christ's full divinity |
| Chalcedon | 451 | Confusion about Christ's two natures |
| Trent | 1545–63 | The Protestant Reformation |
| Vatican I | 1869–70 | Rationalism, liberalism; defined papal infallibility |
| **Vatican II** | **1962–65** | **See §3 — and this is the odd one out** |

> [!warning] The thing that makes Vatican II unusual
> Every previous council was called to **condemn an error**. They produced *canons* ending in *anathema sit* ("let him be cut off"). Vatican II condemned **nothing** and issued **no anathemas**. It was called to renew, not to condemn. This single fact explains most of what follows — and most of the later arguments.

## 2. Why "Vatican II"?

Because Vatican I (1869–70) never finished. It was interrupted when Italian troops seized Rome in September 1870 and was suspended, never formally closed. It had time to define **papal infallibility** and papal primacy — but not to say anything about **bishops**, whose role was next on the agenda.

> [!tip] Remember this
> Vatican I gave a doctrine of the Pope with no matching doctrine of the bishops. For ninety years the Church had a lopsided picture of its own leadership. Vatican II's teaching on **collegiality** ([[05 - Lumen Gentium - The Church]]) is the completion of that unfinished business. This is why collegiality was so fiercely fought over.

## 3. Who called it, and why

**Pope John XXIII** (Angelo Roncalli), elected October 1958 at the age of 76. He was widely expected to be a caretaker — a placeholder after the long, austere reign of Pius XII. He was not.

On **25 January 1959**, barely three months into his pontificate, at the Basilica of St Paul Outside the Walls, he announced to a small group of cardinals that he would convoke an ecumenical council. The story is that they responded with complete silence.

He gave two Italian words for what he wanted:

> [!quote] The two words
> **Aggiornamento** — "updating", literally "bringing up to today". Making the Church's language, worship, and structures able to reach modern people.
>
> **Ressourcement** — French, "return to the sources". Going back behind the manuals of recent centuries to Scripture, the Fathers, and the ancient liturgy.

These are **not the same thing**, and confusing them is a standard exam error:

| | Direction | Motto | Risk if taken alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aggiornamento** | Forward, toward the present | "Open the windows" | Dissolving into the culture |
| **Ressourcement** | Backward, toward the origins | "Back to the sources" | Antiquarianism, romantic nostalgia |

The Council's actual method was to hold them **together**: recover the older, richer tradition *precisely in order to* speak to the present. Nearly every document does this.

## 4. The opening speech — *Gaudet Mater Ecclesia*

On **11 October 1962**, John XXIII opened the Council with an address whose Latin title means "Mother Church rejoices". It set the tone in three moves:

1. **He rebuked the pessimists.** He spoke of "prophets of doom" who saw only ruin in the modern age, and said he disagreed with them.
2. **He distinguished the deposit from its expression.** The substance of the ancient faith is one thing; the way it is formulated is another. This sentence licensed everything the Council went on to do.
3. **He chose mercy over severity.** The Church would meet errors "by the medicine of mercy rather than by taking up arms of severity."

> [!quote] The pivotal sentence
> The deposit of faith — the truths contained in our venerable doctrine — is one thing; the manner in which they are expressed, while retaining the same meaning and sense, is another.

Everything the Council changed lives inside that distinction. Nothing of the *faith* changed. A great deal of the *expression* did.

## 5. So why did it happen *then*?

Four pressures had built up over the preceding century:

- **Intellectual.** Biblical scholarship, patristic recovery, and the liturgical movement had quietly produced a generation of theologians whose work the official manuals could not absorb. Several had been silenced or disciplined in the 1940s–50s; they turned up at the Council as expert advisers. → [[02 - The Church Before the Council]]
- **Political.** Two world wars, the collapse of Christian Europe as a political order, the Cold War, and the atom bomb. The Church's old alliance with throne and altar was gone.
- **Global.** Decolonisation. The Church was becoming genuinely worldwide — Africa, Asia, Latin America — while its liturgy, law, and theology remained thoroughly European.
- **Pastoral.** Ordinary Catholics attended a Mass in a language they did not speak, watched by a priest whose back was turned. Participation had become spectating.

## 6. The shape of what followed

- **Announced:** 25 January 1959
- **Preparation:** 1959–1962 — commissions drafted about 70 preparatory texts (*schemata*), mostly under Roman curial control
- **Opened:** 11 October 1962
- **Four sessions**, each running roughly October to December: 1962, 1963, 1964, 1965
- **John XXIII died** 3 June 1963, after only the first session. **Paul VI** (Giovanni Battista Montini) was elected 21 June 1963 and immediately announced the Council would continue.
- **Closed:** 8 December 1965
- **Output:** 16 documents — 4 constitutions, 9 decrees, 3 declarations

> [!warning] The first surprise
> Of the ~70 prepared texts, the Council **rejected almost all of them** within its first weeks and started over. What the bishops actually produced bears little resemblance to what the preparatory commissions handed them. How that happened is [[03 - How the Council Ran]] and [[09 - The Turning Points]].
