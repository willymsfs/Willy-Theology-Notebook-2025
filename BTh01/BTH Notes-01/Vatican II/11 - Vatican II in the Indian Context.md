# 11 — Vatican II in the Indian Context

> [!info] Why this note exists
> For an Indian student this is not an appendix. Vatican II is the reason an Indian Catholic theology became officially possible at all. Examiners in Indian seminaries expect this material.

## 1. The four doors the Council opened

Indian theology did not invent its warrants; it found them in the conciliar texts.

| Door | Text | What it authorised |
|------|------|--------------------|
| **Liturgical adaptation** | SC 37–40 | The Church "has no wish to impose a rigid uniformity in matters which do not involve the faith"; provision for adapting rites to the genius of peoples |
| **Respect for other religions** | *Nostra Aetate* 2 | The Church "rejects nothing that is true and holy in these religions"; Hinduism and Buddhism named with respect |
| **Culture is not owned by the West** | GS 58 | The Church "is not bound exclusively and indissolubly to any race or nation, any particular way of life or any customary way of life recent or ancient" |
| **Local churches, not colonies** | *Ad Gentes* 22 | Young churches should borrow from the customs, wisdom, learning, arts and sciences of their peoples; theological investigation in each major socio-cultural area |

> [!quote] *Ad Gentes* 22 — the charter sentence for Indian theology
> From the customs and traditions of their people, from their wisdom and their learning, from their arts and sciences, these Churches borrow all those things which can contribute to the glory of the Creator, the revelation of the Saviour's grace, or the proper arrangement of Christian life.

## 2. What Indian Catholics faced before the Council

- A liturgy in Latin, in a culture with its own vast liturgical and devotional inheritance.
- Church architecture, vestment, music and art in European idiom.
- Theology taught from Roman manuals, addressing European questions, in categories drawn from Greek philosophy.
- Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism and Islam — the religious world of the overwhelming majority of Indians — treated in official theology as outside the order of salvation.
- The long shadow of the **Malabar Rites** controversy: Roberto de Nobili's 17th-century experiment in adopting *sannyasi* life and Indian custom had been curtailed by Rome. Adaptation had been tried and stopped.

## 3. The reception: what actually happened

**All India Seminar, Bangalore, May 1969** — "The Church in India Today". A large national gathering of bishops, priests, religious and laity to receive and apply the Council. Widely treated as the founding event of the post-conciliar Indian Church.

**NBCLC, Bangalore** — the National Biblical, Catechetical and Liturgical Centre, established in the late 1960s, became the engine of Indian liturgical and catechetical renewal.

**The Twelve Points of Adaptation (1969)** — the CBCI sought and obtained Roman approval for a set of Indian adaptations to the liturgy, drawing directly on SC 37–40. They included:

- *anjali hasta* — the folded-hands gesture — as a liturgical posture
- *panchanga pranam* — prostration
- removing footwear in the sanctuary
- *aarti* — the offering of light
- use of oil lamps (*deepa*) rather than only candles
- incense in Indian style
- garlanding the Gospel book, the altar, the ministers
- the *thali/taali* and Indian ritual elements in marriage
- Indian musical forms — *bhajan*, *kirtan*, Indian instruments
- seating on the floor; Indian-style low altar
- Indian architectural idiom, including the *mandapam* form

**The Indian Rite Mass / Indian Anaphora** — an experimental eucharistic prayer using Indian religious vocabulary and *sat-chit-ananda* categories. Approved for experimental use and subsequently restricted — a live example of the tension in the next section.

**Ashram movement** — Christian ashrams as a Christian form of Indian monastic life: Shantivanam (Jules Monchanin, Henri Le Saux/Abhishiktananda, later Bede Griffiths), Kurisumala (Francis Mahieu, Bede Griffiths). Christian *sannyasa*.

**Indian theological voices** — the Council made possible the work of theologians such as **D. S. Amalorpavadass** (NBCLC), **Raimon Panikkar**, **Samuel Rayan**, and **George M. Soares-Prabhu**, whose Indian biblical hermeneutic reads Scripture from within India's own religious and social reality.

## 4. Dalit theology — the Indian counter-question

> [!warning] Do not present inculturation as the whole Indian story
> Inculturation as practised drew heavily on **Sanskritic, Brahmanical, Vedantic** categories — *sat-chit-ananda*, *sannyasa*, Upanishadic language. But the majority of Indian Catholics are **Dalit** or Adivasi. Dalit theologians ask a sharp question: whose India is being inculturated?

The critique: adopting the symbols of the tradition that produced caste can baptise caste along with it. A Church that takes on Brahmanical forms while its members face untouchability has inculturated the oppressor's culture, not the people's.

Dalit theology therefore reads *Gaudium et Spes* rather than *Ad Gentes* as its charter — GS 1's "especially those who are poor or in any way afflicted" — and shifts the question from **culture** to **liberation**.

> [!tip] The strong exam answer
> Indian reception of Vatican II runs on **two tracks**: an *inculturation* track (Ad Gentes, SC 37–40, Nostra Aetate — dialogue with Indian religious tradition) and a *liberation* track (Gaudium et Spes — caste, poverty, Dalit and Adivasi reality). Presenting only the first is the commonest weakness in Indian seminary answers. Name both, and name the tension between them.

## 5. Interreligious dialogue in the Indian setting

*Nostra Aetate* has particular force where Christians are roughly 2% of the population and live among Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Buddhists and Jains.

The four classical forms of dialogue, as later specified by the Church:

| Form | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Dialogue of life** | Neighbours sharing joys and sorrows, festivals, ordinary existence |
| **Dialogue of action** | Collaboration for justice, development, communal harmony |
| **Dialogue of theological exchange** | Scholars deepening understanding of each other's traditions |
| **Dialogue of religious experience** | Sharing prayer, contemplation, the search for the Absolute — the ashram movement's own ground |

Contemporary tension worth naming: dialogue proceeds in a context of rising communal politics and anti-conversion legislation in several states. *Nostra Aetate*'s stance is not merely academic in India; it bears on whether Christians are received as neighbours or as agents of conversion.

## 6. The unresolved question

How far may adaptation go before the faith itself is altered? SC 37 draws the line at "matters which do not involve the faith or the good of the whole community" — but locating that line in practice is exactly the difficulty. The restriction of the Indian Anaphora shows Rome and the Indian Church locating it differently.
