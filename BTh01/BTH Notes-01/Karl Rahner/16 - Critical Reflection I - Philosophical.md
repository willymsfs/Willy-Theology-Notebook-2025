## Critique 1 — "This is not Thomism" (Gilson, Fabro, Van Steenberghen)

**(a) Who.** Étienne Gilson and the historical-Thomist school; **Cornelio Fabro**, *La svolta antropologica di Karl Rahner* (1974, "Rahner's anthropological turn"); Fernand Van Steenberghen.

**(b) The argument.**
- Rahner reads Aquinas through **Maréchal's Kantian lens**, and Maréchal through Heidegger. The result is a modern transcendental subject dressed in Thomist vocabulary.
- For Aquinas, the intellect is *receptive*: it abstracts from what is given. For Rahner, the intellect is *productive*: its own a priori dynamism (*Vorgriff*) constitutes the horizon within which objects appear. That is Kant's Copernican revolution, not Aquinas's realism.
- Fabro presses further: Rahner's "anthropological turn" inverts the order of theology. Aquinas begins from God (*Prima Pars*); Rahner begins from the human subject and reaches God as the term of the subject's dynamism. This is not development but reversal.

**(c) Reply.**
- Rahner does not claim to be doing historical exegesis of Aquinas. *Geist in Welt* is an avowedly **systematic retrieval** — asking what Aquinas's doctrine of the *conversio ad phantasma* implies once the modern question of the subject has been raised and cannot be un-raised.
- The receptive/productive contrast is overdrawn: Aquinas's *intellectus agens* is already an a priori active principle. Rahner claims to be radicalising it, not replacing it.
- Beginning from the subject is a **pedagogical and apologetic** order (*ordo inveniendi*), not an ontological one (*ordo essendi*). God remains first in being.

**(d) Verdict.** The historical criticism is **largely correct and largely beside the point**. Rahner is not offering a commentary; he is offering a philosophy. The question is not "is this Aquinas?" but "is it true?" — and on that Fabro's deeper charge (the inversion of theological order) still needs answering, and merges into Balthasar's → [[17 - Critical Reflection II - Theological]].

## Critique 2 — The *Vorgriff* does not prove God (the Kantian objection)

**(b) The argument.**
- Rahner infers from a **dynamism** of the intellect toward the unlimited to an **actually existing infinite term**. But Kant's *Transcendental Dialectic* is precisely a demonstration that reason's unavoidable drive toward the unconditioned licenses no inference to a real unconditioned object. The idea of the infinite is a **regulative** ideal, not a proof of an infinite being.
- Alternative explanations are available: the "openness" may be a formal feature of negation (the mind's ability to say "not this") rather than a positive orientation to Being; or, deflationarily, a byproduct of linguistic recursion.
- The move looks like a disguised **ontological argument**: from the structure of thought to the existence of its object.

**(c) Reply.**
- The *Vorgriff* is not an inference from a concept to an existent; it is a claim that the **actual functioning** of judgement presupposes a real horizon. A horizon that was merely nothing could not function as a measure by which the finite is *recognised* as finite.
- Rahner's is a **retorsion** argument: to deny the *Vorgriff* one must make a judgement, and judgement operates by it. Denial is performatively self-defeating.

**(d) Verdict.** The retorsion argument has genuine force against a *thoroughgoing* denial, but the critic can grant the transcendental horizon while denying it is **God** — it might be the totality of the possible, or the merely formal notion of being. Rahner's identification of the horizon with the God of Christian revelation is a **theological** step, not a philosophical proof. Rahner sometimes acknowledges this; his readers frequently do not.

## Critique 3 — Onto-theology (Heidegger, Marion)

**(a) Who.** Heidegger's charge that Western metaphysics conflates Being with a highest being; developed theologically by **Jean-Luc Marion**, *Dieu sans l'être* (*God Without Being*, 1982).

**(b) The argument.**
- If God is the term of the *Vorgriff auf esse*, then God is thought **within the horizon of being** — the horizon has become the condition under which God may appear. God is measured by our categories.
- Marion: God must be thought as **gift and love** *before* being; the icon (which gives itself and cannot be mastered) rather than the idol (which reflects the gaze back and stops at what the seer can hold). Rahner's horizon is arguably an idol of the concept.

**(c) Reply.**
- Rahner's God is **absolute mystery** (*Geheimnis*) — explicitly incomprehensible, never an object, never grasped, and remaining incomprehensible even in the beatific vision ([[06 - God's Self-Communication and Quasi-Formal Causality]]). This is precisely a refusal of onto-theology.
- The *Vorgriff* terminates in a horizon that **cannot be objectified**. That is much closer to Marion's "saturated phenomenon" than Marion allows.

**(d) Verdict.** Rahner is a **weaker case of onto-theology than Marion suggests**, because his doctrine of incomprehensibility does the very work Marion demands. But the vocabulary of *esse* keeps the charge alive rhetorically. A good essay notes that the two are closer than the polemic implies.

## Critique 4 — The abstract, unhistorical subject (Metz)

**(a) Who.** **Johann Baptist Metz** — Rahner's own doctoral student and closest friendly critic. *Faith in History and Society* (1977); *Theology of the World* (1968).

**(b) The argument.**
- The transcendental subject is **timeless, classless, genderless, bodiless** — an idealist construct. Real human beings are situated in histories of oppression and privilege.
- Transcendental theology cannot generate a **critique of society**. It reconciles the subject with a mystery already present, rather than mobilising resistance against present suffering.
- Metz proposes instead: **memoria passionis, mortis et resurrectionis Jesu Christi** — the "**dangerous memory**" of the suffering of Jesus and of history's victims, which interrupts rather than integrates. Theology must be **narrative, practical and political**, not transcendental.
- The sharpest form: transcendental theology cannot say why the suffering of the dead matters, because it has no category for **unredeemed history**.

**(c) Reply.**
- Rahner **accepted much of this**. He affirmed a "mystical-political" understanding of Christianity, insisted transcendentality is always mediated **categorically** and historically ([[04 - The Transcendental Method in Theology]]), and defended Gutiérrez publicly in 1984.
- He denied that his method entailed political quietism: the same self-communication that grounds prayer grounds the imperative of love of neighbour, which is *unavoidably* social.

**(d) Verdict.** **The strongest critique in this note.** Rahner's concession is genuine but comes as a supplement, not a reconstruction. The system's centre of gravity remains the individual subject before mystery. Metz's point is not that Rahner is wrong but that he is **insufficient** — and that judgement has been broadly accepted, including in India → [[18 - Rahner in the Indian Context]].

## Critique 5 — The linguistic turn (Kerr, Wittgenstein)

**(a) Who.** **Fergus Kerr OP**, *Theology after Wittgenstein* (1986); also *Immortal Longings* (1997).

**(b) The argument.**
- Rahner's anthropology posits a **self prior to language and community** — a subject that has a pre-linguistic transcendental experience which language then expresses.
- Wittgenstein's private-language argument denies this. There is no meaning, and no self-relation, prior to public linguistic practice. The self is constituted *in* language and community, not before them.
- Kerr detects a residual **Cartesianism**: an inner theatre of consciousness, the "mentalist" picture, dressed in Thomist clothes.

**(c) Reply.**
- Rahner's subject is never a *disembodied* subject: he insists relentlessly on *spirit in world*, on the *conversio ad phantasma*, on the body as real symbol. This is anti-Cartesian at the root ([[02 - Philosophical Foundations I - Spirit in the World]]).
- Transcendental experience is **not a private inner episode**. It is the unthematic structure of ordinary, public, embodied acts — of loving, forgiving, hoping. It has no content that could be a "private object."

**(d) Verdict.** Kerr's diagnosis is **partly deflected**, because Rahner's spirit-in-world doctrine anticipates it. But Kerr's deeper point stands: Rahner's *rhetoric* of interiority, depth and the "innermost core" invites the mentalist reading, and his followers often took it. A theology of the symbol ought to have been more resistant to interiorism than Rahner's actually was.

## Critique 6 — Experiential-expressivism (Lindbeck)

**(a) Who.** **George Lindbeck**, *The Nature of Doctrine* (1984), which names Rahner (with Schleiermacher and Tillich) as the paradigm of the **experiential-expressive** model of religion.

**(b) The argument.**
- Lindbeck's typology: **cognitive-propositional** (doctrines as truth-claims), **experiential-expressive** (doctrines as symbolic expressions of a prior universal inner experience), **cultural-linguistic** (religions as language-games that *form* experience).
- On the middle model, which he attributes to Rahner: all religions express one core experience differently — which makes doctrine secondary, and makes "anonymous Christianity" almost trivially true, since everyone has the core experience.
- Lindbeck's counter: **there is no religion-neutral experience**. Learning a religion is learning a language; the language shapes what can be experienced at all. A Buddhist's *nirvāṇa* and a Christian's *grace* are not two expressions of one thing.

**(c) Reply.**
- Lindbeck's classification is a **caricature**. Rahner never says doctrine is *merely* expression: his whole symbol ontology says expression is **constitutive** and that the categorical is **necessary**, not decorative ([[07 - The Theology of the Symbol - The Core Essay]]).
- More sharply: Rahner's position **anticipates** the cultural-linguistic model. The claim that grace must take social, ritual, historical form ([[11 - Anonymous Christians and the Non-Christian Religions]], Thesis 2) is precisely a claim that religions *form* the way grace is lived.
- Lindbeck's model has its own difficulty: if religions are incommensurable language-games, interreligious understanding and God's universal salvific will both become hard to state.

**(d) Verdict.** Lindbeck's typology **misplaces Rahner**, and the symbol ontology is Rahner's best defence — a real symbol is not the *expression of* a prior reality but the reality's own self-realisation. Still, the challenge to identify the transcendental experience *without* borrowing Christian vocabulary remains unmet, and may be unmeetable in principle.

## Critique 7 — Style and unfalsifiability

**(b) The argument.**
- The prose is famously impenetrable: chains of subordinate clauses, coined compounds, qualifications on qualifications. For a theology whose stated aim is **mystagogy**, this is not a trivial defect — it is a performative contradiction.
- Systematically: several central terms (*quasi*-formal causality, *unthematic* experience, *anonymous* Christianity) are constructed so that no possible observation counts against them. A theory immune to counter-evidence buys its safety at the price of content.

**(c) Reply.** Transcendental claims are *by nature* not empirically falsifiable — they state conditions of possibility, and are tested by **retorsion** (can you coherently deny them while doing what you are doing?), not by observation. On style: Rahner wrote prayers, hymns and homilies in an entirely different register, so the difficulty is a feature of the genre, not of the man.

**(d) Verdict.** The style criticism is **fair and worth stating plainly**. The unfalsifiability criticism is **partly answered** by the nature of transcendental argument, but it correctly identifies why Rahner's system is so hard to argue with — and why arguments about him often become arguments about the method rather than about the claims.
