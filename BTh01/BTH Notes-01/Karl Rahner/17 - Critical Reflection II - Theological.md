## Critique 1 — Balthasar: the anthropological reduction

**(a) Who.** **Hans Urs von Balthasar** — fellow Jesuit (until 1950), fellow student of Erich Przywara, and Rahner's great counter-pole. *Cordula oder der Ernstfall* (1966; ET *The Moment of Christian Witness*); *The Theology of Karl Barth*; *Love Alone is Credible*; the *Herrlichkeit* trilogy.

**(b) The argument.**
1. **Method.** Rahner begins from the human subject's a priori structure. Revelation is then received into a framework it did not create, and is measured by categories established in advance. Balthasar's alternative: begin from the **form** (*Gestalt*) of revelation, which is self-authenticating in its beauty, as a masterpiece imposes itself and teaches the viewer how to look.
2. **The blunting of the *Ernstfall*.** If everyone is already anonymously graced, **martyrdom** — the "serious case," the moment of witness unto blood — becomes a vivid illustration of a truth that would have held anyway. Cordula, the legendary companion of Ursula who at first fled and then returned to die, is Balthasar's emblem: the point where Christianity is either everything or nothing.
3. **The Cross.** Rahner's Cross **manifests** the definitive acceptance of God's self-gift. Balthasar's Cross **achieves** something — the Son bears godforsakenness, descends into hell, enters the abyss of the Father's absence. Rahner's soteriology, on this reading, has no room for Holy Saturday.
4. **Aesthetics vs. transcendence.** Beauty, glory, form and drama are near-absent from Rahner; for Balthasar they are the very mode in which God is known.

**(c) Reply.**
- Rahner's a priori is **formal and empty**: it specifies that revelation must be receivable, not what it will contain. He explicitly denies deducing Christ ([[10 - Christology - Self-Communication and Evolution]]).
- Universality of the offer does not lessen the **cost** of an explicit response. The martyr is where the anonymous becomes fully explicit and fully costly.
- Rahner's reticence about satisfaction theories is a considered position, not an omission: he denies that the Cross changes God's disposition, holding that God's saving will is prior and the Cross is its real symbol in history.

**(d) Verdict.** The most **rhetorically powerful** critique, and partly unfair. Balthasar's charge that Rahner deduces Christ from anthropology is not sustainable from Rahner's texts. But (2) and (3) land: Rahner's system genuinely finds it hard to articulate the **urgency and scandal** of the Gospel, and his theology of the Cross is thin. The mature judgement — now widely shared — is that **the two are complementary correctives**: Rahner secures the universality of grace, Balthasar the particularity of the form. A theology with only one of them is defective.

## Critique 2 — Ratzinger: the loss of the concrete

**(a) Who.** **Joseph Ratzinger / Benedict XVI**, *Principles of Catholic Theology*; *Introduction to Christianity*; interviews from the 1980s onward.

**(b) The argument.**
- Transcendental method abstracts from the **concrete, historical, liturgical and biblical** form of revelation. It substitutes a philosophy of the subject for the memory of Israel and the Church.
- Anonymous Christianity relativises the **historical event** of Jesus of Nazareth in favour of a universal structure.
- Post-conciliar Rahnerianism (as distinct from Rahner) fed a theology impatient with tradition, cut loose from the liturgy and the Fathers.

**(c) Reply.**
- Rahner insists relentlessly that transcendental revelation is **never available apart from categorical mediation** — Scripture, dogma, sacrament, Church ([[04 - The Transcendental Method in Theology]]).
- His symbol ontology makes the concrete *necessary*, not optional: what is not expressed is not fully real ([[07 - The Theology of the Symbol - The Core Essay]]).

**(d) Verdict.** The criticism of **Rahner's followers** is more accurate than the criticism of Rahner. But Ratzinger identifies a real structural risk: a method whose starting point is the subject will, in weaker hands, treat the particular as illustration. Note also that Ratzinger's own *Introduction to Christianity* is closer to Rahner on universal grace than the later polemics suggest.

## Critique 3 — Trinity: modalism and the collapse of divine freedom

**(a) Who.** **Jürgen Moltmann** (*The Trinity and the Kingdom*); **Walter Kasper** (*The God of Jesus Christ*); **Paul Molnar** (*Divine Freedom and the Doctrine of the Immanent Trinity*); **Bruce Marshall**.

**(b) The argument.**
1. **Modalism.** "Three distinct manners of subsisting" (*Subsistenzweisen*) with **one** divine consciousness and **one** divine will is, Moltmann charges, Idealist modalism — one absolute subject in three modes. Genuine interpersonal relations (the Son *prays* to the Father) become unintelligible.
2. **The "vice versa."** If the immanent Trinity simply *is* the economic Trinity, God's inner life appears to require the economy. Creation and Incarnation become necessary to God's self-constitution, and grace ceases to be gift.
3. Molnar's precise worry: the axiom, taken ontologically, removes the ground on which one could say God **would still be triune love** had there been no world.

**(c) Reply.**
- *Subsistenzweise* names a **real** distinction (unlike modalism's apparent one), and Rahner retains "person" as the binding dogmatic term, offering his phrase as explanation, not replacement.
- On (2): Rahner says the immanent Trinity **is** the economic, not that it is **exhausted by** it. The processions are necessary; the economy is free. Identity of *reality*, not of *mode*.

**(d) Verdict.** On modalism, Rahner is **defensible but exposed** — one consciousness and one will is the classical Latin position, so Moltmann's charge would convict Augustine and Aquinas too; the real dispute is West vs. East, not Rahner vs. orthodoxy. On the "vice versa," the ambiguity is **real and unresolved in Rahner's own texts**. Best position for an essay: read the axiom **epistemologically** (we have no access to an immanent Trinity behind the economy) rather than **ontologically** (there is nothing more to God than the economy). → [[09 - Rahner's Rule - The Grundaxiom of the Trinity]]

## Critique 4 — Sacraments: manifestation or conferral?

**(a) Who.** **Colman O'Neill OP**, *Meeting Christ in the Sacraments*; Thomist sacramental theologians generally.

**(b) The argument.**
- Trent: the sacraments **contain and confer** the grace they signify (DS 1606). This is real efficient instrumental causality.
- On Rahner's account grace is universally offered **before** the sacrament ([[05 - The Supernatural Existential - Nature and Grace]]); the sacrament makes it tangible, ecclesial, definitive. But "making tangible" is **manifestation**, not conferral.
- Consequence: *ex opere operato* is weakened, and the sacraments become the Church's celebration of a grace already possessed rather than the moment of its giving.

**(c) Reply.**
- On Rahner's ontology, **what is not expressed is not fully real**. Historical, bodily, ecclesial embodiment is therefore not a decoration on an already-complete grace; it *completes* it. The sacrament effects a genuinely new state of affairs.
- *Ex opere operato* is fully preserved and even strengthened: the guarantee attaches to the **Church's self-realisation**, which does not depend on the minister's holiness.

**(d) Verdict.** The reply is **strong if you grant Thesis 1 of the symbol essay and weak if you do not**. This is the clearest case in the whole file where a criticism of Rahner is really a criticism of his ontology. Say that in an essay and you will be marking the actual fault line.

## Critique 5 — Grace: has gratuity survived?

**(a) Who.** Neo-Thomists; more recently **Lawrence Feingold**, *The Natural Desire to See God according to St Thomas Aquinas and His Interpreters*; Steven Long.

**(b) The argument.** A gift given to absolutely everyone, always, as a constitutive feature of concrete existence, is functionally indistinguishable from a property of nature. Rahner keeps the word "supernatural" while emptying its content. *Humani Generis* (1950) warned against exactly this.

**(c) Reply.** Gratuity is located in the **ground** of the gift (God's free will) and not in its **rarity**. A gift given to all is not thereby owed to any. And the *Restbegriff* of pure nature is retained precisely to mark the difference ([[05 - The Supernatural Existential - Nature and Grace]]).

**(d) Verdict.** Rahner's reply is **coherent**. But the critic can restate the worry as an epistemic one: if the existential is universal and unthematic, no experience could distinguish a world with it from a world without it — so the doctrine does no work beyond marking a conceptual boundary. That version is harder to answer.

## Critique 6 — Feminist critiques

**(a) Who.** **Anne Carr** (*Transforming Grace* — a sympathetic Rahnerian feminist); **Elizabeth Johnson** (*She Who Is* — uses Rahner critically); **Mary Ann Hinsdale**; **Susan Ross** on sacramentality.

**(b) The argument.**
- The "transcendental subject" is presented as universal but is in practice the **generic male European subject**; women's specific experience — of embodiment, of exclusion from orders and from theological speech — vanishes into the abstraction.
- Rahner's magnificent theology of the **body** as real symbol is never brought to bear on **sexual difference**, on women's bodies, or on the Church's treatment of them.
- His ecclesiology never seriously interrogates the exclusion of women from ordained ministry — a striking silence for a theologian so willing to question structures.

**(c) Reply / resources within Rahner.**
- The symbol ontology is a **gift to feminist theology**: if the body is the person's real symbol, then bodily particularity is theologically weighty and the abuse of bodies is a theological outrage. Johnson and Ross use exactly this.
- His doctrine of God as absolute incomprehensible mystery undercuts every literalised masculine God-image; Johnson builds *She Who Is* partly on this Rahnerian ground.

**(d) Verdict.** **Correct as a critique of omission**, not of doctrine. The most productive feminist verdict is Carr's: Rahner supplies **tools he did not use**. That is a real criticism and also a real endorsement.

## Critique 7 — Liberation and political critiques

**(a) Who.** **Metz** (see [[16 - Critical Reflection I - Philosophical]]); **Gustavo Gutiérrez**; **Jon Sobrino**; **Ignacio Ellacuría**; in Asia, **Aloysius Pieris**.

**(b) The argument.**
- Sin is treated primarily as the individual's self-closure; **structural sin** — caste, class, colonial dispossession — is under-theorised.
- The "preferential option for the poor" cannot be derived from a transcendental analysis of the subject; it comes from the concrete history of Israel, the ministry of Jesus, and the cry of the oppressed.
- Pieris sharpens it for Asia: Rahner's theology is a theology of *religiousness* without a theology of **poverty**. Asia's reality is both, and the second is where the Gospel bites.

**(c) Reply.** Rahner endorsed political theology in his later work, spoke of the unity of love of God and love of neighbour as an identity rather than a parallel, and defended Gutiérrez to Rome in 1984.

**(d) Verdict.** Fair. The concession is real but architectural change never followed. → [[18 - Rahner in the Indian Context]]

## Critique 8 — The symbol ontology itself (the criticism that matters most for this unit)

**(b) The arguments, in order of seriousness.**

1. **The Hegelian shadow.** "Being must express itself in order to attain its own nature" resembles Hegel's *Entäußerung* — Spirit externalising itself to become itself. If self-expression is **necessary to being as such**, and creation is God's self-expression, is creation free? Does God need a world?
   *Rahner's distinction:* the **inner-Trinitarian** utterance is necessary and complete; the utterance *ad extra* is **free** — the Father's utterance of the Word is not lacking anything that the world supplies.
   *Assessment:* the distinction is correct as far as it goes, but Rahner grounds both in **one law of being**, and the law is stated without qualification. He needed a more careful account of analogy at exactly this point. **This is the most serious philosophical objection to the essay.**

2. **Univocity worries.** The essay moves in a few pages from smiles to the immanent Trinity. Is "symbol" being used **analogically** or **univocally**? Rahner would say analogically; the text does not always show its working. In an exam, note that he owes the reader an explicit *analogia entis* at this juncture.

3. **No theology of the false symbol.** The ontology explains **fullness of expression** superbly and **failure** only privatively. But the body can **conceal** as well as reveal; the Church can **misrepresent** Christ; the sacrament can be received in bad faith. A hypocrite's smile is a real bodily expression of something other than joy. Rahner has no developed account of the **lying symbol**, and this is arguably the largest structural gap in his system → [[12 - Ecclesiology and Sacramental Theology]].
   *Constructive reply:* the missing doctrine can be built from Rahner's own principles — falsity is possible only for free spirit, the liar fails to attain himself, and symbolic causality run in reverse yields the **anti-sacrament**. See [[24 - The Theology of the False Symbol]].

4. **Where is the Cross in the symbol ontology?** Self-expression sounds like plenitude overflowing. But the supreme Christian symbol is a **broken body**. Balthasar's point resurfaces here in its sharpest ontological form: does a metaphysics of expression have room for **kenosis** — for expression as self-emptying rather than self-attainment?
   *A Rahnerian reply worth developing:* the pierced heart *is* the symbol essay's own culminating case, and self-expression there **is** self-emptying — the two coincide precisely in the crucified. That reply is available but Rahner does not develop it. It is developed in [[24 - The Theology of the False Symbol]] §9, where it also turns out to require an **amendment** to Thesis 1: for a being whose nature is self-gift, expressing itself and expending itself are one act.

5. **Defence worth citing.** **Stephen M. Fields SJ**, *Being as Symbol: On the Origins and Development of Karl Rahner's Metaphysics* (Georgetown, 2000), argues the symbol ontology has genuine roots in Aquinas and Bonaventure rather than in German Idealism, and that the necessity objection rests on a misreading. Cite Fields when defending Rahner.

**(d) Verdict.** Objections 1 and 3 are serious and only partly answerable. Objection 4 points to a **development Rahner should have made and did not**. The ontology is fertile, elegant and genuinely illuminating — and it is stated with less metaphysical care than a claim of that scope requires.

> [!tip] The paragraph that wins marks
> "Rahner's critics divide into those who think he began in the wrong place (Balthasar, Ratzinger, Lindbeck) and those who think he did not go far enough (Metz, Gutiérrez, the feminists, the Asian theologians). The first group he can largely answer; the second he substantially conceded. The deepest unresolved difficulty is neither: it is whether an ontology in which being must express itself to attain itself can hold together the **freedom** of God's self-expression and the **kenosis** of the Cross."
