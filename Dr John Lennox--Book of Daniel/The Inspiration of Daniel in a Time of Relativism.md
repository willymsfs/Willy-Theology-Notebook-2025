### **Summary of the Video:**

In this lecture, **Dr. John Lennox** (Emeritus Professor of Mathematics at Oxford University and a renowned Christian apologist) discusses the relevance of the biblical Book of Daniel to the modern world.

**Key themes include:**

- **The Challenge of Relativism:** Lennox addresses how modern society often views truth as relative rather than absolute. He argues that this environment is similar to the pluralistic and relativistic culture of ancient Babylon, where Daniel lived.
    
- **Daniel as a Model:** He presents Daniel as an example of how a person of faith can maintain their intellectual and moral integrity while living in a society with fundamentally different values.
    
- **Faith in the Public Square:** Lennox emphasizes that Daniel did not keep his faith private; he was a high-ranking official who integrated his beliefs with his professional and public life without compromising his convictions.
    
- **Cultural Pressure:** The talk explores the pressures Daniel faced to assimilate into Babylonian culture (such as changing his name, education, and diet) and how he navigated these challenges with "wisdom and courage."
    

This video is the first part of a three-part series based on his book, **_Against the Flow: The Inspiration of Daniel in an Age of Relativism_**. It is widely regarded as a foundational talk for those interested in Christian apologetics and how to navigate faith in a secular or pluralistic environment.

### **Overview of the Lecture Content**

Dr. Lennox begins by examining the life of Daniel as a teenager, likely between the ages of 15 and 20, who was suddenly forcibly removed from his narrow, monotheistic Jewish culture in Jerusalem to the powerful, pluralistic culture of Babylon.

### **Key Themes and Arguments**

- **The Parallel to Modern Culture:** Lennox argues that while Daniel was physically moved to a different culture, many people today experience the same effect as their own culture changes dramatically around them, shifting toward secularism and relativism. He describes this as living in a "Babylonian" environment regardless of geography.
    
- **The Nature of Babylonian Relativism:** The lecturer explains that the Babylonians were essentially materialists who believed the universe arose from primeval matter and made "gods" out of the forces of nature. He compares this to modern physics and the belief that fundamental forces determine everything.
    
- **Public vs. Private Faith:** A central theme is Daniel’s refusal to "privatize" his faith. Lennox criticizes the modern pressure to keep Christianity in a "private box" and highlights Daniel as a model for maintaining a "cutting edge" public witness even while holding a high-ranking professional position.
    
- **Pressure to Assimilate:** Lennox discusses the attempts by Babylonian leadership to re-educate and rename the Hebrew captives to make them indistinguishable from the rest of the culture. Daniel’s "secret" was his decision in his heart to obey God as his highest value, allowing him to accept education without bowing to the underlying polytheistic worldview.
    
- **Literary and Historical Context:** The lecture touches on the unique structure of the Book of Daniel, noting it is written in both Hebrew and Aramaic. Lennox also defends the historicity of the book against critics who suggest it was written much later (2nd century BC), arguing that its accurate predictions are a sign of its divine inspiration.
    
- **Wisdom and Integrity:** Lennox emphasizes that Daniel’s faith did not lead to a "ghetto mentality" but instead allowed him to live a full professional life at the highest levels of administration while remaining true to his convictions.

Dr. John Lennox’s lecture series, **"The Inspiration of Daniel in a Time of Relativism,"** constructs a cumulative argument. He does not treat the Book of Daniel merely as a collection of Sunday School stories, but as a manual for maintaining intellectual and spiritual integrity in a hostile cultural environment.

Here is the systematic and logical progression of his argument across all three parts, connecting the dots from personal identity to public witness, and finally to ultimate reality.

---

### **Phase 1: The Foundation – Internal Identity (Part 1)**

**Theme: Resisting the "Private Box" and Cultural Assimilation**

The logical starting point is the **mind**. Lennox begins by establishing the setting: Daniel is a teenager forcibly moved from a monotheistic culture (Jerusalem) to a secular, pluralistic tyranny (Babylon).

- **The Modern Parallel:** Lennox argues that while we may not have moved geographically, the culture has moved around us. Modern Western society, like Babylon, attempts to "privatize" faith—allowing you to believe what you want inside your head (or your church), provided it never affects your public life.
    
- **The Babylonian Strategy:** The Empire tried to assimilate Daniel through isolation, re-education (University of Babylon), and renaming. They wanted to erase his identity.
    
- **The Line in the Sand:** Daniel did not reject the education or the job; he engaged with the culture. However, he drew a line at the "King’s food." Lennox explains this wasn't just about diet; it was about **allegiance**. Eating the meat offered to idols would have been a participation in the Babylonian worldview.
    
- **The Logic:** You cannot resist public pressure later if you have not resolved your private identity first. Daniel decided _in his heart_ not to defile himself. This internal resolution is the prerequisite for everything that follows.
    

### **Phase 2: The Manifestation – Public Witness (Part 2)**

**Theme: The Conflict between State and God**

Once internal identity is established, it inevitably leads to external conflict. Part 2 moves from the personal decision (diet) to the political arena (power and law).

- **Reason and Revelation (Daniel 2):** Lennox counters the idea that faith is a "blind leap." Daniel interprets the King’s dream not by guessing, but by seeking God. This establishes that **wisdom** comes from God, not just the "University of Babylon."
    
- **Absolutizing the Relative (Daniel 3):** This is Lennox’s core philosophical definition of idolatry. Nebuchadnezzar builds a golden statue, demanding absolute worship of the state (a relative thing).
    
    - _The Connection:_ Because Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego had settled their identity in Part 1, they were able to stand when everyone else bowed in Part 2.
        
- **De-Valuing Biological Life:** Lennox notes that the three friends were willing to die. Why? Because they did not view biological life as the _highest_ value. Loyalty to the Truth was higher. This destroys the leverage the State has over the believer.
    
- **The Logic:** Public witness requires accurate thinking. You must recognize when the State is claiming the authority of God. If you recognize that the State is "relative" (temporary) and God is "absolute," civil disobedience becomes a rational act of loyalty to the higher authority.
    

### **Phase 3: The Validation – Ultimate Reality (Part 3)**

**Theme: History, Prophecy, and the Intellectual Defense**

In the final phase, Lennox cements the courage displayed in parts 1 and 2 by grounding it in **objective truth**. Courage without truth is just foolishness; courage based on reality is wisdom.

- **The Defense of History:** Lennox spends significant time attacking the skeptical view that Daniel was written in the 2nd Century BC (after the events happened). He argues for the book's authenticity as 6th Century BC prophecy.
    
    - _Why this matters:_ If Daniel is a forgery, then God did not predict the future, and He is not in control of history. If the book is true, then the "God of Heaven" truly rules over the kingdoms of men.
        
- **The Clash of Cosmogonies:** He contrasts the Babylonian worldview (universe from primeval matter) with the Biblical worldview (universe from a Creator). He links Babylon to modern materialism/atheism.
    
- **The End Game:** The prophecies in Daniel (the statue, the beasts) show that all human empires—Babylon, Persia, Greece, Rome, and modern superpowers—are temporary. They will be smashed by the "Stone cut without hands" (the Kingdom of God).
    
- **The Logic:** Daniel could stand against the flow because he knew how the story ended. He wasn't betting on the losing side (Babylon); he was investing in the eternal Kingdom.

### **The Synthesis: Connecting the Dots**

If we view the three lectures as a single argument, Lennox is saying:

1. **You are in Babylon:** You live in a culture that wants to erase your distinctiveness and privatize your faith (Part 1).
    
2. **You must resolve your identity:** You must decide _now_, in small things (like the "King's food"), that you serve God, not the culture (Part 1).
    
3. **This will lead to public conflict:** The State or culture will eventually demand you bow to their idols. You must be willing to stand out and even suffer, refusing to treat the "relative" (cultural trends/state power) as "absolute" (Part 2).
    
4. **You can do this because it is True:** You aren't standing on blind faith. You are standing on history, reason, and the certainty that God controls the flow of time and will ultimately vindicate His people (Part 3).
    

**Systematic Summary:**

- **Part 1:** The **Formation** of the Witness (Character).
    
- **Part 2:** The **Expression** of the Witness (Courage).
    
- **Part 3:** The **Foundation** of the Witness (Truth/Prophecy).


Ladies and gentlemen, let us open the text to the very beginning, to Daniel chapter 1. Because if we get the beginning wrong, we shall get the end wrong.

Imagine, if you will, the scene. We are looking at a group of young men—teenagers, really, perhaps no older than fifteen or sixteen. They have been torn from their families, ripped from their spiritual roots in Jerusalem, and marched hundreds of miles across the desert to the great, sprawling, intimidating city of Babylon.

You see, Babylon was not just a city; it was a worldview. It was a culture that prided itself on its achievements, its military power, and its gods. And Nebuchadnezzar, the emperor, was a brilliant strategist. He knew that to conquer a people, you do not merely defeat their armies; you capture their minds. He wanted to take the brightest and the best of the Hebrew nobility and turn them into good Babylonians.

Does that sound familiar? I suspect it does. Because you and I, though we may live in comfortable suburbs, are living in Babylon. We are surrounded by a culture that is relentlessly trying to squeeze us into its mould.

Now, look at the pressure they were under. It was a three-pronged attack.

First, there was **Isolation**. They were cut off from the support of their parents and the temple.

Second, there was **Indoctrination**. The text tells us they were to be taught the "literature and language of the Chaldeans." This was the University of Babylon. And notice something profound here: Daniel did not reject the education. He did not say, "I am a believer in Jehovah, so I will have nothing to do with your pagan science." No! He studied it. He mastered it. He eventually knew more about the Babylonian system than the Babylonians themselves. There is a lesson there for us: we are not called to be ignorant. We are called to engage.

But thirdly, and most insidiously, there was **Assimilation**. They changed their names. Daniel, "God is my Judge," became Belteshazzar, "Lady, protect the King." They tried to wipe out the name of God from his identity.

And then comes the crisis. The King assigns them a daily portion of food and wine from his own table.

Now, why did Daniel object to this? Was he simply a picky eater? Was he worried about calories? No, no. You see, in the ancient world, to eat at the King’s table was to pledge total allegiance to the King. It was a covenant meal. Furthermore, that meat had likely been offered to idols. To eat it would be to signal, publicly, "I belong to Nebuchadnezzar. I am one of you."

And here, my friends, is the turning point of the entire book. Verse 8. It is one of the most magnificent verses in Scripture:

> _"But Daniel resolved in his heart that he would not defile himself..."_

He **resolved**. The Hebrew implies he "laid it on his heart."

You see, the battle was not won in the lion’s den. That came years later. The battle was won right here, as a teenager, in the quietness of his own conscience. He drew a line. He said, "I will learn your language, I will serve in your administration, I will be a loyal citizen—but I will _not_ worship your gods. I will not compromise my primary loyalty to the God of heaven."

This is what I call the **Formation of the Witness**.

It starts in the private life. If Daniel had compromised here—if he had said, "Well, everyone else is doing it, I’d better fit in, I don’t want to cause a fuss"—we would never have heard of him again. He would have vanished into the pages of history, just another assimilated bureaucrat.

But he didn't. He had the courage to be different.

And so, the question I must ask you—and I ask it of myself as well—is this: Have you resolved in your heart? Because if you have not decided _now_, in the small things, where your lines are drawn, you will have no strength to stand when the great tests come.

We need men and women today who, like Daniel, are excellent in their work, engaged with their culture, but who have resolved in the depths of their hearts that they will not bow to the idols of this age. That is where the witness begins. Not on the public stage, but in the private heart.


And so, having settled the question of identity in the private sphere, we now move out onto the public plain of Dura. We move from the dining room to the furnace.

This is what I call **The Expression of the Witness**.

You see, ladies and gentlemen, private convictions have public consequences. If you truly believe that there is a God in heaven who reveals secrets—as Daniel and his friends did—you cannot keep that inside a little religious box when the State demands your soul.

Look at chapter 3. Nebuchadnezzar has had a dream of a statue made of various metals, but he evidently didn't like the bit about the "head of gold" eventually passing away. So, what does he do? He builds a statue that is gold from top to bottom! He tries to freeze history. He attempts to make his temporal power eternal.

And here we come to a profound philosophical point: **Idolatry is the attempt to absolutize the relative.**

The State is a relative institution. It is established by God, yes, but it is temporary; it is derived. But here, the State tries to take the place of the Absolute. Nebuchadnezzar says, effectively, "There is no god above me. When the music plays, you bow to _my_ values, _my_ image, _my_ definition of reality."

Now, imagine the pressure. The music starts—a great orchestra. The psychology of the crowd is overwhelming. Everybody bows. The pressure to conform is immense. "Just bow down! You can cross your fingers behind your back! You can pray to Jehovah in your head! Just don't make a scene!"

But Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego remain standing.

Why? Were they merely obstinate? No. They stood because they were the only free men on that plain. Everyone else was a slave to the culture, a slave to fear, a slave to the Emperor. These three were free because they knew who the true Absolute was. They knew that to bow to the relative as if it were the absolute is to destroy one's own humanity.

And notice their response to the King. It is respectful, but it is rock-solid.

> _"O Nebuchadnezzar... our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace... But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods."_

**"But if not."**

That, my friends, is the most courageous phrase in the Old Testament. They are saying, "Our loyalty to God does not depend on Him doing what we want." They did not regard their own biological existence as the highest value.

You see, in our modern world, we have made safety and survival the highest goods. If safety is your god, you will bow the moment the furnace is heated. But if God is your God, you are free. You can look the most powerful tyrant on earth in the eye and say, "You can kill me, but you cannot own me."

And what happened? They were thrown in. And there, in the fire, the King saw a fourth man, "like a son of the gods."

The lesson is not always that God saves us _from_ the fire—millions of Christians have died for their faith, and we must not dishonour them by suggesting they lacked faith. The lesson is that God is with us _in_ the fire.

This is the public expression of the witness. It is the refusal to let the culture squeeze you into its mould, even when the heat is turned up seven times hotter. It is the courage to say that the State is not God. And in a time of relativism, where we are told there is no truth and everything is subjective, standing for the absolute Truth of God is the most radical, revolutionary, and necessary thing you can possibly do.


We have spoken of the **Formation** of the witness—the decision of the heart. We have spoken of the **Expression** of the witness—the courage in the fire.

But now, ladies and gentlemen, we must face the most critical question of all. On what does this courage rest? Is it merely a psychological trick? Is it just positive thinking? Or is it grounded in reality?

This brings us to **The Foundation of the Witness.**

You see, the modern skeptic—and indeed, many theologians in the last century—will tell you that the Book of Daniel is a forgery. They argue that it was written in the 2nd century BC, hundreds of years after Daniel lived. Why? Because the prophecies regarding the rise and fall of the empires of Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, and Rome are so breathtakingly accurate that they say, "No one could possibly have known that in advance."

Do you see the assumption there? It is what we in science call a _circular argument_. They assume that the supernatural is impossible, they assume that God cannot speak, and therefore, they conclude that prophecy is impossible. They are not following the evidence; they are following their philosophy.

But if there is a God—a God who created this universe, who sustains it, and who stands outside of time—then telling the future is no more difficult for Him than telling the past is for us.

And this is where Daniel moves from being a story of personal piety to a manifesto of **intellectual resistance**.

Consider the vision of the four beasts in Chapter 7, paralleling the statue in Chapter 2. We see a succession of superpowers. The Lion (Babylon), the Bear (Medo-Persia), the Leopard (Greece), and the terrifying Beast (Rome).

Daniel is telling us something profound about the nature of history. The Babylonians believed that the universe emerged from primeval chaos—from matter. Does that sound familiar? It is the same song sung by the modern materialist: "In the beginning were the particles."

But Daniel stands against the flow and says, "No." History is not a random collision of atoms. It is not just one empire grabbing power after another in a meaningless cycle of violence. History is moving towards a destination.

He saw that all these great empires—as impressive as their gold and their armies might be—are fragile. They are, as the text says, like "chaff on a threshing floor." They blow away.

But then, he sees a stone. A stone "cut out by no human hand."

This stone strikes the statue and grows to fill the whole earth. This is the Kingdom of God. This is the reality that Jesus Christ inaugurated.

Do you see why this matters? Daniel could face the Lions' Den in Chapter 6—he could face the possibility of being torn apart by wild beasts—because he knew that the Lions were not the ultimate reality. He knew that the empire of the Medes and Persians, powerful as it was, was a passing shadow.

He had a **cognitive grasp** of the fact that the God he served was the Lord of History.

So, my encouragement to you is this: do not let the world squeeze you into the mould of thinking that your faith is just a "religious feeling" or a "private preference." No! It is anchored in the objective reality of history. The tomb is empty. The stone has struck the statue.

We live in a time of relativism, yes. But we do not serve a relative God. We serve the Absolute. And because of that, we can have a spine of steel in a world of trembling compromises. We can stand.
