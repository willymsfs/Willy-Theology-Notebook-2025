CLASS 1: WALKING TOGETHER, OR WHAT SYNODALITY MEANS

Module 1: Introduction to Synodality

Aim of the Class

This first class fixes the meaning of the word that names the whole course. By the end of it the students should be able to define synodality, distinguish it from a synod and from episcopal collegiality, and state why the Church of our day has placed it at the centre of her self-understanding. The thread that will run through all fifteen classes appears here for the first time: communion, participation, mission.

A Word Recovered from the Road

Synodality comes from the Greek synodos, formed of syn, together, and hodos, road or way. A synodos is a company on one road: fellow travellers, people who share a direction and not merely a location. The word entered Christian speech very early. Saint John Chrysostom could write in the fourth century that Church and synod are synonymous, because the assembly called by God is precisely a walking together. The observation cuts deep. Before the community of Jesus had buildings, budgets, or even the name Christian, outsiders called it the Way, and its members were those who belonged to the Way (Acts 9:2). The Church had a road before she had an address.

The Scriptures of Israel confirm the choice of image. The Hebrew verb halak, to walk, gives the rabbis their name for the whole practice of the covenant, halakhah, the walking; and Jesus gathers the entire vocabulary into his own person when he says, “I am the way” (Jn 14:6). To be a believer, in both dispensations, is less to hold a position than to keep a road.

When theology speaks of synodality, then, it does not first mean a meeting, a document, or a voting procedure. It means the form of life proper to a people who travel one road together behind one Lord. The International Theological Commission gathers this into a compact formula: synodality is the specific modus vivendi et operandi of the Church, the People of God. A style of living and working: that is the primary sense, and every structure the course will examine later exists to serve it.

Example. Every January lakhs of pilgrims cross our own district on the road to the Gangasagar Mela at Sagar Island. Watch them at Sealdah or on the Kakdwip road: strangers from a dozen states, yet unmistakably one body, because they share one direction and one hope. A crowd becomes a company the moment it agrees on a destination. That is the picture hidden inside the word synodos.

One Word, Three Levels

Because the word now appears everywhere, it needs discipline. The International Theological Commission distinguishes three connected senses. First, synodality names a style: the ordinary manner in which the whole Church lives and works, listening to all and deciding with all in mind. Secondly, it names structures and processes: the stable institutions through which that style becomes flesh, such as diocesan synods, pastoral councils, presbyteral councils, and episcopal conferences. Thirdly, it names events: particular assemblies convoked at a given hour, from a parish assembly to an ecumenical council. The three senses stand in strict order. Structures without the style become machinery; events without the style become theatre.

A second distinction guards against a common confusion. Collegiality, taught with such force by the Second Vatican Council, concerns the body of bishops united with and under the Bishop of Rome, cum et sub Petro. Synodality is the wider reality: the common dignity and co-responsibility of all the baptised, within which episcopal collegiality is one specific and necessary expression. Put simply for the classroom: every exercise of collegiality should breathe synodality, but synodality involves far more people than the college of bishops.

Why the Church Speaks of It Now

The vocabulary is old, but its return to the centre is recent, and the dates matter. The Second Vatican Council placed its chapter on the People of God before its chapter on the hierarchy in Lumen Gentium, and the order itself is doctrine: all the baptised together come first, and ordained ministry stands within the people, not above it as a separate caste. In 1965, while the Council was still in session, Paul VI created a permanent Synod of Bishops through the motu proprio Apostolica Sollicitudo, a structure Class 12 will study closely. Fifty years later, on 17 October 2015, Pope Francis delivered the address that set the present course of the Church, telling the assembly that the path of synodality is the very path God expects of the Church of the third millennium, and describing a synodal Church as, before all else, “a Church which listens”, in which the faithful people, the college of bishops, and the Bishop of Rome each listen to the others, and all listen to the Holy Spirit.

From 2021 to 2024 the whole Church then walked through the widest consultation in her history under the title that gives this course its thread: For a Synodal Church: Communion, Participation, Mission. Its Final Document describes synodality, in plain terms, as a path of spiritual renewal and structural reform that enables the Church to become more participatory and more missionary. Francis then took a step without recent parallel: he issued no separate exhortation, declared that the Final Document itself participates in his ordinary magisterium, and asked the local churches to receive and implement it. In short, synodality is not a passing programme. It has been placed on the desk of every bishop, every parish, and every seminary, including ours.

Communion, Participation, Mission

The synodal title is not a slogan but a grammar, and the course will keep returning to it. Communion answers the question of what the Church is: a people gathered into the very life of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, so that the pattern of her common life should mirror the mutual self-giving of the Trinity. Participation answers the question of how she acts: through the gifts and the voice of all the baptised, since the anointing of Baptism, not ordination, is the first ground of dignity and co-responsibility in the Church. The Council teaches that the whole body of the faithful, anointed by the Holy One, cannot err in matters of belief, a supernatural instinct of faith the tradition calls the sensus fidei. Pope Francis draws the practical conclusion: because the people as a whole possess this instinct, the Church does well to listen before she teaches, for God has furnished the totality of the faithful with an organ of discernment. Mission answers the question of why she exists at all: communion and participation are not ecclesial housekeeping but the training of a people to be sent. A synod that ends in its own minutes has failed; a synod that ends at the door, facing the world, has understood itself.

Indian Context. India possesses her own grammar of assembly. The Constitution, through the Seventy-third Amendment of 1992, made the gram sabha of every village a deliberative body of all adult residents, and the PESA Act of 1996 extended this to the Scheduled Areas precisely so that Adivasi communities could decide through their own assemblies. Older still are the manjhi with his village council among the Santals and the parha assemblies of the Oraons, both living traditions in Bengal and Jharkhand. The Indian Church has her own experience too: small Christian communities and parish pastoral councils have practised neighbourhood listening for decades. Synodality therefore lands on prepared soil in India; the task is to let Baptism, and not caste, wealth, or gender, decide who speaks.

What Synodality Is Not

Precision also requires negation, and here the teacher must be firm, because misunderstandings arrive in class before definitions do. Synodality is not parliamentary democracy. Pope Francis himself warned the bishops in 2015 that a synod is neither a convention nor a parliament where deals are struck and majorities decide the truth. Revelation is not up for a vote; discernment asks what the Spirit says, not what fifty-one per cent prefer. Nor is synodality a technique of endless meetings. India, in fact, has no shortage of committees; what it lacks, in Church and state alike, is the patient discipline of listening that changes the listener. Nor, finally, is synodality a soft word for the surrender of pastoral authority. Bishops and priests do not preside less in a synodal Church; they preside differently, as men whose first act is to hear. The rest of the course is the long demonstration that this is not the novelty of one pontificate but the recovered shape of the whole tradition, from Sinai to the upper room to Nicaea to our own parish councils.

Questions for Discussion

Questions for Discussion

  1. The gram sabha gives every adult villager a voice in local decisions, and PESA extends this principle to Adivasi areas. Where has your home parish given the baptised a comparable voice, and where has it kept them silent?
  2. Chrysostom says that Church and synod are synonymous. On an Indian street where temple, mosque, gurdwara, and church stand within sight of one another, what would a Church visibly defined by walking together say to neighbours of other faiths that a Church defined by buildings cannot?
  3. Many in India are weary of committees, elections, and meetings that change nothing. What makes ecclesial discernment different from these, and how would you explain that difference to a tired parish council member in one minute?

CLASS 2: THE PILGRIM PEOPLE OF GOD AND THE COVENANT ASSEMBLIES

Module 2: Biblical Foundations I

Aim of the Class

Module 2 asks whether synodality is a modern graft or the native grain of the biblical wood. This class answers by watching Israel come into being: a people constituted on the road and consolidated in assemblies where God speaks and the whole people answer. Students should leave able to explain the word qahal, to narrate the assemblies of Sinai, Shechem, and the Water Gate, and to show how Lumen Gentium 9 stands on this foundation.

A People Made While Walking

Israel does not begin as a settled institution; she begins as a summons to move. Abram hears “Go from your country” (Gen 12:1) before he hears anything else, and the covenant is later sealed with the command, “walk before me, and be blameless” (Gen 17:1). Centuries after, when an Israelite farmer brings his first fruits to the sanctuary, the creed placed on his lips opens not with a doctrine but with a road: “A wandering Aramean was my father” (Deut 26:5). The Exodus deepens the pattern: the defining act of God is to bring a crowd of slaves out of Egypt and to make them, across forty years of wilderness, into a people who know how to walk together, quarrel together, repent together, and camp around one tent of meeting. The wilderness is the school of the assembly. Identity, in the Bible, is remembered movement; a people that forgets it was once on the road soon forgets whose people it is.

Qahal, the Called Assembly

The decisive term of this class is the Hebrew qahal, from a root meaning to call together. A qahal is not a crowd that happens; it is an assembly that is convoked, and in Israel the convoker is finally God himself. Deuteronomy remembers Sinai simply as “the day of the assembly”, yom ha-qahal (Deut 9:10; 10:4; 18:16). When Jewish scholars rendered the Scriptures into Greek, they translated qahal by ekklesia, the word Greek cities used for the citizen body called out by a herald. The consequence for us is enormous: when the writers of the apostolic age chose ekklesia as the name of the community of Jesus, they were not borrowing civic vocabulary at random. They were claiming that the assembly at the foot of Sinai had been reconvened in Christ. Every time we say Church, Scripture hears assembly; and behind assembly stands a mountain, a summons, and a people answering with one voice.

Sinai, Where the People Answer Together

Read Exodus 19:3-8 slowly in class. God proposes: if the people keep the covenant, they shall be his own possession among all peoples, “a kingdom of priests and a holy nation” (Ex 19:6). Moses does not sign on their behalf. He set before them all these words, and “all the people answered together” (Ex 19:7-8). Watch the grammar of the scene: proposal, mediation, common response. The ratification in Exodus 24 repeats and strengthens it. Moses tells the people all the words of the LORD; the people answer with one voice; the covenant is written and then read aloud in the hearing of the assembly; and the people commit themselves: “All that the LORD has spoken we will do, and we will be obedient” (Ex 24:3-7). Blood is thrown on altar and people alike, binding both parties of the covenant in one rite (Ex 24:8).

Three features deserve the board. First, initiative belongs wholly to God: this assembly is convoked, not self-assembled, and that priority of grace will separate every ecclesial assembly from every merely political one. Secondly, the word is published before it binds: the book is read aloud in the hearing of the people, an ancient rule against every government by whisper. Thirdly, consent is corporate and vocal: the people answer together, with one voice, and their answer belongs to the covenant itself. Participation, in other words, is not a concession the Bible makes to modern taste; it is written into the founding rite of the People of God.

Shechem and the Water Gate: Assemblies of Choice and Understanding

The covenant assembly is not a single event but a renewable institution. At Shechem, Joshua gathers all the tribes, rehearses the deeds of God, and then does something no dictator does: he sets the people free to refuse. “Choose this day whom you will serve”, he tells them, “but as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD” (Josh 24:15). The people choose, are warned of the cost, choose again, and become witnesses against themselves (Josh 24:16-24). A great stone is set up as a listening witness, for it has heard all the words of the LORD (Josh 24:26-27). Covenant fidelity, this scene teaches, must be re-chosen by every generation in assembly; inherited faith that is never personally answered dies in the second generation.

Centuries later, after the exile, Ezra reads the book of the law at the Water Gate square in Jerusalem “before the assembly, both men and women and all who could hear with understanding” (Neh 8:2). The detail is revolutionary twice over. Women stand inside the listening assembly as full hearers. And thirteen named Levites move through the crowd giving the sense, “so that the people understood the reading” (Neh 8:7-8). Israel here invents something the Church must never lose: the ministry of explanation. An assembly where decisions are announced but never explained, where the word is proclaimed but never interpreted into the language of the hearers, has fallen below the standard of the Water Gate.

Example. Set three gatherings side by side for the class. The evening crowd on a platform at Sealdah shares a place but no bond; each person would board a different train without a backward glance. A wedding procession shares a bond of occasion; it walks together tonight and disperses tomorrow. The assembly at Sinai shares a bond of promise made before God and answered aloud; it remains a people even in the wilderness, even in exile. Crowd, function, covenant: ask the students which of the three their own parish most resembles on an ordinary Sunday.

The Council Reads the Pattern

Lumen Gentium 9 gathers this whole trajectory into a single sentence of doctrine: it has pleased God to sanctify and save human beings not merely as individuals, without any bond between them, but by making them into a people that acknowledges him in truth and serves him in holiness. The Council then names Israel expressly as the first form of that people and the Church as its fulfilment: a messianic people whose head is Christ, whose condition is the dignity and freedom of the children of God, whose law is the new commandment of love, and whose end is the Kingdom. Everything Class 1 asserted in general is here given its biblical charter. Communion is covenant bond; participation is the answering voice of the assembly; and mission is already present at Sinai, since the kingdom of priests exists “among all peoples; for all the earth is mine” (Ex 19:5). A priestly people is never for itself.

Indian Context. India also constituted herself through an assembly. Between December 1946 and November 1949 the Constituent Assembly deliberated clause by clause, with B. R. Ambedkar chairing the Drafting Committee, and gave the nation a founding text adopted in the name of We, the People. The analogy is genuinely instructive: publication, debate, explanation, corporate adoption. The difference is equally instructive: at Sinai the initiative is divine, and the assembly answers a grace it did not summon, whereas a constitution rests on popular sovereignty. The Church in India lives between these two grammars and must keep both truths. She owes the republic her full civic participation, and she owes God an obedience no majority can amend.

Questions for Discussion

Questions for Discussion

  1. The Census of 2011 counted more than 450 million internal migrants within India, and Bengal knows both departure and arrival. If Israel learned to be the People of God while on the move, what would a parish that took migrants as seriously as Scripture does look like in practice?
  2. At the Water Gate the law was read to men and women alike, and Levites gave the sense until the people understood. Which groups in your home parish hear decisions announced but never explained, and what would a ministry of explanation look like there?
  3. The Constituent Assembly gave India a founding covenant through public deliberation. What should the Church in India learn from that achievement, and at what point does covenant with God differ from constitution by consent?

CLASS 3: COMMUNAL DISCERNMENT AND PROPHETIC LISTENING

Module 2: Biblical Foundations I

Aim of the Class

Sinai showed a people that answers together; this class shows a people that must first hear together. It recovers the Hebrew verb shama, follows Israel as she learns discernment through elders, outsiders, children, and silence, and then meets the prophets, the men God raises when his people stop listening. By the end the students should be able to explain why, in biblical grammar, obedience is a form of listening, and why a synodal Church must give first hearing to those whom India refuses to hear.

One Verb for Hearing and Obeying

The daily creed of Israel begins with an imperative of the ear: “Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD” (Deut 6:4). The verb is shama, and it refuses to be split the way our languages split it. Shama means to hear, to listen, and to obey in one movement; biblical Hebrew possesses no separate everyday word for obey, because for Israel a hearing that does not move the feet has not yet happened. Latin preserved the same insight: oboedire, the root of obedience, is ob-audire, to listen towards someone. In both sacred tongues, then, obedience is not the crushing of the will but the completion of listening. This single philological fact reorders authority in the People of God. Even the king, Deuteronomy insists, must write out a copy of the law with his own hand and read it all the days of his life, so that his heart may not be lifted up above his brethren (Deut 17:18-20). Israel is to be a listening people under a listening king; rule that has stopped hearing has already lost its legitimacy.

How Israel Learned to Discern Together

Discernment in Israel is communal from the start, and the narratives are wonderfully concrete. In Exodus 18 the structure of shared judgement comes from outside the covenant itself: Jethro, a priest of Midian, watches Moses judging alone from morning till evening, tells him plainly that the thing is not good, and designs with him the tiers of elders that save both leader and people (Ex 18:13-27). Israel receives her first administrative reform from a religious outsider, a standing lesson in listening past our own borders. In Numbers 11 the LORD takes some of the spirit that is upon Moses and puts it on seventy elders; and when the spirit falls also on Eldad and Medad, two men outside the tent, Joshua begs Moses to stop them. The answer of Moses is the charter of everything this course will mean by the sensus fidei: would that all the people of the LORD were prophets, and that the LORD would put his spirit upon them all (Num 11:29). The spirit of discernment is not the private property of office.

Two further scenes complete the school. The boy Samuel hears a voice in the night and runs, three times, to the wrong door; it is old Eli, whose own sons no longer listen, who finally teaches the child the posture of Israel: “Speak, LORD, for your servant hears” (1 Sam 3:9). The old need the young in order to hear at all; the young need the old in order to know who is speaking. And Elijah at Horeb learns that the LORD is not in the wind, nor the earthquake, nor the fire, but in “a still small voice” (1 Kings 19:12). Communal discernment therefore requires an ecology of silence. An assembly that cannot be quiet together will mistake its own noise for the Spirit.

Example. Ask the class how many notifications their phones have received since the class began. We hear hundreds of sounds a day and listen to almost none of them; hearing has become cheap and listening rare. Then ask what happens inside a person when, in a crowded train compartment, someone suddenly speaks his name. Sound becomes address. Shama names exactly that difference: Israel is the people for whom the noise of the world has become the address of God.

The Prophets: Ears and Voice of the Covenant

What happens when the people will not hear? God does not cancel the covenant; he raises hearers by vocation. The nabi, the prophet, is first of all a listener seconded to a deaf nation. Abraham Heschel taught that the prophet is one who feels the pathos of God, who carries in his own nerves the divine grief over injustice, so that concern for justice in a human being is not sentiment but likeness to God. Walter Brueggemann adds the double task: prophetic speech dismantles the settled royal order that calls its own comfort peace, and energises the community with a newness it had stopped imagining. The pattern begins at the burning bush, where the vocation of Moses rests on the hearing of God himself: “I have seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt, and have heard their cry” (Ex 3:7). Whatever God hears first, the prophet must hear first.

That is why the prophets collide again and again with power on behalf of the unheard. Nathan traps David inside a parable until the king pronounces sentence on himself (2 Sam 12:1-7). Elijah confronts Ahab over the judicial murder of Naboth for a vineyard, the oldest story on record of land seized from a small man by the state (1 Kings 21). Amos stands in the royal sanctuary and announces that God despises the feasts and solemn assemblies of a people whose courts crush the poor: “let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream” (Amos 5:24). Watch the target carefully: it is the qahal itself, the worshipping assembly, that stands condemned when it sings well and hears nothing. Micah compresses the whole prophetic canon into one requirement: to do justice, to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God (Mic 6:8); and the last verb, walk, returns us to the vocabulary of Class 1.

The tradition is honest about its counterfeit. Hananiah preached exactly what a frightened nation wished to hear, and was dead within the year (Jer 28). Israel therefore developed tests for true prophecy that every synodal assembly still needs. The true word stands with the covenant and its poor rather than with the appetite of the crowd; it usually costs the speaker something; and it proves itself in fruit. Discernment of spirits, long before Ignatius, is in fact a biblical discipline of the listening assembly.

From Shama to Synod

Gather the threads. The Second Vatican Council opened its constitution on revelation by describing itself, before it teaches anything, as an assembly “hearing the word of God with reverence and proclaiming it with faith”. Pope Francis stood in the same grammar when he described the synodal Church as a listening Church in which everyone has something to learn: the faithful people, the college of bishops, the Bishop of Rome, each listening to the others, and all listening to the Spirit. And George Soares-Prabhu, reading the Bible with Indian eyes, insisted that the poor are not merely objects of the mercy of the Church but privileged hearers and interpreters of the word, so that a reading of Scripture which bypasses them bypasses the place where God has chosen to speak. When Class 14 examines the method the recent Synod called conversation in the Spirit, the students should recognise it at once: it is the discipline of shama, of Eli and Samuel, of Horeb silence and prophetic courage, finally given a procedure. The Bible does not merely permit a listening Church. It knows no other kind.

Indian Context. A listening Church in India must begin with those the nation has trained itself not to hear. National Crime Records Bureau figures work out to one recorded crime against a Dalit roughly every eighteen minutes, and the true figure is higher, since fear keeps many victims silent. More than ten thousand farmers lose their lives to suicide each year. Workers still die cleaning septic tanks by hand. Dalit Christians carry a double silence: excluded from Scheduled Caste protections by the Presidential Order of 1950 because of their Baptism, and, in some places, still given separate seating or separate burial ground within the Church herself. Exodus 3:7 states the order of divine attention: God hears the cry of the enslaved first. A synodal Church in India that does not give its first hearing to these voices may hold every meeting and still hear nothing.

Questions for Discussion

Questions for Discussion

  1. The record shows one crime against a Dalit about every eighteen minutes, and some Christian communities still keep separate seating or separate burial grounds. If shama means that to hear is already to begin to obey, what has our long refusal to hear Dalit Christians already decided about us?
  2. Moses wished that all the people of the LORD were prophets (Num 11:29). Name one lay man or woman, in your parish or village, whose word has functioned prophetically. What allowed that voice to be heard, or what silenced it?
  3. Hananiah preached what the nation wanted; Jeremiah preached what the covenant demanded. Between WhatsApp forwards, television debates, and pulpits, how should a seminarian in India today test which voices are prophetic and which are merely loud?

CLASS 4: JESUS FORMS A COMMUNITY OF DISCIPLES

Module 3: Biblical Foundations II

Aim of the Class

Module 2 watched a people constituted at Sinai and taught to listen through elders, children, and prophets. This class asks how Jesus of Nazareth gathers that inheritance and gives it a new centre in himself. Students should leave able to show that Jesus does not merely instruct individuals but forms a community with a distinctive shape, and able to read the first summaries of the church of Jerusalem in Acts 2 to 4 as the earliest picture of that community at prayer, at table, and in the sharing of goods. The word to master is koinonia.

A Master Who Forms a Body, Not a Following

The teachers of Israel gathered pupils; a rabbi with his disciples was a familiar sight. Yet the practice of Jesus differs at a decisive point that the students should grasp early. A disciple ordinarily chose his rabbi and, in time, became a rabbi himself with disciples of his own. The disciples of Jesus do not choose him; he calls them by name from their nets and their tax tables (Mk 1:16-20; 2:14), and they never outgrow him, for he alone remains the teacher and the way. What he forms, therefore, is not a school that reproduces masters but a community that keeps a single Lord.

The appointment of the Twelve makes the intention plain. Jesus goes up the mountain, the place of Sinai resonance, and appoints twelve, that they might be with him and that he might send them out to preach (Mk 3:13-19). The number is not administrative but prophetic: twelve for the twelve tribes, a signal that in this small company the whole People of God is being re-founded. Being with him comes first, sending follows; communion precedes mission in the very grammar of the call. And the company he forms is deliberately mixed, holding a tax collector who worked for Rome beside zealots who wanted Rome expelled, fishermen beside the educated, women who followed and provided out of their means (Lk 8:1-3) beside the men. A community that could contain such opposites had its unity from the one who called it, not from agreement among the called.

Two acts of Jesus give this community its inner law. At the Last Supper he takes the place of the household slave, washes the feet of the Twelve, and tells them plainly that authority among them is to be inverted: the greatest must serve (Jn 13:1-15; Lk 22:24-27). And he prays, on the night he is handed over, that they may all be one, as he and the Father are one (Jn 17:21). The unity of the disciple community is therefore not a human federation but a share in the unity of God, and its exercise of authority is not domination but the towel. Everything the later course will say about synodal office stands under these two acts.

Example. A cricket team and a crowd at a match are both large groups watching the same pitch. The crowd shares attention and nothing else; when the match ends it dissolves into thousands of strangers. The eleven on the field share a captain, a plan, assigned roles, and a common end, so that the failure of one is felt by all and the success of one belongs to all. Jesus did not gather a crowd around a teaching; he formed a team around himself. Ask the class which of the two a Sunday congregation more often resembles.

The First Portrait of the Church: Acts 2 to 4

When the Spirit is poured out at Pentecost, the community Jesus formed becomes visible in its settled life, and Luke paints it in two famous summaries. The first tells us that the believers devoted themselves to the teaching of the apostles and to the koinonia, to the breaking of bread and to the prayers (Acts 2:42). The Greek koinonia means far more than friendly fellowship; it means a real holding in common, a partnership in one life. Luke immediately shows what it costs: all who believed were together and had all things in common, ta panta koina, selling their possessions and distributing to any who had need (Acts 2:44-45). The second portrait sharpens it further: the company of those who believed were of one heart and soul, no one claimed private ownership of anything, and there was not a needy person among them, for distribution was made to each as any had need (Acts 4:32-35). Here the pattern of Sinai reappears transfigured. A people gathered by divine initiative, bound in a covenant now sealed in the blood of Christ, expresses its communion in the most concrete currency there is, which is property. The four marks of Acts 2:42, teaching, koinonia, breaking of bread, and prayer, remain to this day the load-bearing pillars of every Christian assembly, and the course will test every later structure against them.

Three points deserve the board. First, communion in Acts is visible and material; it touches the purse, not merely the heart, and a communion that costs the sharer nothing would not have been recognised by the church of Jerusalem as koinonia at all. Secondly, the goal of the sharing is stated with precision: that there be no needy person among them (Acts 4:34), which is the ancient covenant promise of Deuteronomy 15:4 finally kept. Thirdly, the community holds together word and table, apostolic teaching and the breaking of bread, so that doctrine and Eucharist are never separated from the care of the poor. Participation in the goods of the community and participation in its worship are one movement.

Communion, Participation, Mission in the Apostolic Community

The triad of the course is already whole in these chapters. Communion is the one heart and soul of Acts 4:32, grounded not in shared opinion but in the risen Lord and his Spirit. Participation is the material sharing in which every member both gives and receives, so that the community has no purely passive members and no needy ones either. Mission is the plain result Luke records: day by day the Lord added to their number those who were being saved (Acts 2:47), and the shared life itself was the argument, since the world was drawn less by what the believers said than by how they held everything in common. The order matters for our own day. The church of Jerusalem did not run an outreach programme; she lived a common life so compelling that it became mission. A community that must advertise its love has usually stopped living it.

Indian Context. The holding of all things in common speaks with force in India, where the gap between wealth and want is among the widest in the world and reaches inside the Church herself. Many Adivasi communities in Bengal and Jharkhand still practise forms of common holding of land and shared labour at harvest, and the older village ethic of no one eating while a neighbour starves is closer to Acts 4 than to the market. Yet the honest teacher must also name the counter-witness: in some parishes the seating, the associations, and even the burial grounds still follow caste and class, so that the one heart and soul of Acts 4:32 is contradicted at the very altar where it should be sealed. The small Christian communities of the Indian Church were built precisely to recover the neighbourhood koinonia of Acts, sharing faith, sharing need, and sharing decision at the level of the lane and the village. The question for the seminarian is stark: does the Eucharist he will preside over create the koinonia of Acts, or merely gather people who keep their possessions, their prejudices, and their distances intact?

Questions for Discussion

Questions for Discussion

  1. The church of Jerusalem measured its communion by whether any member was left in need (Acts 4:34). By that measure, and looking honestly at your home parish, is it a koinonia or a congregation that happens to share a building?
  2. Jesus formed one body out of a tax collector and a zealot, men who wanted opposite things for their nation. Where in the Church in India today are the divisions, of caste, language, or politics, that we treat as too deep for one heart and soul, and what would the towel of John 13 require of us there?
  3. Adivasi villages preserve real practices of common holding and shared labour, while the wider economy teaches every person to secure his own. What can the Church in India learn from these communities about the koinonia of Acts, and what must the Gospel still purify in them?

CLASS 5: THE COUNCIL OF JERUSALEM, THE ABIDING SYNODAL MODEL

Module 3: Biblical Foundations II

Aim of the Class

This is the pivot of the biblical modules and one of the most important classes of the whole course. In Acts 15 the apostolic church faces its first church-dividing question and answers it not by fiat but by an assembly that listens, disputes, weighs experience against Scripture, and decides together in the Spirit. Students should leave able to narrate the five movements of the assembly, to explain the formula “it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us”, and to state precisely why the tradition and the recent Synod both treat this chapter as the permanent pattern of synodal decision.

The Question That Could Have Split the Church

The crisis is real and near the bone. Gentiles are turning to Christ in Antioch without becoming Jews first, and men come down from Judea teaching that unless the converts are circumcised according to the custom of Moses they cannot be saved (Acts 15:1). Everything hangs on the answer. If circumcision and the whole law are required, the church remains a movement within Judaism and the mission to the nations dies at birth. If they are not, the church must explain how the ancient covenant relates to the new. This is not a quarrel over ceremony; it is the first great doctrinal question of Christian history, the question of grace and works, of whether salvation is the gift of Christ or the reward of the law. Paul will spend the letter to the Galatians on the same fire. The church could have fractured here, in its first generation, and did not, because it had a way of deciding that was neither the rule of one man nor the count of many votes.

The Five Movements of the Assembly

Read the chapter as a sequence of five deliberate movements, and hold the class to the order, for the order is the argument of this class.

First, the local church refers what it cannot settle alone. Antioch does not resolve the dispute by its own authority, nor does it simply submit; it appoints Paul and Barnabas and others to go up to the apostles and elders in Jerusalem about this question (Acts 15:2). A local difficulty that touches the whole church is carried to the wider church. The instinct is synodal before any procedure is named: no community decides in isolation a matter that belongs to the communion of all.

Secondly, the assembly gathers and lets the disagreement be spoken. The apostles and the elders were gathered together to consider this matter, and there was much debate (Acts 15:6-7). Luke does not hide the conflict; he records that the argument was long and sharp. A synodal assembly does not begin by suppressing dissent but by giving it air, because a decision reached without hearing the objection is a decision that has not been tested.

Thirdly, the assembly listens to experience before it reasons from Scripture. Peter rises and tells what God has already done, how the Spirit fell on the household of Cornelius, uncircumcised, cleansing their hearts by faith and making no distinction between them and the Jewish believers (Acts 15:7-11). Then Barnabas and Paul relate the signs and wonders God had done through them among the Gentiles (Acts 15:12). The order is striking and deliberate: the assembly attends first to what God is manifestly doing, to the lived testimony of the mission, and only then turns to the interpretation of the Scriptures. Discernment reads the acts of God in history alongside the word of God in the text.

Fourthly, Scripture is brought to interpret the experience, and one voice gathers the discernment. James, who presides in Jerusalem, speaks last. He takes the testimony of Peter and reads it in the light of the prophets, citing Amos on the rebuilding of the dwelling of David so that the rest of humanity may seek the Lord (Acts 15:13-18, quoting Amos 9:11-12). Experience did not overrule Scripture, nor Scripture silence experience; the one illuminated the other. Then James offers his judgement: he does not impose it, but proposes it to the assembly, that the Gentiles not be troubled, with four practical requirements to make table fellowship possible between Jewish and Gentile believers (Acts 15:19-21). The one who presides does not decide alone at the outset; he listens through the whole assembly and then articulates the discernment that has ripened in it.

Fifthly, the whole church owns and communicates the decision. It seemed good to the apostles and the elders, with the whole church, to choose men and send them to Antioch with a letter (Acts 15:22). The decision is not a private memorandum from the leadership but the act of the whole body, carried by chosen delegates and set down in writing so that all may know it. The letter opens with the sentence that has echoed through every council since: “it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us” (Acts 15:28).

It Has Seemed Good to the Holy Spirit and to Us

Everything in this class concentrates in that one clause, edoxen gar to Pneumati to hagio kai hemin. Two subjects share a single verb: the Holy Spirit and the assembled church decide together. The order is not accidental. The Spirit stands first, for the decision is not the church congratulating her own consensus but the church discerning the will of God; yet the church truly stands there too, joined by the small word and, for the Spirit chose to work through this human process of referral, debate, testimony, Scripture, and shared judgement rather than around it. Here is the whole theology of synodality in a sentence. The assembly is neither a democracy in which the majority creates the truth, nor an autocracy in which one voice dictates it, but a discernment in which a listening church receives together what the Spirit gives. Strip out the Spirit and the assembly becomes a parliament; strip out the church and the appeal to the Spirit becomes the alibi of the autocrat. The clause holds both, and the health of every synod since is measured by whether it can say those words truthfully.

Example. Watch how a good village elder settles a hard dispute under the banyan tree. He does not announce the verdict on arrival, nor does he count raised hands. He lets both sides speak until they are spent, he draws out the ones who have not spoken, he recalls what the elders before him decided in like cases, and only when the sense of the gathering has formed does he say aloud what everyone has begun to see. That is close to what James does in Acts 15. The presider gives voice to a discernment the assembly reached together; he does not manufacture it alone and impose it.

Why the Tradition Calls It the Model

The church has never forgotten this chapter. The ecumenical councils understood themselves as continuing the assembly of Jerusalem, and the recent synodal magisterium returns to it as the biblical charter of the whole enterprise. The International Theological Commission reads Acts 15 as the icon of the synodal church, the event in which the apostolic community shows, once for all, how the people of God resolves what threatens its communion, by convoking, listening, discerning, and deciding together under the word and in the Spirit. What the seminarian must carry away is that synodality is not a nervous modern experiment imported from secular assemblies, but the oldest way the church knows for deciding the questions that matter most. Sinai gave the answering assembly; the upper room gave the serving community; Jerusalem gives the discerning council. The three together are the biblical foundation on which every later chapter of this course is built.

Indian Context. The Church in India carries Acts 15 as an unfinished agenda, because her own great questions of inculturation are the same question in a new dress. Just as the church of Jerusalem had to decide whether a Gentile must first become a Jew to become a Christian, the Church in India has long wrestled with whether an Indian must first become culturally Western to become fully Catholic. The FABC has read Acts 15 precisely this way, as the warrant for a genuinely Asian face of the Church. Two Indian instincts prepare the ground for the method of Acts 15. The village panchayat and the tribal council both reach decisions less by counting than by talking a matter through until a shared mind forms, which is nearer to discernment than to the ballot. Yet the Gospel must purify these assemblies too, for the traditional panchayat often excluded women and the lower castes from the circle that decides, and a synodal church cannot inherit that exclusion. The task in India is to keep the patience of the indigenous assembly while widening its circle to the whole baptised people, so that the one who was left outside the banyan-tree council finds a voice inside the church of Christ.

Questions for Discussion

Questions for Discussion

  1. The Jerusalem assembly decided only after listening to lived experience, first Peter, then Paul and Barnabas. What would this order of listening before deciding look like in a parish council in West Bengal where domestic workers and migrant labourers sit beside professionals?
  2. Set the discernment of Acts 15 alongside decision making in a tribal village council or a gram sabha. What can the Church in India receive from these indigenous assemblies, and what must the Gospel purify in them?
  3. “It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us” (Acts 15:28). Where does this joint subject, Spirit together with community, unsettle any clerical monopoly over decisions in our dioceses today?

CLASS 6: LOCAL SYNODS AND THE FIRST ECUMENICAL COUNCILS

Module 4: The Early Church

Aim of the Class

Module 4 asks what the church did with the pattern of Acts 15 once the apostles had died and the communities had spread across the Mediterranean. The answer is that she made it into an institution. This class follows the birth of the synod as a regular structure: the letters of Ignatius of Antioch on the bishop within his council of presbyters and people, the principle of Cyprian of Carthage that he would decide nothing without the counsel of his clergy and the consent of his people, the rise of regular provincial synods, and their culmination in the first ecumenical council at Nicaea. Students should leave able to show that the synod is not a papal invention of 1965 but the ordinary constitution of the ancient church.

Ignatius: The Bishop Within the Assembly, Not Above It

Around the year 107, on the road to his martyrdom in Rome, Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, wrote letters to the churches that give us our earliest detailed picture of church order after the apostolic writings. He is famous for exalting the bishop, urging the communities to do nothing without him. Yet the careless reader stops too soon. For Ignatius the bishop never stands alone. He is always set within a threefold harmony: the bishop presides, the council of presbyters surrounds him like the apostles around Christ, and the deacons serve, and none of the three acts without the others or without the people. Ignatius reaches for musical images, the presbytery attuned to the bishop as strings to a lyre, the whole community singing in one voice. The point for this course is exact: the authority of the bishop in the ancient church was real, and it was corporate. He was the centre of an assembly, not a monarch over subjects, and to picture the early bishop as a solitary ruler is to misread the very texts that praise his office.

Cyprian: Nothing Without Counsel, Nothing Without Consent

The clearest ancient statement of synodal governance comes from Cyprian, bishop of Carthage from about 249 to 258, in the storm of the persecution of Decius and the bitter question of how to readmit those who had lapsed under torture. Cyprian could have ruled by decree; the crisis would have excused it. Instead he wrote to his clergy the sentence that the whole later tradition has treasured: from the beginning of his episcopate he had resolved to do nothing on his own private opinion without the counsel of his presbyters and the consent of his people. Two distinct acts are named, and the distinction still matters. From his clergy Cyprian sought consilium, counsel: the reasoned advice of those who share pastoral responsibility. From his people he sought consensus, consent: the agreement of the faithful without which a decision, though valid, would not be received. A bishop governs neither by his own will alone, nor by a mere vote of the people, but through counsel taken and consent secured. Cyprian also gave the church a lasting image of the whole episcopate as a single office held jointly, the episcopatus unus, in which each bishop holds the one episcopate in solidarity with all the others, so that the bishops too must act synodally among themselves.

Set beside Cyprian the practice that grew up across the same centuries: the regular provincial synod. Bishops of a region met, at first as need arose and then at fixed intervals, to settle disputed questions, to judge appeals, to guard the faith, and to keep the many local churches in one communion. By the fourth century such gatherings were so normal that the church legislated their frequency, the council of Nicaea requiring that the bishops of each province assemble twice a year. The synod, in other words, had become the ordinary machinery of church life, the standing expression at the regional level of the discernment first seen at Jerusalem.

Example. Think of how a large joint family in India settles a marriage or a partition of land. The eldest may preside, but he does not simply announce his will; he calls the brothers for counsel and he waits until the women and the wider family have given their assent, because a decision the family has not accepted will poison the household even if it is technically his to make. Cyprian governed his church in much the same spirit: counsel from those who share the burden, consent from those who must live with the result. The authority is real, but it works through the family, not over it.

Nicaea: The Assembly of the Whole World

What the provincial synod did for a region, the ecumenical council did for the whole church. When the teaching of Arius threatened the confession of Christ as truly God, the answer was not a ruling from a single see but a council. In 325 bishops gathered at Nicaea from across the known world and, after fierce debate, confessed the Son to be of one being with the Father, homoousios, and set down the creed the church still sings. The council understood itself as standing in the succession of Acts 15, deciding a church-dividing question by assembly, discernment, and common confession rather than by decree. The seven ecumenical councils of the first millennium became the supreme instances of synodality, the whole episcopate gathered to guard and to state the faith. The students should notice the pattern reaching all the way up: a difficulty referred, an assembly convoked, disagreement spoken, Scripture and tradition weighed, and a judgement owned by the whole body and sent out to the churches. The five verbs of Class 5 govern the council of the world as surely as the council of Jerusalem.

Indian Context. India possesses one of the oldest continuous experiences of synodal church life in the world, and the seminarian ought to know it. The Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala governed their communal affairs for centuries through the palliyogam, the parish assembly, and the yogam at higher levels, in which the laity held a genuine and settled voice in the temporal and even the disciplinary life of the church, long before such structures were discussed in the West. This indigenous synodality was steadily narrowed after the Synod of Diamper in 1599 and the Latinisation that followed, a history that still shapes debates in the Eastern Catholic churches of India. The lesson cuts two ways. It shows that synodal governance is native to Indian Christianity and not a foreign import, so that the present synodal reform recovers something the Indian Church already knew. And it warns that hard-won synodal structures can be dismantled by centralising pressure, which is precisely the story Module 5 will tell on a larger canvas. The Church in India has both a synodal inheritance to reclaim and a cautionary tale to remember, held within her own history.

Questions for Discussion

  1. Cyprian took counsel from his clergy and consent from his people, and treated the two as distinct. In your home diocese today, where is counsel genuinely sought, and where is the consent of the faithful assumed rather than won?
  2. The Saint Thomas Christians governed themselves through the palliyogam long before synodality became a slogan, and later saw that voice narrowed. What should the wider Church in India learn from both the achievement and its loss?
  3. Nicaea settled the deepest question about Christ not by decree but by an assembly of the whole church. What does it say about the nature of Christian truth that the Church has so often chosen to discern it together in council rather than to receive it from a single authority?

CLASS 7: MINISTRY WITHIN THE COMMUNITY AND MONASTIC DISCERNMENT

Module 4: The Early Church

Aim of the Class

Class 6 followed the synod outward, from the local church to the province to the council of the whole world. This class turns inward, to the shape of ministry within the single community and to the school of discernment that the monasteries became. Students should leave able to show that ordained ministry in the ancient church stood inside the people rather than above it, that bishops and deacons were often chosen with the participation of the community, and that the Rule of Benedict placed a settled duty on the abbot to consult the whole community, including its youngest members, before deciding grave matters.

Ministers Chosen With the People

The ancient church did not experience ordained ministry as a caste dropped upon the community from outside. Ministers rose from within the people and were frequently chosen with the participation of the people. The African councils over which Cyprian presided held that the choice of a bishop should be made in the presence of the people, who knew the life of each candidate, so that an unworthy man might be exposed and a worthy one confirmed by the testimony of those among whom he had lived. The famous rule attributed to Pope Celestine, that no bishop should be given to those unwilling to receive him, and the acclamation by which congregations sometimes elected their pastors, of which the sudden election of Ambrose at Milan is the best-known instance, both witness to the same conviction: the one who presides must be received by the community he serves. Election, examination, and consent belonged together, and the modern rediscovery of the participation of the faithful in the life of the church is in part a return to this ancient practice.

The deacon deserves particular attention, for the diaconate is the ministry of the ear and the hand within the community. From the appointment of the seven in Acts 6, chosen by the whole assembly to serve the neglected widows so that no group would be overlooked, the deacon was the minister who kept the community attentive to its poorest members. In the ancient church the deacon knew the households, carried the Eucharist to the absent, managed the relief of the needy, and brought the condition of the people to the bishop. Where the bishop presided, the deacon listened at the edges, and the two ministries together kept the church both ordered and attentive. A community that hears its poor is a community with living deacons, whatever their number.

The Monastery as a School of Discernment

When the age of the martyrs ended and the church settled into the world, it was the monasteries that became the laboratories of communal life, and it is there that the practice of shared discernment was most carefully refined. Basil of Caesarea, writing his rules for community in the fourth century, insisted that the monk does not seek the will of God in isolation but within the brotherhood, where the counsel of many and the correction of the community protect the individual from the deceptions of his own heart. For Basil the common life is not merely a convenience but a spiritual necessity, since charity cannot be practised alone and the gifts of one are given for the building of all. The monastery was, in this sense, a small synodal church, a place where the discernment of the will of God was a shared labour.

The Rule of Benedict, written in the sixth century and destined to shape the whole of Western monastic and, through it, ecclesial life, gave this discernment a durable form in its third chapter, on calling the brethren to counsel. The chapter deserves to be read aloud in class. Whenever any weighty matter is to be decided in the monastery, Benedict directs, the abbot shall call the whole community together and set the matter before them. He then adds the sentence that startles every reader: the reason the whole community is summoned is that the Lord often reveals to the younger what is better. The youngest and least senior monk may be the one through whom God speaks, and so all must be heard. The brothers give their counsel with humility and do not presume to defend their own views stubbornly; the decision rests finally with the abbot, who must weigh what he has heard and then determine what is best. Here is the ancient synodal balance held with perfect precision: universal consultation and personal responsibility, everyone heard and one deciding, the voice of the young protected and the office of the one who presides preserved.

Example. Picture a village headmaster deciding whether to change the timing of the school day during the monsoon. A foolish head announces the new timing on a notice at the gate. A wise head calls the teachers, the older students, the parents who walk their children along the flooded road, and even the youngest child who knows which lane fills first, and only then decides. He still decides, and the decision is his; but he decides having heard the one whose feet are actually in the water. That is precisely the instinct of chapter three of the Rule of Benedict: the Lord often reveals to the younger what is better.

Indian Context. The Indian religious inheritance holds its own schools of communal discernment, and the seminarian formed in a religious congregation already knows several of them. The chapter of a religious house, in which the community gathers to decide its common affairs and to elect its superiors, is a direct descendant of chapter three of the Rule of Benedict, and it remains one of the most genuinely synodal structures the Indian Church possesses. The ashram tradition, taken up by Christian pioneers such as those at Shantivanam and Kurisumala, sought a common life of shared prayer and shared decision in an Indian idiom. Even the older tribal institutions carry the same instinct, for the parha of the Oraons and the manjhi-than of the Santals reach decisions by talking a matter through the whole gathering rather than by the will of one man. The task before the Indian Church is to let these living schools of discernment shape the parish and the diocese, so that the synodal habit practised in the novitiate and the tribal village is not left behind at the door of the presbytery.

Questions for Discussion

  1. In the ancient church the community that knew a candidate testified to his fitness before he was ordained. What would it change in the formation and appointment of priests in India today if the people who had lived alongside a candidate were genuinely heard?
  2. Benedict summoned the whole community because the Lord often reveals to the younger what is better. In your diocese or congregation, where is the voice of the youngest genuinely sought, and where is seniority allowed to silence it?
  3. The chapter of a religious house and the tribal parha both decide by talking a matter through the whole gathering. What would a parish pastoral council in West Bengal look like if it truly worked in that manner rather than merely ratifying decisions already taken?

CLASS 8: MEDIEVAL DEVELOPMENT AND DECLINE

Module 5: From the Middle Ages to the Modern Period

Aim of the Class

This class narrates the hardest stretch of the whole history, the long centuries in which the synodal instinct of the ancient church weakened while authority gathered increasingly at the centre. Students should leave able to explain why councils continued yet changed in character, what the conciliarist crisis of the fifteenth century was and why the church rejected its extreme form, how the Council of Trent both reformed the church and, in its aftermath, tightened central control, and how this centralising current reached its high point at the First Vatican Council. The class is not an accusation but a diagnosis, so that the students may understand what the twentieth century set out to recover.

Councils That Continued but Changed

The medieval church never stopped holding councils, and the great councils of the West, from the Lateran gatherings to Lyons and Vienne, legislated for the whole church with real authority. Yet their character shifted in ways the students should notice. The ancient council had been chiefly an assembly of bishops discerning the faith in communion with one another and with their churches. The medieval general council increasingly became an instrument of the papacy, convoked by the pope, presided over in his name, and understood as deriving its authority from him. The participation of the wider church thinned. The sensus fidei of the whole body of the faithful, so alive in the ancient centuries, was less and less invoked as a source of the discernment of the church. Authority did not disappear from the church; it concentrated. The living circulation between the head and the members, which the ancient synod had kept in motion, began to slow.

The Conciliarist Crisis

The concentration provoked a reaction, and the reaction overreached. In 1378 the Western church fell into the scandal of two, and then three, rival claimants to the papacy, and no one on earth held clear authority to judge among them. Into this vacuum stepped the conciliarist theologians, who argued that a general council, representing the whole church, stood above the pope and could judge and depose him. The Council of Constance, which finally ended the schism in 1417 by securing the resignation or deposition of the rival claimants and electing a single pope, issued in its decree Haec Sancta the strong claim that the council held its power immediately from Christ and that everyone, including the pope, was bound to obey it in matters of faith and the reform of the church. Constance ended the schism, a genuine service to the church, but the principle it invoked contained a grave danger, for a council permanently superior to the pope would have dissolved the office of unity that holds the whole church in communion.

The church, in the end, did not receive the extreme conciliarist thesis. The claim that a council stands habitually above the pope was rejected, and the papacy recovered its central place. Yet the manner of the rejection carried a cost that the honest teacher must name. In recoiling from conciliarism the church grew suspicious of the general council itself, so that the very instrument of synodal discernment came to be regarded with wariness, as a potential threat to unity rather than as a normal organ of the life of the church. For long stretches after Constance, councils were feared more than they were desired. The synodal principle, wounded in the reaction against its own distortion, was slow to heal.

Trent: Reform and the Tightening That Followed

The Council of Trent, meeting across the middle of the sixteenth century in answer to the Protestant Reformation, was a true and necessary reforming council, and it must not be caricatured. It clarified the faith on Scripture and tradition, justification, and the sacraments, and it reformed the life of the church in lasting ways, above all by requiring every diocese to establish a seminary for the training of its priests, the very institution in which these notes are read. Trent also legislated for regular provincial and diocesan synods, intending that the synodal machinery of the ancient church should run again. Yet history took a different turn from the intention. In the centuries after Trent the implementation of its reforms was increasingly directed from Rome through the new permanent congregations of the Roman Curia, which interpreted the council, resolved disputed questions, and supervised the local churches. The provincial synods that Trent had mandated gradually fell into disuse. The result was a church more disciplined, better instructed, and more uniform than before, and at the same time more centralised, in which the initiative of the local church and the participation of its faithful were quietly reduced. The reform succeeded; the synodal life it had meant to revive did not.

Example. Consider a large trust that runs many schools across several states. In a season of crisis the trustees rightly impose uniform standards from the head office to save the schools from collapse, and the schools are in fact saved and improved. But if, once the crisis passes, every decision continues to be referred to the head office, and the local school councils that once knew their own children stop meeting, then something living has been lost even though the schools are well run. Efficiency at the centre has been purchased at the price of participation in the parts. That is close to what befell the church in the centuries after Trent: real reform, real gain, and a quiet cost in synodal life.

The Centralised High Point

The centralising current reached its high point at the First Vatican Council in 1870, which defined the primacy of jurisdiction of the Bishop of Rome over the whole church and, under carefully guarded conditions, the infallibility of his solemn definitions of faith and morals. These definitions belong to the faith of the church and are not in question here. What the students must understand is the historical shape of the moment. Pastor Aeternus was intended to be one half of a fuller teaching on the church, but the council was suspended when war broke out and never returned to complete the picture. The result was that the church entered the twentieth century with a solemn definition of the office of the one who presides and no corresponding conciliar teaching on the bishops gathered around him or on the whole People of God. The head had been defined without the body. It would be the work of the Second Vatican Council, ninety years later, to supply what had been left unspoken, setting the primacy within the collegiality of the bishops and the collegiality of the bishops within the People of God. Class 10 will take up that recovery. The students should leave this class understanding that Vatican II did not invent synodality but restored a balance that centuries of centralisation had let fall.

Indian Context. The Indian Church knows the weight of centralisation from her own history, and from two directions at once. Within the church, the Latinisation imposed on the ancient Saint Thomas Christians after the Synod of Diamper in 1599 is the same story in miniature: an indigenous synodal tradition overridden by a centralising authority that trusted uniformity more than participation, a wound whose healing is still sought in the Eastern Catholic churches of India today. From the wider society, the long experience of centralised colonial administration, which concentrated every decision in a distant capital and reduced the local community to a recipient of orders, left a habit of mind that the Indian Church has not wholly escaped, so that many a diocese and parish still waits for instruction from above rather than discerning together below. The history in this class is therefore not the record of a foreign past but a mirror. The Church in India, having tasted both the wound of internal Latinisation and the habit of colonial centralism, has particular reason to welcome a reform that returns discernment to the whole body.

  2. The Latinisation of the Saint Thomas Christians after Diamper overrode a living synodal tradition in the name of uniformity. What should the whole Indian Church, Latin and Eastern together, learn from that history as she takes up the present reform?

CLASS 9: REFORM CURRENTS AND RECOVERY BEFORE VATICAN II

Module 5: From the Middle Ages to the Modern Period

Aim of the Class

Class 8 ended at the centralised high point of 1870. This class tells the more hopeful half of the same century, the recovery that prepared the ground for the Second Vatican Council. Students should leave able to describe three currents that kept the synodal memory alive and gave it fresh theological force: the rediscovery of the church as a living communion animated by the Spirit in the work of Johann Adam Moehler, the argument of John Henry Newman that the faithful must be consulted in matters of doctrine, and the liturgical movement that restored to the whole people its active part in the worship of the church. Together these currents form the seedbed of the recovery that Module 6 will harvest.

Moehler: The Church as a Living Communion

The recovery began, as recoveries often do, with a return to the ancient sources. Johann Adam Moehler, teaching at Tuebingen in the eighteen-twenties, immersed himself in the writings of the Fathers and came away with a picture of the church very different from the juridical model then dominant. In his early work on the unity of the church, Moehler described the church not first as an institution governed from above but as a living communion, a fellowship brought into being and held together by the Holy Spirit poured into the hearts of the faithful. Unity, for Moehler, wells up from within, from the one Spirit dwelling in all the members, before it is expressed in the outward bonds of office and structure. This was the ancient vision of communion recovered against the centuries of centralisation, and though Moehler matured and qualified his early enthusiasm, his central insight passed into the bloodstream of Catholic theology and reached its full flowering at the Second Vatican Council. To see the church as communion before institution is to see her, once again, as the synodal body the ancient church had known.

Newman: On Consulting the Faithful

The sharpest single contribution came from John Henry Newman, whose essay of 1859, published in the journal The Rambler, bore a title that reopened the whole question of this course: On Consulting the Faithful in Matters of Doctrine. Newman studied the great Arian crisis of the fourth century and drew from it an observation that scandalised some of his contemporaries: during that crisis, when many bishops wavered and compromised, it was often the mass of the ordinary faithful who held firm to the true confession of the divinity of Christ. The faith, Newman argued, had in that hour been maintained more by the body of the faithful than by the episcopate. He therefore insisted that the sensus fidelium, the instinct for the truth that lives in the whole believing people, is a real witness to divine revelation and ought to be consulted, in the way that one consults a barometer, as a genuine indicator of the mind of the church. Newman was careful and precise; he did not make the faithful judges over the bishops, but he refused to let them be treated as merely passive recipients with nothing to contribute to the discernment of the truth. For this essay he fell under a cloud of suspicion in his own lifetime, delated to Rome and misunderstood for years. Yet a century later the Second Vatican Council would teach exactly his point, that the whole body of the faithful, anointed by the Holy One, cannot err in belief, and Newman would be vindicated, canonised, and named a witness to the very doctrine for which he had been suspected.

Example. A wise doctor treating a patient does not rely on the instruments alone; he also asks the patient where it hurts, because the one who lives inside the body knows something the charts cannot show. He remains the doctor, and the diagnosis is his; but he would be a fool to ignore the testimony of the one whose body it is. Newman asked the church to treat the faithful in just this way: not as the physicians, but as witnesses whose lived sense of the faith carries real information about the health of the body. To stop consulting them is to diagnose the church with the instruments switched off.

The Liturgical Movement: The People Take Their Part

The third current flowed through the worship of the church. Beginning in the monasteries of the nineteenth century and gathering force in the twentieth, the liturgical movement sought to recover the liturgy as the action of the whole assembled people rather than a rite performed by the priest while the people watched in silence. Its watchword became active participation, participatio actuosa, a phrase Pope Pius the Tenth used as early as 1903 when he called the active participation of the faithful in the sacred mysteries the foremost and indispensable source of the true Christian spirit. Pioneers such as Lambert Beauduin in Belgium and the monks of the great liturgical abbeys taught that the people gathered for the Eucharist are not an audience but a priestly assembly, called to offer and to respond, and that a church which restores to the people their active part in worship will, in time, restore to them their active part in her whole life. The instinct proved true. The liturgical recovery of the assembly as a participating body prepared the church, at the deepest level, for the recovery of the participating body in every other dimension, for a people accustomed to answer aloud at the altar will not long remain silent in the councils of the church. Worship reformed the imagination of the church before doctrine reformed her structures.

Indian Context. The Indian Church has her own long labour of liturgical participation, and it stands unfinished. The movement for the Indianisation of worship after the Second Vatican Council, the search for an authentically Indian expression of the Eucharist in gesture, posture, music, and tongue, is the same instinct at work in Indian soil, the conviction that the whole people, praying in its own idiom, must take an active part rather than receive a rite in a foreign form. Yet participation in worship in India is still blocked by the very barrier the liturgical movement most needed to overcome, for in some parishes caste still governs who may enter the sanctuary, who may distribute communion, who may sit in the front, and even where the dead of a lower caste may be buried. A liturgy in which the whole priestly people genuinely takes its part cannot coexist with a sanctuary divided by caste. The unfinished liturgical reform in India is therefore also a synodal reform, for the assembly that cannot yet worship as one body will not easily learn to discern as one body.

CLASS 10: LUMEN GENTIUM, THE PEOPLE OF GOD AND COLLEGIALITY

Module 6: The Second Vatican Council

Aim of the Class

This is the doctrinal centre of the whole course, the class towards which the first nine have been walking and from which the last five will draw. In the Dogmatic Constitution on the Church the Second Vatican Council recovered, as settled teaching, the very things that Module 5 watched decline: the whole People of God before the hierarchy, the instinct for the faith that lives in every believer, and the college of bishops gathered around and under the Bishop of Rome. Students should leave able to explain the order of the chapters of Lumen Gentium, to define the sensus fidei with precision, and to state the doctrine of episcopal collegiality together with its guard, the principle cum Petro et sub Petro.

The Order of the Chapters Is Itself Doctrine

The first thing to teach about Lumen Gentium is its architecture, because the Council preached by the order in which it placed its chapters. The original schema prepared before the Council opened had followed the centralised pattern of the preceding centuries, treating the hierarchy first and the body of the faithful afterwards, as though the church were essentially the clergy with laity attached. The Council fathers rejected that draft and reordered the whole document. Chapter I speaks of the mystery of the church; chapter II, before any word on popes or bishops, speaks of the whole People of God; only in chapter III does the Council turn to the hierarchy, and there it presents ordained ministry expressly as service within the people, not as a rank above it. The reversal was deliberate and it is doctrine in the form of a table of contents. What is common to all the baptised, the dignity of the children of God, comes first; the distinctions of office are real, but they exist inside the one people and for its sake. Every claim this course has made about synodality rests on that single editorial decision, for a church that thinks of itself first as the whole People of God will govern itself differently from a church that thinks of itself first as a hierarchy.

A Priestly, Prophetic, and Kingly People

Chapter II teaches that the whole People of God shares, by Baptism, in the threefold office of Christ as priest, prophet, and king. This is the doctrinal ground of participation. Every one of the baptised is consecrated by Baptism into a holy priesthood, offering spiritual sacrifices and witnessing to Christ; the common priesthood of the faithful and the ministerial priesthood differ not merely in degree but in kind, and yet the two are ordered to one another, the ordained existing to serve the priesthood of the whole body. Because all share the prophetic office of Christ, the Council can make the claim that gathers up the whole argument of Newman from Class 9 and gives it conciliar authority: the body of the faithful as a whole, anointed by the Holy One, cannot err in matters of belief, and it shows this instinct through the supernatural sense of the faith of the whole people, from the bishops down to the last of the faithful, when it manifests a universal agreement in matters of faith and morals. Here is the doctrine the course has been building towards. The teaching church and the learning church are not two separate bodies; the same one People of God both hands on the faith and carries within itself, by the gift of the Spirit, a genuine instinct for its truth.

The Sensus Fidei, Rightly Understood

The sensus fidei must be taught with precision, because it is the most abused idea in the whole course. It is a supernatural instinct, a gift of the Holy Spirit given with the faith itself, by which the believing people recognises what accords with the Gospel and recoils from what betrays it, in the way a healthy body recognises wholesome food and rejects poison. Three things must be held together for the students to use the idea rightly. The sensus fidei is supernatural, not a matter of natural opinion; it belongs to a people formed by Scripture, the sacraments, and prayer, not to the nominally baptised who have ceased to believe or practise. It is a sense of the faith, not a vote on the faith; it recognises the truth handed down, it does not manufacture new truth by majority. And it is discerned, not counted; the pastors of the church must listen for it, weigh it, and confirm it, for it is read by discernment and not measured by a survey. Hold these three and the sensus fidei is the engine of a listening church. Loosen them and it becomes the slogan of every faction that wishes to bind the church to the mood of the hour.

Example. A family that has cooked one dish for four generations carries a knowledge no written recipe holds. The grandmother cannot always state the rule, yet she knows at once when the proportion is wrong, because the dish lives in her hands. If a clever visitor insists on altering it by a formula, the whole family senses the loss before they can argue it. That instinct is not a vote and not a mere preference; it is a knowledge born of long fidelity to the thing itself. The sensus fidei is that kind of knowledge in the believing people: not the opinion of the passer-by, but the trained instinct of those who have kept the faith with their whole lives.

Episcopal Collegiality

The third recovery of Lumen Gentium supplied precisely what Vatican I had been suspended before it could say. Chapter III teaches that the bishops form a single college, the successor of the college of the apostles, and that this college, together with its head the Bishop of Rome and never without him, holds supreme and full authority over the whole church. Two truths are joined here that the centuries had struggled to hold together. The primacy of the Bishop of Rome, defined at Vatican I, remains whole and untouched. And the bishops are not merely his branch managers, each isolated in his own diocese and receiving all direction from the centre, but a true college that shares, with him and under him, responsibility for the whole church. The Council expressed the balance in a phrase the students must carry: the college of bishops exercises its authority cum Petro et sub Petro, with Peter and under Peter. With Peter, so that the bishops are genuine sharers in the governance of the universal church and not mere executors; under Peter, so that their collegial action is always in communion with the one who holds the office of unity and never against him or without him. Collegiality is the doctrinal seed of everything the rest of the course will call synodality at the level of the bishops.

Indian Context. The teaching of Lumen Gentium on the whole People of God fell on Indian soil already prepared to receive it and already resistant to it, and both must be named. On the side of reception, the Indian Church quickly took up the People of God as the ground for small Christian communities, for the pastoral councils that Class 11 will treat, and for a laity conscious of a genuine share in the mission of the church. On the side of resistance, the doctrine that every baptised person shares the priestly, prophetic, and kingly office of Christ collides directly with a caste order that ranks persons by birth and with a clericalism that treats the priest as a sacred person set above an inferior laity. The sensus fidei of the whole people cannot be heard where the voice of the Dalit Christian, the woman, and the poor is silenced before it is spoken. To teach Lumen Gentium honestly in India is therefore to teach a doctrine that judges the church that teaches it, for a church that confesses one dignity in Baptism while practising many dignities by caste has not yet believed its own constitution.

CLASS 11: GAUDIUM ET SPES AND THE PARTICIPATORY STRUCTURES

Module 6: The Second Vatican Council

Aim of the Class

Class 10 gave the doctrine of the People of God; this class gives its turn towards the world and its translation into working structures. In Gaudium et Spes the Council set the church to read the signs of the times and to serve the human family, and in a set of accompanying documents and later norms it built the councils and assemblies through which the participation taught in Lumen Gentium could become daily practice. Students should leave able to explain the phrase reading the signs of the times, to describe the four participatory bodies that give synodality its ordinary machinery, and to state why a doctrine of participation without structures of participation remains a dead letter.

A Church That Reads the Signs of the Times

Gaudium et Spes, the Pastoral Constitution on the Church in the Modern World, opens with one of the most quoted sentences of the Council, which declares that the joys and the hopes, the griefs and the anxieties of the people of this age, above all of those who are poor or in any way afflicted, are also the joys and hopes and griefs and anxieties of the followers of Christ. The church here refuses to stand apart from the world as a fortress and steps into the world as a servant. The document then lays on the whole church a permanent duty: to scrutinise the signs of the times and to interpret them in the light of the Gospel, so that the church may respond to the enduring questions of humanity in the language of each generation. This duty of reading the signs of the times is the outward face of synodality. A church that listens to the Holy Spirit must also listen to the age in which the Spirit is at work, attending to the movements, the sufferings, and the hopes of real people as a place where God is speaking. Discernment, which Module 2 traced in the assemblies of Israel, is here turned towards history itself. The Council thus binds together the two directions of listening that the whole course has followed: listening within the church to the sensus fidei of the faithful, and listening outward to the cry of the world, both under the one word of God.

From Doctrine to Structure: The Four Councils

A teaching of participation that built no structures of participation would have died with the generation that proclaimed it. The Council and the reform that followed therefore created, or restored, four bodies through which the whole People of God could share in the discernment and the mission of the church. The students should hold all four, for these are the ordinary machinery of synodality between one great assembly and the next.

First, the parish pastoral council. The Council desired that in each diocese, so far as possible, councils be established to assist the apostolic work of the church, whether in evangelisation, in works of charity, or in other matters, with clergy, religious, and laity working together. At the level of the parish this became the pastoral council, the body in which the baptised of a place share in discerning the mission of their own community. Where it is alive, the prophetic office of the laity has a real organ; where it exists only on paper, or merely ratifies decisions already taken by the priest, the participation taught by Lumen Gentium is quietly denied.

Secondly, the diocesan pastoral council. The Decree on the Pastoral Office of Bishops asked that in each diocese a pastoral council be set up, over which the bishop presides, in which chosen clergy, religious, and laity study and weigh the pastoral undertakings of the diocese and draw practical conclusions about them. This body lifts the participation of the whole people from the single parish to the level of the local church around its bishop.

Thirdly, the presbyteral council, the senate of priests. The same Decree provided that the bishop have around him a council or senate of priests representing the presbyterate, whose counsel can effectively assist him in the governance of the diocese. This is the modern form of the ancient council of presbyters that surrounded the bishop in the letters of Ignatius, studied in Class 6, now given stable shape. It expresses the collegial bond between a bishop and his priests, so that he governs with their counsel and not by his will alone.

Fourthly, the conference of bishops. The Council gave stable form to the gathering of the bishops of a nation or region into an episcopal conference, in which they jointly exercise their pastoral office for the greater good of the church in their territory. The conference is collegiality made regular below the level of the universal college, the bishops of a region discerning together the mission of the church among their own peoples, and it is the direct descendant of the provincial synods traced in Class 6.

Example. Consider a large panchayat samiti that governs many villages. Suppose its constitution grants every villager a real voice, yet no gram sabha ever meets, no ward committee is convened, and the block office decides everything alone. The right of participation would exist on paper and be worth nothing in fact, because a right without a structure through which to exercise it is no right at all. The four councils of the church are exactly those structures. Lumen Gentium granted the right of participation; these bodies are the meetings at which it is exercised. A parish that never convenes its pastoral council has, in practice, repealed the teaching of the Council for its people.

Indian Context. The Indian Church has one of the richest experiences of participatory structures in the Catholic world, and also one of the widest gaps between structure on paper and structure in practice. The small Christian communities, the basic ecclesial communities built lane by lane and village by village, are among the most genuine embodiments of the participation Gaudium et Spes intended, gathering neighbours to read the word, to share their needs, and to act together for justice in their locality. The Indian episcopal conferences and the wider Asian gathering of bishops have read the signs of the times with real courage, naming poverty, communalism, and the wounds of caste as the places where the Gospel must speak. Yet in many a parish the pastoral council does not meet, or meets only to approve the accounts and the feast-day arrangements, while the real decisions are taken by the priest alone, and the diocesan pastoral council exists more in the statutes than in the calendar. The signs of the times in India, the migrant on the train, the farmer in debt, the Dalit at the edge of the village, are precisely the joys and griefs that Gaudium et Spes made the church own as her own. A participatory structure that never turns to face them has forgotten why it was built.

CLASS 12: APOSTOLICA SOLLICITUDO AND THE SYNOD OF BISHOPS

Module 7: The Synod of Bishops

Aim of the Class

Class 11 built the participatory structures below the level of the universal church. This class follows the same principle to the top, tracing the permanent Synod of Bishops from its institution by Paul VI in 1965 to its transformation by Pope Francis in 2018. Students should leave able to explain why Paul VI created the Synod, what its original consultative character was, how the assemblies developed across half a century, and how the apostolic constitution Episcopalis Communio reshaped the Synod from a single event into a whole process that begins and ends in the local churches.

Why Paul VI Created the Synod

The Second Vatican Council had taught that the bishops form a college sharing responsibility for the whole church, but a college that meets only in a full ecumenical council, a rare and vast undertaking, could not easily exercise that shared responsibility in the ordinary life of the church. Paul VI answered this need while the Council was still sitting. On 15 September 1965 he instituted, through the motu proprio Apostolica Sollicitudo, a permanent Synod of Bishops, a standing body of bishops chosen for the most part by the episcopal conferences of the world, to be convoked by the Bishop of Rome to offer their counsel on the questions he set before them. The name was chosen with care, for synodos, as Class 1 recovered, means a walking together, and the new body was meant to keep the bishops of the world walking with their head between the great councils. Two features of the original institution must be fixed clearly. The Synod was permanent as an institution, though it acted through particular assemblies convoked from time to time. And its role was consultative: it existed to advise the Bishop of Rome, who could grant it deliberative power in a given case but who otherwise received its counsel and then decided. The Synod was, from birth, an instrument of collegial counsel, not a parliament sharing sovereignty.

Half a Century of Assemblies

From 1967 onward the Synod met in a long series of assemblies, and the students should grasp their range rather than memorise a list. Ordinary general assemblies took up broad questions of the life of the whole church, from evangelisation and catechesis to the family, the word of God, and the priesthood. Extraordinary assemblies were convoked for urgent matters, including the assembly of 1985 that took stock of the Council twenty years on and gave the church the language of communion as the guiding idea for reading all its documents. Special assemblies gathered the bishops of a single continent or region, and it is here that the Indian Church has her direct stake, for the special assembly for Asia in 1998 produced the vision of a church with a genuinely Asian face, at home among the poor, the cultures, and the religions of the continent. Across these decades the Synod became the ordinary organ through which the concerns of the local churches were carried to the centre and the counsel of the world episcopate was offered to the pope. Yet a limit was increasingly felt. The Synod remained an assembly of bishops meeting in Rome, and the wider People of God, whose sensus fidei Lumen Gentium had honoured, had no clear place in its working. The instrument of collegiality had been built; the instrument of the fuller synodality was still to come.

Example. Think of a large and scattered family firm whose branches lie across many states. The founder alone cannot hold a full assembly of every worker for each decision, so he forms a standing committee of branch heads who gather from time to time to advise him on the questions he puts to them. This keeps the firm united and the founder informed between the rare full gatherings. But suppose the ordinary workers, who know the customers and the local markets better than anyone, are never consulted, and the committee hears only the branch heads. The firm would be well governed at the top and deaf at the base. That was the strength and the limit of the Synod in its first fifty years: a real instrument of counsel among the leaders, not yet a channel for the discernment of the whole body.

Episcopalis Communio: From Event to Process

In 2018 Pope Francis reshaped the Synod through the apostolic constitution Episcopalis Communio, and this is the pivot of the class. The document changed the Synod from a single event, a meeting of bishops in Rome, into an entire process with three phases: a preparatory phase of consultation that begins in the local churches, the celebratory phase of the assembly itself, and an implementation phase that returns the fruit of the assembly to the local churches to be received and put into practice. The consequence for the theology of this course is large. The constitution states that the Synod, though composed chiefly of bishops, does not act separately from the rest of the faithful but is an apt instrument to give voice to the whole People of God, precisely through the bishops, who are set as guardians and interpreters of the faith of their people. Two principles from earlier classes are joined here. The consultation of the faithful, which Newman defended in Class 9 and Lumen Gentium taught in Class 10, is now written into the working of the universal Synod, so that the process must begin by listening to the People of God in every local church. And the discernment of the bishops, the collegiality of Class 10, remains the means by which that listening is gathered, weighed, and offered to the Bishop of Rome. Episcopalis Communio thus turns the Synod into an exercise of synodality in the full sense, the whole People of God consulted, the bishops discerning, and the head confirming, which is precisely the pattern of Acts 15 from Class 5, now given a worldwide procedure.

The constitution also settled the standing of the final document of an assembly. When the members of an assembly approve a final document and the Bishop of Rome ratifies it, that document participates in the ordinary magisterium of the successor of Peter. This provision, little noticed in 2018, became decisive in 2024, when Pope Francis chose not to write a separate exhortation after the assembly on synodality but to give the final document itself this magisterial standing, as Class 1 anticipated and Class 14 will treat in full. The Synod, in other words, was made ready by Episcopalis Communio to carry the weight that the synodal process of 2021 to 2024 would place upon it.

Indian Context. For the Indian Church the reform of the Synod is not a distant Roman affair but a summons that reaches to the parish, because the process now begins with the listening of the local church. When Episcopalis Communio requires that the synodal process open by consulting the People of God in every place, it requires the diocese in Bengal and the parish in the coalfields to gather and to listen before anything is carried to Rome, which is a demand the older model never made of them. The special assembly for Asia had already given the Indian bishops the vision of a church at home among the poor, the cultures, and the many religions of this land; Episcopalis Communio now gives the method by which the whole People of God, and not the bishops alone, can share in shaping that church. The test for the Indian Church is whether the consultation reaches those it has always found easiest to skip, the Dalit Christian, the Adivasi, the woman, the labourer, or whether it gathers only the familiar voices of the parish council and calls that the sensus fidei. The instrument now exists; whether it hears the whole body is a question India must answer in her own dioceses.

CLASS 13: THE SYNODAL VISION OF POPE FRANCIS

Module 8: Pope Francis and the Synodal Church

Aim of the Class

The recovery traced through Module 6 needed a hand to set it in motion for the whole church, and that hand was the pontificate of Pope Francis. This class gathers his synodal vision as it stands in his teaching: the missionary church that goes out to the margins, the church that listens with the whole People of God, and the inverted pyramid in which those who preside stand beneath and serve. Students should leave able to state the shape of the vision of Francis from Evangelii Gaudium and the address of 17 October 2015, to connect it to the doctrine of Module 6, and to place it correctly now that his pontificate has closed and his successor carries the same vision forward.

A Church That Goes Forth

The synodal vision of Francis begins not with structures but with mission, and this order is deliberate. In the apostolic exhortation Evangelii Gaudium of 2013, the programmatic document of his pontificate, Francis called for a church that goes out from herself towards the margins, and declared that he preferred a church that is bruised, hurting, and dirty because it has been out on the streets to a church that is unhealthy from being confined and from clinging to its own security. The whole synodal reform stands under this priority. Francis never sought participation for its own sake, as though the church needed better meetings; he sought a church so alive in communion and so shared in participation that she would be driven outward in mission to the poor, the migrant, and the forgotten. Evangelii Gaudium also grounded the dignity of every believer in the same exhortation, teaching that by Baptism all the members of the People of God have become missionary disciples, so that evangelisation is the task of the whole people and not of a clerical caste alone. The line from Lumen Gentium runs straight through: the priestly, prophetic, and kingly people of Class 10 is here sent to the streets. Synodality, for Francis, is what a missionary church looks like from the inside, the shared life that makes the going forth possible.

A Church That Listens

On 17 October 2015, at the ceremony marking fifty years since the institution of the Synod of Bishops, Francis delivered the address that became the charter of his whole synodal programme, and every student should know its heart. A synodal church, he said, is a church which listens, aware that to listen is more than to hear, and that in this mutual listening the faithful people, the college of bishops, and the Bishop of Rome each have something to learn, each hearing the others, and all hearing the Holy Spirit, the Spirit of truth, in order to know what he says to the churches. Three things in this sentence gather the whole course. Listening is made the first act of the church, which is the discipline of shama from Class 3 given magisterial form. The three subjects, people, bishops, and Bishop of Rome, are named in that order, the whole People of God first, exactly as in the chapters of Lumen Gentium. And all alike listen to the Spirit, so that the church is not seeking her own consensus but discerning the will of God, the pattern of Acts 15 from Class 5. The address did not invent a new church; it named the one the whole tradition had been recovering.

The Inverted Pyramid

In the same address Francis gave the church an image that has become the signature of his ecclesiology, and it repays slow teaching. The church, he said, is not to be pictured as a pyramid with the pope at the top, the bishops below, and the people at the base receiving from above. It is an inverted pyramid, in which the summit lies below the base: those who exercise authority are called to serve beneath the holy People of God, for in the church the one who governs is the one who descends to serve, and the only authority is the authority of the towel and the washing of feet. The image is not a denial of hierarchy but a definition of it. The pope and the bishops remain, and their authority is real, but its whole direction is reversed from domination to service, so that the higher the office, the lower the place, and the greater the authority, the deeper the descent. This is the towel of John 13 from Class 4 raised into a principle of church order. For the seminarian preparing for ordination it is the most searching image in the course, because it decides in advance what kind of priest he will be: one who stands at the top of a pyramid and receives, or one who kneels at the inverted summit and serves.

Example. Consider two headmasters of the same school. The first sits in an office at the top of the building, is seen rarely, and lets every request climb up to him through layers of staff. The second is found each morning at the gate, knows the children by name, carries the bag of the smallest, and is last to leave. Both hold the same authority on paper. But the school of the second is a different place, because authority there flows downward as service rather than upward as tribute. Francis asked the whole church to become the second school. The inverted pyramid is simply that picture drawn for the People of God: the higher the office, the lower the feet must kneel.

This vision did not remain words. Francis embedded it in the reformed Synod of 2018, studied in Class 12, and then submitted the whole church to it in the process of 2021 to 2024, which Class 14 will treat. His pontificate closed with his death on 21 April 2025, and the church, in conclave, elected Robert Francis Prevost, who took the name Leo the Fourteenth on 8 May 2025. The successor of Francis has continued the synodal vision rather than reversing it, carrying forward the implementation phase and confirming that synodality is now the settled path of the church and not the programme of a single pope. The students should grasp the significance of this continuity. A reform that outlives the reformer, received and carried on by his successor, has passed from the will of one man into the life of the church.

Indian Context. The vision of a church that goes out to the margins reads with peculiar force in India, where the margins are vast and near at hand. When Francis speaks of a church bruised from the streets, the Indian priest should see the actual streets of his own town, the migrant sleeping at the station, the Dalit family at the edge of the village, the farmer crushed by debt, the domestic worker with no rest. The option of Francis for the poor is not a foreign import here but the recovery of a demand that runs from the prophets of Class 3 through the table fellowship of Jesus, and it meets in India a church that has too often preferred the security of the compound to the dust of the road. His inverted pyramid judges an Indian clericalism in which the priest is treated as a sacred person seated above the laity, and it judges a caste order that seats persons by birth. To receive the synodal vision of Francis in India is to accept that the seminarian now being formed is being formed for the inverted summit, to kneel and serve beneath the very people whom caste and clericalism have taught him to expect to be served by.

  2. The inverted pyramid places those who preside beneath the People of God as servants. How does this image judge the way authority is actually exercised by priests and bishops you have known, and how does it judge the way caste seats people in your parish?

CLASS 14: THE GLOBAL SYNODAL PROCESS AND ITS METHOD

Module 8: Pope Francis and the Synodal Church

Aim of the Class

This class studies the largest consultation in the history of the church, the synodal process of 2021 to 2024, and the method that carried it. Students should leave able to describe the three stages of the process, to explain the method called conversation in the Spirit and to connect it to the biblical discernment of Module 2, to state what the Final Document of October 2024 taught and the unusual authority Francis gave it, and to describe the implementation phase now running towards the Ecclesial Assembly of 2028.

The Three Stages of the Process

In October 2021 Francis opened a synodal process on the theme that gives this course its thread, For a Synodal Church: Communion, Participation, Mission, and he did something no synod had done before: he began not in Rome but in every parish and diocese of the world. The first stage was the diocesan phase, running through 2021 and 2022, in which local churches across the globe were asked to gather and to listen, and in which tens of millions of the faithful took part. The syntheses of the dioceses rose to the level of the episcopal conferences, and the conferences to the level of the continents. The second stage was the continental phase, across 2022 and 2023, in which seven continental assemblies each produced a document from the listening of their region; the Asian continental assembly met at Bangkok in February 2023, and the Church in India shared in it. The third stage was the celebratory phase in Rome, held in two sessions rather than one, in October 2023 and October 2024, so that the bishops and the other members could carry the fruit of the first session back to the churches for a further year of listening before returning to conclude. The shape of the whole was the shape of Episcopalis Communio made visible: consultation of the whole People of God, then discernment by an assembly, all of it beginning and ending in the local churches.

Conversation in the Spirit

The method that carried the process is the practical heart of this course, and the teacher should have the students learn it as a skill and not only as an idea. It is called conversation in the Spirit, and it unfolds in three movements around a small group that has first prayed and reflected on a question. In the first movement each person speaks in turn, from prayer and lived experience, while the others listen without interrupting and without responding, so that every voice is heard before any is answered. In the second movement each person speaks again, this time of what struck them in the words of the others, naming the resonances and the places where the Spirit seemed to move, so that attention shifts from defending my own view to receiving the view of my neighbour. In the third movement the group seeks together what the Spirit is asking of them, listening for the convergences that have grown and honouring the points that remain open. The method is deliberately not a debate and deliberately not a vote. It is the discipline of shama from Class 3 and the pattern of Acts 15 from Class 5, in which the assembly listened to Peter and to Paul and to Barnabas before James gathered the discernment. Its genius is that it disarms the loudest voice and protects the quietest, for the man who would dominate a debate cannot dominate a silence in which he must first listen, and the timid woman whose voice is never heard in argument is given her turn by the rule itself.

Example. Set the method against an ordinary parish meeting. In the usual meeting three confident people speak at once, the loudest carries the room, the timid say nothing and go home unheard, and the decision belongs to whoever pressed hardest. Now run the same meeting by conversation in the Spirit. Each person, in turn, speaks once while the rest keep silence, so the quiet widow who cleans the church is heard as fully as the wealthy trustee. Then each says what moved him in another, so the trustee must repeat back what the widow said before he adds his own. Only then does the group seek the common mind. The same people, the same question, and a wholly different result, because the method itself redistributes the power of speech. That redistribution is why conversation in the Spirit matters so much in India, where the power of speech is so unequally held.

The Final Document of October 2024

The second Roman session closed on 26 October 2024 with a Final Document titled, once again, For a Synodal Church: Communion, Participation, Mission, which gathered the fruit of three years of listening into a settled teaching on the synodal church, treating the equal dignity of all the baptised, the participation of all in mission, the renewal of the participatory bodies, transparency and accountability in the exercise of authority, and the formation of the whole People of God for a synodal way of life. Francis then took a step that the students must understand, because it is without recent parallel and it depended on the provision of Episcopalis Communio from Class 12. He chose not to write a separate apostolic exhortation after the assembly, as popes had always done. Instead, in a Note of Accompaniment of 24 November 2024, he declared that the Final Document participates in his ordinary magisterium and asked the local churches to receive and implement it. The meaning is large. For the first time the document produced by the whole synodal process, and not a text the pope wrote afterwards on his own, was given magisterial standing. The method had been trusted with the teaching. A church that had spent three years listening did not then hand the conclusion to a single pen; it received its own discernment, confirmed by the one who presides, as a word to be obeyed.

The Implementation Phase

The process was not finished with the document, for a synod that ended in a text would have betrayed its own teaching that synodality begins and ends in the local churches. In March 2025 the General Secretariat of the Synod opened the implementation phase, a period of three years running to an Ecclesial Assembly in October 2028, in which the dioceses, the episcopal conferences, and the religious communities of the world are to receive the Final Document and translate it into concrete practice, accompanied by synodal teams of clergy, religious, and lay faithful. The death of Pope Francis in April 2025 and the election of Pope Leo the Fourteenth in May did not halt this phase; the new pope approved the guiding document of the implementation, the Pathways, and the process continued under his authority, which is itself a sign that synodality has become the settled path of the church. The students should carry away the shape of the whole: a church that consulted her whole people, discerned in assembly, received the discernment as teaching, and then sent it back to the local churches to become life. That is the pattern of Acts 15 written on a global scale, and it is not yet complete, for the implementation is happening now, in this seminary and in the dioceses these students will serve.

Indian Context. India took part in this global process on a scale worth stating to the class. The consultation reached across the dioceses of the three churches of India, the Latin, the Syro-Malabar, and the Syro-Malankara, and the reports record that groups long left at the edge felt themselves included: the laity, women, youth, migrants, and people of other faiths, along with many who had felt themselves distant from the church. The Church in India carried into the process the very realities that this course has named throughout, the wounds of caste, the vast movement of migrant labour, the poverty of the farmer, and the daily fact of living the Gospel among many religions. Conversation in the Spirit is a gift precisely suited to Indian conditions, for in a church marked by caste, wealth, and clerical distance, a method that gives the cleaner of the church the same turn to speak as the trustee, and that requires the powerful to repeat back the words of the powerless, is quietly revolutionary. The test now, in the implementation phase, is whether the parishes and dioceses of India will practise this method where it costs something, at the level of the parish council and the diocesan assembly, or whether they will admire it in Rome and abandon it at home.

Questions for Discussion

  1. Conversation in the Spirit gives every person one turn to speak in silence and requires each to repeat back what struck him in another. Why is this method particularly disarming in an Indian parish marked by caste, wealth, and clerical distance, and where would it meet the most resistance?
  2. Francis gave the Final Document of the synod a share in his ordinary magisterium rather than writing a separate exhortation, trusting the discernment of the whole process. What does this decision teach about the standing of the sensus fidei of the whole People of God?
  3. The implementation phase is happening now, in your own diocese, and asks parishes to translate synodality into practice. Name one concrete change in your home parish that a serious implementation of this process would require, and one reason it might be resisted.

CLASS 15: SYNODALITY RECEIVED IN INDIA, AND THE COURSE DRAWN TOGETHER

Module 8: Pope Francis and the Synodal Church

Aim of the Class

The final class does two things. It receives synodality into the concrete life of the Church in India, through the Asian vision of the FABC, the synthesis of the Indian bishops, and the living structures of small Christian communities and parish councils. And it draws the whole course together, tracing the single thread of communion, participation, and mission from Sinai to the present hour. Students should leave able to describe the Indian and Asian reception of synodality, to name honestly the obstacles that remain, and to give an account of the whole course as one continuous recovery.

The Asian Face: The Bangkok Document

The Church in Asia had prepared her own reception of synodality even before the global process concluded. In October 2022, the Federation of Asian Bishops Conferences held its fiftieth anniversary general conference at Bangkok and produced the document known as the Bangkok Document, under the title Journeying Together as Peoples of Asia. The document reads the whole of Asian church life through the account of the Magi in the second chapter of Matthew, who journeyed, looked to the star, discerned, offered their gifts, and returned by another way, and it arranges itself in five movements from that account: walking together, looking at the emerging realities of Asia, discerning what the Spirit is saying, offering the gifts of Asian culture and spirituality, and opening new pathways. Its first movement takes up the synodal call directly in the three words of this course, communion, participation, and mission, and names the concrete realities the Asian church must face, among them the plight of migrants, refugees, and indigenous peoples. Its new pathways include a turn from a church that dominates to one that inculturates, from basic Christian communities to communities that build up the whole human neighbourhood, and, in a phrase that gathers the whole, from dialogue to synodality. The Asian church thus offered synodality her own face before Rome concluded, and the process has continued, with the Asian bishops preparing a plenary assembly for 2026 to carry it further.

The Indian Reception

Within this Asian setting the Church in India made her own synthesis. The Latin bishops of India, gathered in their conference, produced a synthesis of the first Roman session under the title A Synodal Church in Mission, and the wider work of the Indian church has since taken shape in a national pastoral plan that carries synodality towards the year 2033, drawing together the reports of the many dioceses of India into a set of pastoral priorities for a synodal and missionary church. The Indian reception has two features the students should hold. It is genuinely of the whole church of India, gathering the three churches, the Latin, the Syro-Malabar, and the Syro-Malankara, into one synodal effort. And it is unflinching about the wounds, for the Indian synthesis names openly that discrimination by caste, by language, by ethnicity, by economic and social standing continues to fragment the community of the baptised, which is a rare and healthy honesty, a church willing to confess in its own official documents the divisions it has not yet healed. Recent gatherings of the Indian bishops have continued to press this synodal commitment, binding it to the witness of the church in public life and to the empowerment of the laity, the youth, and women.

The Living Structures: Small Christian Communities and Parish Councils

Beneath the documents lie the living structures in which synodality is either practised or betrayed, and here the Church in India has a genuine gift. The small Christian communities, the neighbourhood gatherings built lane by lane across the dioceses of India, are among the most authentic embodiments of synodality anywhere in the Catholic world, for in them the baptised of a locality gather to read the word, to share their needs, to discern together, and to act for justice in their own place. Where they are alive, they are the parish become synodal at the level of the street, the koinonia of Acts from Class 4 made flesh in an Indian lane. The parish pastoral council and the diocesan pastoral council, studied in Class 11, are the next circles outward, and the whole question of Indian synodality can be put very simply: whether these bodies will truly discern together in the method of conversation in the Spirit, or whether they will remain, as too often now, gatherings that approve the accounts and the feast while the real decisions are taken elsewhere. The instruments exist. The teaching is settled. What remains is the will to use them where they cost something.

The Whole Course in One Thread

Now walk the whole course once more, quickly, so that the students see it as one thing. At Sinai a people was constituted by a call heard together and a covenant answered together, and Israel learned to be the assembly, the qahal, that God convokes. In the prophets and the schools of discernment that people learned to listen, for shama means that to hear is to begin to obey, and God hears the cry of the poor first. Jesus formed not a school but a community, washed its feet, and prayed that it be one, and the church of Jerusalem held all things in common. At the council of Jerusalem the apostolic church met its first dividing question and answered it by referring, debating, listening, discerning, and sending, and said, it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us. The early church made this the ordinary shape of its life, in the bishop within his council, in the principle of nothing without counsel and nothing without consent, in the provincial synods and the councils of the whole world. Then the long centuries let the synodal half wither while authority gathered at the centre, until the head was defined without the body. The reformers kept the memory alive, the Council recovered the whole People of God, the sensus fidei, and collegiality, the reformed Synod gave collegiality an instrument, Francis set the whole recovery in motion, and the global process of our own day submitted the whole church to it and sent it back to the local churches to become life. One thread runs through all of it, unbroken: communion, the people gathered into the life of God; participation, the whole body sharing by Baptism in the discernment and mission of the church; and mission, the sending that is the reason for both. The course has been the single demonstration that synodality is not the novelty of one pope but the recovered shape of the whole tradition, from the mountain to the upper room to the council to the parish lane.

Indian Context. Leave the class with India before their eyes, for this is where these students will serve. The synodal church in India will be real when the gram sabha of the village and the pastoral council of the parish are equally places where the baptised are heard; when the migrant at the station, the Dalit at the edge of the village, the farmer in debt, and the woman at the back of the church are given the same turn to speak that conversation in the Spirit grants them; when the sanctuary is no longer divided by caste and the burial ground no longer separated by birth; and when the priest formed in this seminary takes his place not at the top of a pyramid but at its inverted summit, kneeling to serve beneath the very people whom caste and clericalism taught him to expect to be served by. The whole course has been walking towards this parish, in this land, at this hour. The theology of synodality is finally not a subject to be examined but a church to be built, and the building of it, in Bengal and across India, now passes to those who have studied it here.
