Modern Theologians Since 1918 — Map of Content

The whole field of Christian theology from Barth's Romans (1919) to the present, built from the Ford–Muers The Modern Theologians (42 chapters, 8 parts, epilogue, glossary). This unit is the post-1918 continuation of 10 - Major Theologians and Their Methods, which runs Clement → Packer. Where that note asks who ran which method, this unit asks what happened to theology after Christendom, the trenches, Auschwitz, and empire.

The one-sentence core

Modern theology is not theology that abandons tradition but theology done by people whose societies were remade by the Renaissance, Enlightenment, industry and the sciences — so it must take account of that context in content and method, whether to adapt to it, criticise it, resist it, or try to reverse it (Ford, Introduction).

Ford's two criteria for inclusion

A theologian earns a chapter if they (1) wrote constructively across a broad range of theological issues, and (2) are widely studied now in universities and seminaries. "Quality and significance" decided the borderline cases.

01 — What Makes Theology Modern: Ford's Map

A. The minimal definition

Theology is "modern" not because it is liberal, sceptical or new, but in a minimal sociological sense: theologians work inside societies dramatically reshaped since the Middle Ages — Renaissance, Enlightenment, Industrial Revolution, the rise of the natural and human sciences.

Ford's definition

Modern theology does not attempt simply to repeat past theology; it takes account of the changed modern context in its content and method — whether in order to adapt to, criticise, resist, or try to reverse the developments of modernity.

That last clause is the key to the whole book. A fiercely anti-modern theologian is still modern theology, because the anti-modernity is itself a considered response to the modern situation. What is not modern theology is theology that has not noticed anything happened.

B. The forces that made theology since 1918

The book takes 1918 as its watershed: the year the liberal Protestant synthesis of faith and European culture died in the trenches, and the year before Barth's Romans.

Exam framing

Almost every movement in this unit can be read as an answer to one item in that left column. Barth ← the war and his teachers' war policy. Bonhoeffer ← Nazism. Post-Holocaust theology ← the camps. Liberation theology ← postcolonial poverty. Feminist theology ← the struggle against sexism. Ecotheology ← the ecological crisis. Postcolonial criticism ← decolonization. Name the crisis and you have half the answer.

C. Ford's five types

A spectrum measuring how Christian identity relates to modernity — not a ladder of orthodoxy.

Type Position Representatives
1 Repeats a traditional theology or version of Christianity and sees all reality in its own terms, with no recognition of other perspectives or of recent centuries' developments Strict traditionalisms, fundamentalisms
2 Gives priority to the self-description of the Christian community — Christian identity is primary and reality is construed in relation to it — yet insists Christianity must be continually rethought and seriously engaged with the modern world Barth, Bonhoeffer, de Lubac, Balthasar
3 Theology of correlation at the centre of the spectrum: traditional faith in open dialogue with modernity, correlating the two without subsuming either into the other Tillich
4 Integrates Christianity and modernity by consistently reinterpreting Christianity in terms of a particular modern philosophy, conceptuality or pressing issue Bultmann (existentialism), Pannenberg (universal history/rationality)
5 Gives complete priority to a modern secular philosophy or worldview, assessing Christianity entirely by external criteria and accepting it only so far as it fits Radical liberal postmoderns, Cupitt-style non-realism

How not to abuse the typology

  • It measures a relationship, not quality or truth. A Type 2 theologian is not thereby more orthodox than a Type 3.
  • Most major figures shift type by topic: Rahner is Type 4 in fundamental theology and much closer to Type 2 in his ecclesiology and spirituality.
  • Types 1 and 5 are the limit cases — deliberately, few chapter-length figures sit there. The interesting arguments happen between 2, 3 and 4.

D. Who gets in — and what the book leaves out

Ford's two criteria: (1) wrote constructively across a broad range of theological issues; (2) is widely studied now in universities, seminaries and other third-level institutions. Borderline cases were settled by the frankly contested criterion of "quality and significance."

What this selection costs

The criteria are institutional. They favour figures with published systematic corpora in European languages and university readerships. That is why the book has a chapter on Pannenberg and a paragraph on Dalit theology; why oral, sung and danced African theology (the AICs) appears mostly as something described rather than an author quoted; why Indian theology is one chapter for a subcontinent. Maluleke's protest inside the book — that African theology is "danced, sung and lived rather than written" — is a critique of the very criteria that selected him.

E. The three publics, and the danger

Theology is largely produced in universities and seminaries, and so risks becoming a specialized, marginal, fragmented professional discipline. Ford's answer: theology must stay an integrated, cross-disciplinary endeavour speaking to its three publics —

  1. the academy — arguing at the level of the other disciplines;
  2. the churches — serving actual worshipping communities;
  3. society — providing high-quality public discourse in an era when religion cannot be privatized.

The permanent tension is identity vs relevance: keeping Christianity distinctively itself (Type 2 pull) while keeping it intelligible and answerable to modern thought (Type 4 pull). Every figure in this unit is a particular settlement of that tension.

02 — Classics of the Twentieth Century

The six the book calls classics: three Protestant, three Catholic; three German-speaking Swiss/German Reformed and Lutheran, three French-German Catholic. Between them they set the agenda every later chapter argues with. All six sit at Ford's Type 2 or 3.

1. Karl Barth (1886–1968) — chapter by Daniel W. Hardy

Context. Swiss Reformed; studied under the leading liberals including Harnack. The crisis: parish ministry, the outbreak of the First World War, and his own teachers' public support for German war policy. He rejected the liberal fusion of Christian faith with modern culture. Taught at Göttingen, Münster, Bonn, Basel; drafted the Barmen Declaration for the Confessing Church against Nazi assimilation; dismissed and exiled by Hitler.

Key works — The Epistle to the Romans (1919; radically rewritten 1922) · Christian Dogmatics (1927) · Fides Quaerens Intellectum (Anselm) · the unfinished Church Dogmatics.

Central concepts - The theological object — not "religion" or human experience but the dynamic relation of the divine and the human, which grounds all inquiry. - Infinite qualitative distinction — Kierkegaard's phrase: the unbridgeable gap between time and eternity, Creator and creature, that only God's grace crosses. - Actualism — God is not a fixed "state" of being but sovereign in freedom and love, actively actualizing his relation to humanity. - Operative conditions — the divine actualizations that make human realities (preaching, the Bible) become the Word of God. Hence the threefold Word: revealed, written, preached.

Method. Early: the dialectical method, holding grace/judgment and Creator/creature in tension. Later: dogmatics as an ecclesiastical science serving the Church — rigorously Christocentric, with the incarnate Mediator at the centre of God's veiling and unveiling, proceeding by the "rational acknowledgment" (intelligere) of faith.

Significance. He restored Christian theology to strength by overthrowing the nineteenth-century correlation of human experience with God, and pioneered a total integration of scripture, historical theology, dogmatics and ethics re-centred on God's objective, sovereign revelation.

Criticism. The focus on actualism and discrete moments of divine decision can bypass the historical complexity and immanent detail of ordinary human life; his trinitarian theology leans on a "vertical" logic that under-appreciates God's full historical participation.

Today. Barth is "the point of departure for others" — his formulations on revelation, Christology and Trinity remain the baseline against which contemporary dogmatics defines itself. See 04 - Reappropriating Traditions for the post-Barthians.

2. Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945) — chapter by Wayne Whitson Floyd

Context. Aristocratic Berlin family; Tübingen and Berlin. Three encounters broadened him: Barth's theology, the black church in Harlem, and Roman Catholicism. Confessing Church leader, ran the underground Finkenwalde seminary, joined the conspiracy, hanged at Flossenbürg for his part in the July 1944 plot against Hitler.

Key works — Sanctorum Communio · Act and Being · Creation and Fall · Christ the Center · Life Together · Discipleship · Ethics · Letters and Papers from Prison.

Central concepts - Stellvertretung — vicarious representative action: the heart of responsible life, where Christians confess and take on guilt for the world in participation with Christ. - Analogia relationis — the imago Dei is found in the relationality between human beings, mirroring the inner-trinitarian relations. - Cheap grace vs costly discipleship — cheap grace is grace without the cross and without Jesus Christ; discipleship costs power and privilege. - Cor curvum in se — the sinful heart curved in on itself, unable to meet God or the neighbour. - From prison: religionless Christianity, the world come of age, living etsi deus non daretur — as if God were not given — before the God who is pushed out of the world onto the cross.

Method. He refused rigid systematics for dialogical, "both/and" thinking, integrating pastoral spirituality with academic rigour and treating Christian truth as ongoing formation in community.

Significance. He re-imagined faith and the secular: redemption is found in the centre of earthly life, not beyond it. He grounded ethics in relationality and the boundary of the Other.

Criticism. The fragmentary prison writings invited "creative misuses" by death-of-God and secular theologians who took phrases out of context. He also carried blind spots on Judaism — holding that Jews must eventually accept Jesus.

Today. His work is a "Rorschach test for modern theology", claimed by liberals, conservatives and liberationists alike. Read him for community, creation and costliness.

3. Paul Tillich (1886–1965) — chapter by David H. Kelsey

Context. Conservative Lutheran home; the First World War turned him from monarchist to cultural pessimist and religious socialist. Suspended by Hitler from Frankfurt in 1933, he fled to the USA — Union, Harvard, Chicago — and became an intellectual superstar in America.

Key works — Systematic Theology (3 vols) · The Shaking of the Foundations · The Courage to Be · Theology of Culture.

Central concepts - Ultimate concern — his definition of religion: whatever concerns you ultimately is your god. - Ground of being / power of being — God is not a supreme being (which would be finite) but being-itself, the unconditioned source resisting the threat of non-being. - The demonic — a finite reality (a system, a nation, a culture) elevated to ultimate concern, and so made destructive. - New Being — the healing power mediated through Jesus as the Christ, overcoming existential estrangement.

Method. The method of correlation: fundamental human questions raised by the culture (finitude, estrangement, meaninglessness) correlated with answers carried by Christian symbols (God as Creator, Jesus as the Christ). Ford's Type 3, the centre of the spectrum.

Significance. He made Christianity intelligible to a sceptical, secular culture and legitimated the "theology of culture," making apologetics integral to systematics rather than a preliminary.

Criticism. The non-theist doctrine of God laid him open to the charge of atheism; his Christology is said to make the historical facticity of Jesus irrelevant; correlation is accused of reducing faith to secular cultural conviction.

Today. He shaped how "religion" itself is understood through ultimate concern — even in US law — and continues to drive theological aesthetics, literature, interfaith dialogue and pastoral psychology.

4. Henri de Lubac (1896–1991) — chapter by John Milbank

Context. French Jesuit; formed by the First World War and by resistance to the Nazi occupation. Central figure of the nouvelle théologie against sterile neoscholasticism; silenced under Humani Generis (1950); returned as a decisive contributor to Vatican II.

Key works — Catholicisme (1938) · Surnaturel (1946) · Corpus mysticum · Exégèse médiévale · Pic de la Mirandole.

Central concepts - Surnaturel — the divine intruding into the cosmos: deification by grace as human nature's true destiny. - Ressourcement — recovery of the tradition's riches, especially before 1300, to renew speculative theology. - Natura pura — the neoscholastic notion that human nature has its own autonomous end apart from grace. De Lubac fought it as the very root of secularism: invent a self-sufficient nature and you have invented a world that does not need God.

Method. Chiefly historical theology — a "non-ontology" between pure philosophy and dogmatics — championing spiritual, fourfold (allegorical and typological) exegesis over purely historical-critical method.

Significance. He overthrew the nature/grace dualism, showing human nature to be intrinsically ordered to the supernatural, and so catalysed the Catholic cultural revival and Vatican II.

Criticism. His post-1950 attempts to placate Humani Generis by conceding a formal "pure nature" left him in severe theoretical incoherence; direct dogmatics and metaphysics are relatively absent — the mark of a thinker constrained by ecclesiastical caution; he never tackled patriarchy or male hierarchical rule.

Today. Milbank's claim: Catholic theology can avoid the twin traps of secular liberalism and closed conservatism only by recovering the natural desire for the supernatural.

5. Karl Rahner (1904–1984) — chapter by Karen Kilby

Context. German Jesuit; studied philosophy under Heidegger; his doctoral thesis was failed by a traditionalist supervisor and later accepted by the Innsbruck theology faculty. Suspect before the Council, peritus at Vatican II, then the leading voice of the new Catholic mainstream.

Key works — Spirit in the World · Hearer of the Word · Foundations of Christian Faith · Theological Investigations (23 vols).

Central concepts - Vorgriff auf esse — in every act of knowing or willing a finite thing there is an implicit reaching toward infinite being, and so toward God. - Supernatural existential — grace is not an episodic addition but a universal, ever-present gift shaping the foundation of all human existence, including unbelievers'. - Anonymous Christians — those who are not professed Christians yet implicitly accept the grace of Christ offered in the depths of their experience.

Method. The transcendental method (Kant, Maréchal, Heidegger): what must be true of human beings a priori for them to receive revelation? He rarely wrote systematic tomes, preferring short, probing, pastorally oriented essays (Quaestiones Disputatae).

Significance. He integrated robust Catholic orthodoxy with modern philosophy and honest engagement with the world; legitimated the anthropological turn; shaped Vatican II.

Criticism. The transcendental method risks reductionism — forcing the particular, historical rootedness of Christianity into the procrustean bed of an a priori anthropology. "Anonymous Christian" is attacked from both sides: pluralists call it patronizing and imperialistic; Balthasar and conservatives say it makes explicit faith and Church membership unnecessary.

Today. Ecclesiastical fashion has swung toward Balthasar, but Rahner remains the model of theology that is "simultaneously faithful and creative." Deep dive already in this vault: 10a - Karl Rahner - A Deep Dive.

6. Hans Urs von Balthasar (1905–1988) — chapter by Ben Quash

Context. Swiss Catholic; formed by Benedictines and Jesuits; doctorate in German literature and philosophy. The decisive influence was his collaboration with the mystic Adrienne von Speyr, with whom he founded a secular institute in Basel — which cost him the Jesuits. Exiled from mainstream academia, rehabilitated after Vatican II, named to the Papal Theological Commission, died days before receiving the cardinalate.

Key works — Apokalypse der deutschen Seele · the trilogy The Glory of the Lord → Theo-Drama → *Theo-Logic · Mysterium Paschale*.

Central concepts - Theological aesthetics — perception of the self-disclosing, enrapturing form of God's revelation (beauty, glory) comes before critical analysis. - Gestalt Christi — the form of Christ, in which every other worldly form finds its centre, ground and judgment. - Theo-drama — Christian truth as dramatic action, the intersection of divine and human freedom, structured by mission (Sendung), not as a static system. - Maior dissimilitudo — any similarity between creaturely form and divine revelation is suspended in an ever-greater dissimilarity.

Method. Contemplative and anti-reductionist: priority to the object of faith (God in Christ) over the subject's conditions of knowing — the exact inversion of Rahner. He draws on the non-verbal arts, literature, the Fathers, and the lived theology of the saints as typological models.

Significance. He brought beauty back into theological discourse and built a towering integration of theology, aesthetics and drama, defending Christian particularity while resetting all worldly thought inside it.

Criticism. The dramatic aesthetic can become an overarching "supra-drama" that too readily intuits a Hegelian harmony, side-stepping real contradiction, darkness and the consequences of history.

Today. He offers a comprehensive vision of the mystery that accommodates the tensions of human life in their variety; his theology of the cross and Holy Saturday continues to resource theologies worldwide.

The single most useful contrast in Part I

Rahner vs Balthasar. Same Church, same Council, opposite starting points: Rahner asks what must the hearer be for revelation to land; Balthasar says look at the form and let it seize you first. 03 — Theological Responses to Modernity: Germany, Britain, USA

Part I gave the classics. Part II asks what the next generation did with them — in three national idioms with three different temperaments: German system-building, British essayistic wisdom, American public ethics.

A. Germany

Wolfhart Pannenberg (1928–2014)

Works — Revelation as History (1961) · Jesus – God and Man (1964) · Theology and the Philosophy of Science (1973) · Anthropology in Theological Perspective (1983) · Metaphysics and the Idea of God (1988) · Systematic Theology (3 vols, 1988–93).

Concepts - Revelation as history — God reveals himself indirectly through his acts in history, and is fully knowable only at the end of history. - Proleptic actualization — the resurrection of Jesus is the anticipation, in advance, of that end. - Cognitio Dei naturalis insita — an innate knowledge of God grounded in human "excentric openness" (we are constitutively directed beyond ourselves). - Divine attributes on the analogy of a universal field of force rather than substance.

Method. Christology "from below": start from the historical reality of Jesus, not from assumed divinity. He works first from the external perspective of reason and anthropology to test truth-claims, then from the internal perspective of faith to reconstruct doctrine. Ford's Type 4.

Significance. He met the modern atheist critique of religion head-on without immunizing theology from it, and produced one of the most rigorous systematics of the late twentieth century.

Criticism. Over-reliance on Hegelian metaphysics of history; a twofold indeterminacy — if what a thing is is settled only in its future, every present interpretation is irreducibly hypothetical; his use of philosophy as praeambula fidei risks letting secular reason set theology's categories.

Today. Ecumenical dialogue, theology of nature and the sciences, interdisciplinary work with philosophy and the human sciences.

Jürgen Moltmann (1926–2024)

Works — the trilogy Theology of Hope (1964), The Crucified God (1972), The Church in the Power of the Spirit (1975); then the "contributions": The Trinity and the Kingdom, God in Creation, The Way of Jesus Christ, The Coming of God.

Concepts - Dialectic of cross and resurrection — continuity in radical discontinuity. - Divine passibility — God's capacity to suffer, in solidarity with the world. - Social trinitarianism — God as three subjects in mutual, loving relationship (against a monarchical monotheism that legitimates earthly domination). - Trinitarian panentheism — the world existing within God's trinitarian experience.

Method. Dialectical, integrating praxis and doxology; eschatologically driven; a genuinely public theology in dialogue with Marxism, critical theory and Jewish thought (Bloch's Principle of Hope).

Significance. He rehabilitated future eschatology from the margins of modern theology and built an eschatological theodicy in which God suffers in solidarity with the oppressed.

Criticism. Early one-sided emphasis on the future at the expense of present experience; social trinitarianism accused of bordering on tritheism; panentheism suspected of nearing pantheism; Anglo-Saxon critics find the argument short on logical rigour.

Today. He is the connective tissue between European systematics and liberation, political and ecological theology — human rights, suffering, and the transformation of the earth. Note how much of Part V leans on him.

B. Britain

T. F. Torrance (1913–2007)

Works — Theological Science (1969) · Space, Time and Incarnation (1969) · Divine and Contingent Order (1981) · The Trinitarian Faith (1988) · The Christian Doctrine of God (1996).

Concepts — the homoousion as the hinge of everything (the internal relations of the Trinity correspond exactly to God's external acts); realism (thought corresponds to reality when the knower conforms to the mode of rationality the object affords); the "new objectivity" taken from Einsteinian physics.

Method. Scientific theology, strictly a posteriori, grounded in the givenness of God's self-revelation: epistemology follows ontology — the nature of the reality investigated dictates how it must be known.

Significance. A powerful realist alternative to accommodationist liberalism, bridging natural science and dogmatics by showing their shared epistemological structure.

Criticism. Relies heavily on physics while ignoring the biological and social sciences; terse, dense prose; an actualist ontology with exclusivist and occasionalist tendencies.

Today. He animates the theology-and-science dialogue and the revival of trinitarian dogmatics (06 - Theology and the Sciences).

Anglican theology: the wisdom tradition

Figures and works — Michael Ramsey, The Gospel and the Catholic Church · Austin Farrer, Finite and Infinite, The Freedom of the Will · Donald MacKinnon, Kenosis and Establishment · Stephen Sykes, The Integrity of Anglicanism · Daniel Hardy, Finding the Church · Rowan Williams, On Christian Theology, Lost Icons.

Concepts — the search for "wisdom theology" integrating prayer, spirituality and culture; sociality (humanity's fundamental interrelatedness); the parabolic and dramatic character of truth; kenosis.

Method. They resist German-style systematization, preferring the essay, philosophical interrogation, and textual reasoning rooted in historical, patristic and liturgical traditions.

Significance. They held a rigorous theology of transcendence against mid-century logical positivism and then against 1960s sweeping liberalism, forging a distinctly Anglican intellectual seriousness.

Criticism. Sykes charged Ramsey's celebrated Anglican "comprehensiveness" with amounting to theological incoherence and a failure of self-definition. Williams criticized the older Lux Mundi "incarnational consensus" for sacralizing the present social order and abstracting from the contingent conflicts of Jesus's cross.

Today. Central to ecclesiology, social and political theology, and post-secular argument in an age of fragmented civic identity.

C. USA

H. Richard Niebuhr (1894–1962)

Works — The Social Sources of Denominationalism (1929) · The Kingdom of God in America (1937) · The Meaning of Revelation (1941) · Radical Monotheism and Western Culture · Christ and Culture (1951) · The Responsible Self (1963).

Concepts — radical monotheism (loyalty to the One beyond all the many centres of value); fate (the history we cannot escape); inner vs outer history; the responsible self — the human being primarily as a responder to action upon them, displacing the models of man-the-citizen and man-the-maker.

Method. Troeltsch's sociological-historical sensibility joined to Barth's constructive theology, worked out through a typological method — most famously the five types of Christ and Culture.

Significance. Arguably the most important American Christian ethicist of his era; he laid the groundwork for the recovery of the virtues and narrative theology later carried by the Yale school.

Criticism. Yoder: the typology pretends to a neutral, objective stance while in fact advancing Niebuhr's own agenda, using divine transcendence to relativize and erode orthodox truth-claims.

Reinhold Niebuhr (1892–1971)

Works — Moral Man and Immoral Society (1932) · An Interpretation of Christian Ethics (1935) · The Nature and Destiny of Man (1941–43) · The Children of Light and the Children of Darkness (1944).

Concepts — Christian realism; the paradox of finitude and freedom generating anxiety, which is the precondition (not the cause) of sin; the equality of sin corrupting all human justice; the Kingdom as a transcendent ideal always only fragmentarily realized in history.

Method. Dialectical, apologetic correlation of faith with history and sociology; Marxian and Augustinian insights deployed against both the sentimental idealism of the Social Gospel and secular cynicism.

Significance. Extraordinary influence on American public and political life: how to pursue relative justice without utopian crusades or moral despair.

Criticism. Lehmann: a Christology fixed on Father and Son that excludes the Spirit, making justification a principle rather than an actualized reality. Others: theology reduced to an anthropology that accommodates power struggles and betrays the Church's distinct mission of peace (Hauerwas's line).

Today. The touchstone for Christian realism in international relations, debates on imperialism, democracy and power.

Revisionists and liberals — Farley, Kaufman, Ogden, Tracy

Works — Farley, Ecclesial Man, Ecclesial Reflection, Divine Empathy · Kaufman, Systematic Theology, In Face of Mystery · Ogden, The Reality of God, Faith and Freedom · Tracy, Blessed Rage for Order, The Analogical Imagination, Plurality and Ambiguity.

Concepts — Tracy: the method of correlation and religious "classics"; Ogden: dual criteria of appropriateness (to the apostolic witness) and credibility (to common human experience), and a "revisionary Christology"; Kaufman: humans as "biohistorical" beings, theology as the imaginative construction of the concept of God; Farley: ecclesial universals and "theological portraiture."

Method. Mediation of tradition with modern philosophy, by four different routes — Tracy hermeneutics and Tillichian correlation; Ogden linguistic analysis and process philosophy; Kaufman historicist pragmatism; Farley Husserlian phenomenology.

Criticism. Postliberals (Thiemann, Lindbeck) charge them with surrendering Christian distinctiveness, swapping a divine foundation for human imagination, and not taking the Nietzschean/genealogical critique of modernity seriously enough.

Today. Their models remain the working equipment of interreligious dialogue and of engagement with pluralism in a secular public. Kaufman is already in this vault: Gordon Kaufman.

Process theology — Whitehead, Hartshorne, Cobb, Loomer, Wieman

Concepts — naturalism; the mutual immanence of God and world; reality as actual entities/occasions in process; panentheism; the dipolar God — a primordial nature of potentiality and a consequent nature of actuality; God as "the great companion, the fellow-sufferer who understands."

Method. Whitehead's imaginative generalization: replace static substance metaphysics with an ontology of relational becoming; interpret logic and relatedness ontologically.

Significance. A radical reinterpretation of the doctrine of God, away from classical impassibility toward a God who genuinely suffers and grows with creation.

Criticism. An internal split over how much tradition can be retrieved (Cobb much; Loomer and Wieman little). Hartshorne's rationalism is said to undercut Whitehead's ultimate "Creativity"; theologians warn the system reduces God to the abstract structure of a world-process rather than a free, distinct agent.

Today. Powerful in ecotheology, theology and the biological sciences, and Buddhist–Christian dialogue (Whitehead mapped onto śūnyatā).

04 — Reappropriating Traditions

The contemporary scene's characteristic move: not correlation with modernity but retrieval of tradition — after the critique of foundations has made naive retrieval impossible.

A. Postliberal theology (James Fodor) — the Yale school

A movement for the faithful retrieval of Christian tradition and the ecumenical renewal of the church.

George Lindbeck — The Nature of Doctrine (1984)

Three models of religion and doctrine:

Model Doctrine is… Weakness
Cognitive-propositional Objective truth-claims about reality Cannot explain doctrinal change without contradiction
Experiential-expressive (liberal, post-Schleiermacher) Expression of a prior inner feeling or experience Makes the community's language secondary and interchangeable
Cultural-linguistic (Lindbeck) Communally authoritative rules of discourse, attitude and action Charged with relativism and sectarianism
  • Rule theory of doctrine — "Jesus is Lord" functions as a second-order directive governing Christian speech and life, not primarily a first-order metaphysical report. This is what lets doctrine be both constant and historically changing: the rule holds while its formulations shift.
  • Intratextuality — theology redescribes reality inside the scriptural framework instead of translating scripture into alien extra-scriptural categories. Slogan: "the text absorbs the world."

Hans Frei

  • The eclipse of biblical narrative — modern historical consciousness made theologians fit the biblical stories into external explanatory frameworks (either transcendent truths or historical facts), robbing scripture of its reality-constituting power.
  • Identity before presence — Christians must first read the gospel narratives to learn Jesus's specific identity before asking what his presence means for believers now. Reverse the order and you make Christ a function of religious experience.

Criticisms of postliberalism

  1. The focus on narrative obscures the cognitive and metaphysical work of systematic discourse.
  2. If the text "absorbs" the world, can the Church still be judged and corrected by the cultures it meets — or does it merely assimilate them?
  3. Is the authoritative reading grounded in the texts themselves or in a community's practices? Postliberals are pressed to say.
  4. They need a more robust account of truth than "modest cognitivism."
  5. The standing charge: sectarianism and political quietism. Their reply — the "separated life" is a reflection of God's elective love and an appropriated sense of Israelhood, undertaken for the service of the world.

Why this matters in India

Lindbeck's cultural-linguistic turn is the most powerful available argument that a local church's grammar of faith is not a defective version of a universal essence. It is also the sharpest argument against the inculturationist assumption that the gospel is a translatable kernel inside a cultural husk. Set it against Pieris and the Dalit critique in 09 - Particularizing Theology II - African, South Asian, East Asian, Postcolonial.

B. Systematic theology after Barth (John Webster)

Building on Barth while emerging from his shadow.

Eberhard Jüngel (1934–2021)

Barth plus existential Lutheranism (Bultmann, Fuchs, Ebeling). The revelatory word is an eschatological "speech-event" that interrupts human self-identity. The doctrine of God is derived from the cross: God's being is in becoming, and the "death of God" at Calvary expresses God's sovereign freedom to identify with the crucified Jesus. His anthropology rests on justification — passive reception of grace over human self-realization.

Robert Jenson (1930–2017)

Radicalizes Barth to free theology from residual Greek metaphysics. In The Triune Identity and Systematic Theology: God's identity is located in his dramatic, eschatological movement with creatures toward the future, not in a timeless pretemporal eternity. He extends the Lutheran communicatio idiomatum to ecclesiology — the sacramental church as "the risen Christ's Ego."

Colin Gunton (1941–2003)

Critiques Barth's Augustinian doctrine of the Trinity for subordinating the distinct persons to a monistic unity and producing a restricted pneumatology. He proposes the Trinity as a communion of persons and stresses the mediation of the triune God through creaturely means by the Spirit. The One, the Three and the Many offers a trinitarian metaphysic of culture affirming creaturely particularity against modern homogenization.

The internal argument of post-conciliar Catholicism

Lonergan and Schillebeeckx push method and experience; Küng pushes institutional reform; Wojtyła answers with a christocentric personalism that confronts the culture rather than correlating with it; Lash refuses both triumphalism and accommodation. All five are inside the same council's reception — which is why "the spirit of Vatican II" arguments never resolve. Cross-reference 000 - Vatican II MOC and 05 - Theologians Influence Map.

05 — Texts, Truth and Signification

Three chapters on the same underlying question: after the Enlightenment, what authorises a theological statement? The Bible read how? Philosophy in what role? And what survives when the very idea of stable meaning is put under suspicion?

A. Biblical interpretation (Anthony Thiselton)

  • Historical-critical method (Semler, Michaelis): interpret the Bible free of imposed theological categories, attending to the historical particularity of the texts. Its later radicalization: Räisänen's programme of a wholly non-confessional biblical study.
  • Theology of the Word — Barth and Bultmann's joint revolt against Harnack's liberalism. They then split: Barth on the Bible as witness to God's sovereign grace; Bultmann on demythologization — interpreting mythological texts existentially to expose their challenge to human decision. Ford's Type 4 in its purest form.
  • The British line — the Cambridge triumvirate (Westcott, Lightfoot, Hort) and successors (Dodd, Caird, Barrett, F. F. Bruce, Barr, Stanton, Dunn, N. T. Wright): historical rigour joined to theological commitment.
  • Interdisciplinary hermeneutics — Gadamer (fusion of horizons, effective history) and Paul Ricoeur: the double hermeneutic of suspicion and retrieval, and the mutual implication of time and narrative.
  • Canonical criticism — Brevard Childs: the authoritative object is the final form of the text within the canon and its reception, not the reconstructed sources behind it.
  • Also in this stream: Horace Bushnell and G. E. Wright on language and God's acts in history.

B. Philosophical theology (Ingolf Dalferth)

Emerged with modern science and fractured into three programmes after Kant and Hume demolished the traditional proofs:

Programme What it does Representatives
Dogmatic philosophical theology (philosophical theism) A decontextualized rational account of God, independent of confessional faith Swinburne, Plantinga
Critical philosophical theology Explores the internal grammar of a particular faith and relates it to wider cultural and scientific contexts Kant → Schleiermacher → Tillich
Philosophy of religion Refocuses on religion as a universal or historical phenomenon: how beliefs and practices orient human life Hick, D. Z. Phillips

C. Postmodern theology (Graham Ward)

Postmodernism, in the book's glossary

An intellectual position regarding the present as discontinuous with modernity, sceptical about universal rational principles, objective truth and progress. Deconstruction: a destabilizing analysis exposing the arbitrariness or bias of texts and showing how their logic invites its own refutation. Metanarrative: an overarching narrative claiming to comprehend and explain all others.

Liberal postmoderns — radicalizing liberal theology

  • Mark C. Taylor — deconstructive "a/theology": the endless deferral of truth, linguistic idealism, the erasure of the transcendent signified.
  • Thomas J. J. Altizer — the death of God as an evolutionary historical era in which Christ merges entirely into secular creation.
  • Don Cupitt — French poststructuralism enlisted for a naturalistic, anti-realist philosophy of life; God as a guiding spiritual ideal, not a being.
  • Also: David Ray Griffin, Charles Winquist, Robert Scharlemann.

Conservative postmoderns — postmodern critique used for theology

  • Jean-Luc Marion, God Without Being — critique of the onto-theology of modern metaphysics; a phenomenology of the icon (against the idol): the invisible God crosses into the visible as pure gift of love, revealed supremely in the kenosis of the cross.
  • Michel de Certeau, The Mystic Fable — a "poetics of the body" and the experience of the divine Other; mysticism as a rupture of the logic of the written word, opening new spaces of knowledge driven by spiritual desire.
  • John Milbank / Radical Orthodoxy — Christianity must reject secular social theory and postmodern nihilism alike. Theology must not accommodate secular reason but proclaim its own cultural-linguistic metadiscourse, grounded in an ontology of peace rather than of violence. (His critique of sociology is in 06 - Theology and the Sciences.)

The dividing line

Both wings accept that modernity's foundations have collapsed. The liberals conclude that theology must give up transcendence; the conservatives conclude that theology was never dependent on those foundations in the first place and is now free of the secular referee. Whether that freedom is a recovery or a retreat is the argument of the last thirty years.

06 — Theology and the Sciences

A. Theology and the physical sciences (Philip Clayton)

The story. Modernity downgraded the theology of nature: Kant and Schleiermacher turned theology toward the subject, while the sciences pursued a reductionist programme treating all reality as "matter in motion." Theology retreated to inwardness and history and left nature to the physicists.

The reopening. The collapse of the reductionist programme — relativity, quantum indeterminacy, chaos theory — reopened the door. Nature is no longer a closed deterministic machine, so the theology of nature is thinkable again.

The divine action debate. How does God influence the world without violating natural law? Clayton's spectrum of eight positions, from most to least accommodating of strict science:

# Position Representative
1 Naturalist / physicalist — no special divine action —
2 Deism —
3 A single divine act (creation as one act; no particular interventions) Maurice Wiles
4 Double agency — divine and creaturely causality on different levels Austin Farrer, Whitehead
5 Top-down causality Arthur Peacocke
6 Transhistorical / mystical truths —
7 Natural order respected except for the resurrection —
8 Robust, regular miraculous intervention C. S. Lewis, C. John Collins

Panentheism and the panentheistic analogy. The world exists in God, though God is more than the world. God's action on the world is likened to the mind's top-down influence on the body: God works through natural processes without breaking them.

Significance and method. Physics supplies new models for doctrine — e.g. quantum complementarity as an analogy for the two natures of Christ. Big Bang cosmology and fine-tuning yield no strict deductive proof of God, but can be compellingly incorporated into a post-reductionist theology of nature.

B. Theology and the biological sciences (Celia Deane-Drummond)

Pierre Teilhard de Chardin (1881–1955)

Jesuit palaeontologist; a grand synthesis of evolutionary science and Christian mysticism. - Law of complexity–consciousness — matter carries an innate drive toward spiritualization: as material organization grows more complex, consciousness rises with it. - Evolution crosses critical thresholds — matter → life → thought (the noosphere) → a planetary super-consciousness. - Omega Point — the term and attractor of the whole process, identified with the cosmic Christ: the "Christification of the universe" (Christogenesis).

Arthur Peacocke (1924–2006)

Biochemist and priest. Models theology on scientific empiricism: emergence, top-down divine interaction, panentheism; humanity as "co-creator, co-worker and co-explorer with God."

Evolution and theodicy

The immense suffering built into evolutionary process pushes Peacocke and Moltmann toward a "pathetic, self-emptying God" who shares intimately in the suffering of every creature. Evolution does not merely raise the problem of pain — it makes divine passibility close to obligatory.

Ecological theology

The standing tension is anthropocentrism vs biocentrism. Ecofeminists — Rosemary Radford Ruether, Sallie McFague — link the patriarchal oppression of women to the degradation of nature, sometimes drawing on the Gaia hypothesis for deep ecological interconnectedness. (Ruether at length in 08 - Particularizing Theology I - Feminist, Black, Latin American.)

Method. Reciprocal listening: no empirical imperialism, but real openness to how biology reshapes Christian anthropology — e.g. human kinship with the primates. Practically relevant to biotechnology ethics, environmental policy and social justice.

Indian and pastoral hook

The ecological chapter is where this book is most immediately usable in Indian pastoral work: Laudato si', the theology of water and land, adivasi land struggles, and the tribal theology of creation all plug straight into the anthropocentrism/biocentrism tension and into Ruether's link between domination of women, of the poor, and of the earth.

C. Theology and the social sciences (Richard H. Roberts)

Sociology arose as a "grand theory" positioning itself as the emancipatory successor to theology, treating religion as a premodern residue. Five theological strategies in reply:

Strategy Figure Move Cost
Fundamentalism — Repels modernity and social science; subcultural isolation and literalism Withdrawal from public reason
Sociology overcomes theology Ernst Troeltsch Theological questions absorbed into historicism; the church–sect typology; all historical truth relative Loses normative claim
Theology recruits sociology Dietrich Bonhoeffer Sanctorum Communio uses Gemeinschaft/Gesellschaft to analyse the church; wrestles with secularization — Christians come of age and live etsi deus non daretur, relying on the suffering God of the cross Requires constant theological control of the borrowed tools
Mutual assimilation Edward Farley Phenomenology plus sociology to depict the "social duration" of "ecclesial being"; avoids authoritarianism Risks losing prophetic critique
Postmodern quasi-fundamentalism John Milbank Secular sociology is a Christian heresy, an anti-theology born of an ontology of violence; refuse apologetic mediation; ground ecclesial practice in an ontology of harmonic peace and difference Refuses the mediation that made public theology possible

The upshot. A "somatic" and contextual theology is needed — one that addresses deep socialization and global fragmentation, and that engages the human sciences to stay intelligible in a detraditionalized high modernity.

07 — Theology, Prayer and Practice

The part of the book most directly useful for seminary formation: what happened when theology was separated from prayer, and what happened when pastoral care was separated from theology.

A. Theology and spirituality (Mark A. McIntosh)

The lost unity

For most of Christian history theology (the study of God) and spirituality (the transforming encounter with God) were indissoluble. Evagrius's maxim: "the theologian is the one who prays."

The divorce — two stages

  1. Early modernity / university scholasticism — theology moved from contemplative, biblical encounter to abstract logical propositions. Knowing God became mastering a body of statements about God.
  2. The modern turn to the subject — spirituality was reduced to interior psychological or emotional states, severed from trinitarian and historical realities. Now you had a doctrine without prayer and a prayer without doctrine, each impoverished.

The reunion — championed by the nouvelle théologie

  • Anselm Stolz traced the divorce to the extrinsic separation of nature and grace (the same natura pura de Lubac attacked). His counter-thesis: true mysticism is fundamentally sacramental and Trinitarian, grounded in the historical missions of the Son and the Spirit — not in exceptional private psychological states.
  • Simone Weil (1909–43) — a theology of hidden divine presence amid affliction (malheur). The universe exists through God's loving renunciation; by ascetical apprenticeship the intellect learns to renounce egocentrism and offer compassionate attention, discovering the trinitarian pattern in all creation. Attention, for Weil, is itself a form of prayer.
  • Hans Urs von Balthasar — Ignatian to the core: "the life of the saints is theology in practice." He rejects rationalistic theology divorced from prayer; spiritual calling and theological vision are united in the objective, historical form of Christ and the trinitarian life.

The epistemological point. Reintegration requires accepting that theological truth is not the mastery of facts but transformative participation in the divine life. That is what moves the discipline past the modern impasse between reductionist cultural analysis and essentialist psychology of religion.

B. Pastoral and practical theology (Stephen Pattison and Gordon Lynch)

The discipline's shift. From transmitting pastoral "hints and tips" to a sophisticated hermeneutical discipline. It rejects Schleiermacher's applicationist model (theory formulated elsewhere, then deductively applied to practice) in favour of an experiential-inductive method: a critical three-way conversation between

  1. contemporary lived experience,
  2. theological norms, and
  3. the human sciences.

Interdisciplinary theological reflection aimed at theoretical and practical transformation — the Marxian eleventh thesis inside theology: not only to interpret, but to change. Its standing gift to the academy is to hold theology accountable to everyday suffering and faith; its standing problem is balancing academic rigour, public intelligibility and confessional identity.

Use in seminary formation

These two chapters are the theological warrant for a formation programme that refuses to split the spiritual director from the dogma professor. Stolz's argument (mysticism is sacramental and trinitarian, not a private psychological elite) is the exact answer to the seminarian who treats prayer as a temperament. Browning's four stages are a usable structure for a pastoral case study; Oden's retrieval is the corrective when pastoral counselling has quietly become secular therapy with a Roman collar.

08 — Particularizing Theology I: Feminist, Black, Latin American

What "particularizing" means

The deliberate adoption of standpoints previously marginalized in "mainstream" theology — gender, race, class, colonial history — not as special-interest supplements but as the place from which theology is done. The claim underneath all of Part V: the universal theology of the textbooks was always somebody's particular theology, with the particularity hidden.

A. Feminism, gender and theology (Rachel Muers)

Figures

  • Rosemary Radford Ruether — traces the connections between patriarchy, militarism and environmental degradation. Reconstructs theology from the prophetic and wisdom traditions; explores the tension and complementarity of "Gaia" (divinity immanent in interconnected nature) and "God" (transcendent divine demand). Reads Jesus within the Hebrew prophetic tradition, in solidarity with the poor.
  • Chung Hyun Kyung (Korea) — grounds theology in the lived experience and popular religiosity of Asian women, including frank syncretism. Famously invoked Kwan Yin, the East Asian goddess of compassion, as an image of the Holy Spirit and of a feminine Christ. Aims at purging han (accumulated anger, resentment and despair).
  • Luce Irigaray — philosopher and psychoanalyst, heavily appropriated by theologians: without female images of God, women can only be thought as the negation or unacknowledged ground of male subjectivity ("the other of the same"). Proposes a "sensible transcendental" to revalue female embodiment.

Key terms

Patriarchy · kyriarchy (Schüssler Fiorenza: intersecting systems of domination — gender is never the only axis) · idolatry of the male God · revaluation of the body (against spirit/matter binaries) · ekklesia of wo/men / women-church · spiritual ecofeminism.

Method

Theology as a political and ethical act: a hermeneutic of suspicion turned on the idolatry of exclusively male God-language, a re-reading of scripture and tradition to recover suppressed female voices, and gender analysis exposing the binaries (man/spirit vs woman/body/nature) that justify oppression.

Significance, criticism, today

It has permanently altered the landscape — theological anthropology, theology of the body, and atonement theory cannot now be done without addressing theology's implication in gender domination. Criticism: early white Northern feminist theology was race-blind, projecting white middle-class experience as universal "women's experience" — the womanist and Third World protest. Debate continues over whether female God-images and female identification with the body reinforce the very binaries they mean to break. Today: intersections with postcolonial theory, ecofeminism, and gay, lesbian and queer theologies challenging normative heterosexuality and binary gender.

B. Black theology of liberation (Dwight N. Hopkins)

The founding

Sparked by the 1966 "Black Power" statement of black clergy. James H. Cone, Black Theology and Black Power (1969): the core message of the Bible is the liberation of the materially poor and oppressed, and all God-talk inevitably advances some notion of racial power. There is no neutral theology in a racialized society.

Not primarily academic discourse but reflection on, and guidance for, praxis located in grassroots communities, using the social sciences — including Marxist social analysis — to critique the structures that manufacture poverty.

Significance

It ruptured modern theology by shifting the interlocutor from the bourgeois subject confronted with atheism to the non-person confronted with poverty. It demonstrated the situatedness of all knowledge and made ideology critique part of theological method.

Criticism and current state

First-World critics attacked the near-equation of liberation with redemption as temporal messianism; others found the social theory of consciousness and structure underdeveloped. The three critiques these movements make of each other

  • Womanist → black theology: you made black women invisible.
  • Womanist and Third World → white feminism: your "women's experience" was white and middle class.
  • Postcolonial → liberation theology: you romanticized the Bible, privileged Exodus while ignoring the Canaanites, and homogenized "the poor" in purely economic terms (see 09 - Particularizing Theology II - African, South Asian, East Asian, Postcolonial). Learn these three, and you can situate almost any contemporary contextual argument.

09 — Particularizing Theology II: African, South Asian, East Asian, Postcolonial

The most important note in this unit for my own context. Chapters 28–31 are where the book stops describing theology done about the two-thirds world and starts quoting theology done from it.

A. African theology (Tinyiko Sam Maluleke)

The two founding paradigms — and their collapse into each other

Paradigm Home Question
Inculturation Sub-Saharan "African theology" How does the gospel speak in African culture and religion?
Liberation South African Black theology How does the gospel confront apartheid and economic oppression?
The historic divide between the two has collapsed; no serious African theologian now treats culture and justice as alternatives.

Figures

  • Kwame Bediako and Lamin Sanneh — translation theologies: the explosive growth of African Christianity is due to the gospel's translatability into African vernaculars, which shifts the story away from missionary imperialism toward African agency. Christianity is not a Western religion arriving; it is a faith that becomes indigenous by being translated.
  • Mercy Amba Oduyoye — pioneer of African feminist theology; founder of the Circle of Concerned African Women Theologians. She critically appropriates African traditional wisdom (proverbs, storytelling) against both colonial and patriarchal oppression.
  • Jesse Mugambi and Charles Villa-Vicencio — the theology of reconstruction: after the Cold War, Africa must move past the reactive paradigms of inculturation and liberation to a proactive agenda of democracy, human rights and nation-building.

Method, significance, criticism

African theology is utilitarian and ecclesial — often danced, sung and lived rather than written (the African Initiated Churches as enacted, oral theology), and necessarily interreligious, in constant dialogue with African Traditional Religions (ATR). It established African culture as a legitimate interpretive framework for the gospel.

Maluleke's criticisms, from the inside: - Reconstruction theologies "jump too quickly from Egypt to Canaan," skipping the wilderness of post-colonial Africa. - African theology has historically lacked a vigorous biblical hermeneutics debate, treating the Bible as an absolute oracle. - Harmonizing Christianity and ATR can be patronizing, treating ATR merely as a praeparatio evangelica.

Today: HIV/AIDS, globalization, and an expanding African feminist/womanist theology that critiques African culture without denigrating it.

Method

Highly contextual and multi-religious: theology is done in dialogue with classical Hinduism, Buddhism and Islam, and the "dialogue of everyday life" — not the seminar — is the starting point of a theology of religions.

Significance

South Asia triggered a paradigm shift in the theology of religions, moving past both exclusivism and inclusivism. The formula: "Jesus is the Christ; Christ is not Jesus only."

The internal quarrel — and why it matters most

The Dalit critique of inculturation

Dalit Christians attack the mainstream inculturation movement for drawing on classical, Brahminical Hinduism — the very tradition that legitimated their oppression. An ashram theology built on Sanskrit advaita can be, in practice, the sacralizing of the caste order in Christian dress. The permanent tension is between incarnation (rooting the church in the local culture) and prophecy (denouncing that same culture).

Today: it forces fundamental theology past abstract questions of faith and reason to primordial life issues — food, water, shelter; and it supplies resources for Christians surviving as religious minorities (alongside Islam in Pakistan and Bangladesh, Buddhism in Sri Lanka, Hindutva majoritarianism in India).

D. Postcolonial biblical interpretation (R. S. Sugirtharajah)

Figures. Sugirtharajah — contrapuntal reading: view a text simultaneously from the experience of the marginalized and of the mainstream to expose imperial imbalance. Fernando Segovia — the theoretical and methodological basis; a hermeneutics of diaspora privileging the periphery over the centre, insisting that both texts and readers are culturally conditioned.

Key terms. Orientalism (Western misrepresentation and homogenization of the Other) · hybridity · subaltern · contrapuntal reading · hermeneutics of diaspora.

Method. An oppositional, anti-colonial discourse that does two things at once: it scrutinizes the biblical text itself for imperial impulses, land appropriation and hegemonic intent; and it unmasks the Eurocentric ideology hidden inside "neutral" Western historical criticism.

Significance. Colonialism is put at the centre of both the biblical narratives (produced in the shadow of Egypt, Assyria, Babylon, Rome) and of the history of interpretation.

The quarrel with liberation hermeneutics. Postcolonial critics charge liberation theology with: 1. Romantically elevating the Bible — postcolonialism sees it as a tainted text of both emancipation and enslavement; 2. Privileging the Exodus while ignoring the displacement of the indigenous Canaanites — read from an adivasi or Palestinian standpoint, Exodus is a conquest narrative; 3. Homogenizing "the poor" in purely economic terms while ignoring race, gender and ethnic plurality.

Today: globalization, neo-colonialism, mass migration, refugees, and the multi-layered identities of diaspora.

The single most transferable idea in this note

Cross-textual hermeneutics (Lee) + contrapuntal reading (Sugirtharajah) + Pieris's two poles. Together they give a workable Indian exegetical procedure: read the pericope with the Indian text (story, caste reality, scripture of the neighbour) as a genuine second text; read it once from the centre and once from the subaltern position; and ask what it says to both the religiosity and the poverty. That is a method a seminarian can actually run — and it is close to what Soares-Prabhu was already doing.

10 — Global Engagements

Four bodies of theology the standard Western systematics syllabus tends to skip — and which together account for the majority of the world's Christians.

A. Ecumenical theology (Mary Tanner)

Origins. The modern ecumenical movement was born of the missionary movement of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, where cooperation mattered more than denominational difference (Edinburgh 1910). Two streams — Faith and Order (doctrine) and Life and Work (social action) — converged in the World Council of Churches, 1948.

The permanent tension. Reflective ecumenism (doctrinal agreement for visible unity) vs practical ecumenism (unity found in social action and mission).

The methodological turn — Montreal 1963. From comparative theology (each tradition stating its position over against the others) to a consensus/convergence method, recognizing that scripture and tradition properly belong together.

BEM — Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry (Lima, 1982). The most widely distributed ecumenical document of the twentieth century; a convergence text recording real breakthroughs across Catholic, Orthodox, Evangelical and Pentecostal theologians — e.g. anamnesis used to understand the Eucharist as making Christ's passion present, and bridges built over infant/believer baptism and the threefold ordained ministry.

Koinonia. Communion/fellowship became the framework: divided churches can recognize an already existing degree of communion — real though imperfect — and move step by step toward full visible communion.

Current problems. Balancing the quest for unity with the world's agenda of justice and peace; sharp method tension between theology done from within ancient tradition and theology begun from a specific context; and the unresolved question of the goal: organic union (denominations cease to exist) or reconciled diversity (distinct bodies in full fellowship).

B. Eastern Orthodox theology (Rowan Williams)

Figure Programme
S. N. Bulgakov (1871–1944) Sophiology — a unified theological metaphysic centred on Sophia, the organic whole of reality, fragmented in the universe but one in God. Kenosis is normative for all language about God in creation and redemption. Christ is where uncreated and created Sophia are united, overcoming human isolation
V. N. Lossky (1903–58) Violent rejection of Russian philosophical idealism for an "authentic" patristic synthesis. Apophaticism as the primordial theological moment — a stripping of the intellect leading to ecstasy and the birth of personal being in response to God's self-gift. Champions Palamas's essence/energies distinction: God is genuinely participated in without compromising his incomprehensibility
G. V. Florovsky (1893–1979) Drives the neo-patristic synthesis and "Christian Hellenism" — theology must return to the baptized Hellenism of the Fathers. History as a pattern of free acts; the historical Jesus's podvig (achievement/exploit) as the triumph of freedom over necessity and death, liberating humanity for proper historical action
John Zizioulas (1931–2023) Ecclesiology tied to a metaphysic of relation: the ultimate source of reality is not isolated substance but the trinitarian relational system — being as communion
Dumitru Stăniloae (1903–93) Renewal of Romanian dogmatics through Maximus the Confessor: the world as a vehicle of divine agency, brought to completion in Christ and the Church

The internal Orthodox argument

Bulgakov's sophiology was condemned by both Moscow and the Karlovtsy synod; Lossky was among its sharpest critics. The Lossky–Florovsky "neo-patristic synthesis" won, and it is the reason twentieth-century Orthodoxy presents itself as the patristic option in ecumenical discussion. The cost, as Williams notes, is a certain flattening of Russian religious philosophy's creativity.

C. Pentecostal and charismatic theology (Allan Anderson)

Three phases 1. Classical Pentecostalism — from the Azusa Street Revival, 1906–08, forming distinct denominations. 2. The Charismatic movement — from the 1960s, within historic Protestant and Catholic churches. 3. Neopentecostal / "Third Wave" — independent megachurches from the mid-1970s.

Theological emphases. A dynamic, enacted pneumatology: the Spirit as the active leader of worship who empowers believers to prophesy, speak in tongues, heal and exorcise. The "full gospel" — Jesus as Saviour, Healer, Baptizer in the Spirit, and Soon Coming King. Biblical literalism. Spiritual warfare and deliverance. The classical "doctrine of consequence" (tongues always follow Spirit baptism) is increasingly challenged from within.

Global significance. The fastest-growing segment of world Christianity. Its holistic worldview, uniting the physical and the spiritual, addresses felt needs for healing and power far more effectively than rationalistic Western theology — which is why it is reshaping religion in the global South, and why it is draining Latin American base communities (08 - Particularizing Theology I - Feminist, Black, Latin American).

11 — Theology Between Faiths, and Theology in Many Media

A. Theology of religions (Gavin D'Costa)

The threefold typology

Position Claim
Exclusivism Salvation requires explicit confession of Christ
Inclusivism Christ is the normative revelation, but salvation is possible outside the visible Church
Pluralism All religions are valid paths to the divine

The two named cases

  • John Hick — pluralism. From a fundamentalist background to a theocentric pluralism in which all religions are grounded in one divine reality; later a Kantian distinction between the noumenal "Eternal One" and the phenomenal, culturally conditioned religious responses to it. Christological language becomes metaphorical rather than metaphysical ("the myth of God incarnate").
  • Karl Rahner — inclusivism. The anonymous Christian: on transcendental grounds, all humans have an unthematic, prereflective awareness of grace; when non-Christians respond to it in trusting love they implicitly respond to Christ, the prime mediator, however imperfectly mediated through their own religion.

D'Costa's own move

He attacks the typology itself. "Pluralism" is a disguised exclusivism — an exclusivist secular agnosticism — because it intolerantly rules out any truth-claim that fails its own liberal modern criteria. His alternative: use trinitarian theology to engage other religions critically, holding radical difference and fragmentary commonality in tension rather than dissolving one into the other.

Why this matters for Indian theology

The South Asia chapter (09 - Particularizing Theology II - African, South Asian, East Asian, Postcolonial) says the paradigm shift has moved past exclusivism and inclusivism; D'Costa says pluralism is not the exit. Between them lies the actual Indian working position: dialogue at the level of life and of core religious experience (Pieris), not at the level of a theory that has already decided all religions say the same thing.

B. Judaism and Christian theology (Peter Ochs)

  • Supersessionism — the traditional belief that the Church replaced Israel in God's covenant.
  • Post-Holocaust theology — from the 1950s–60s, Roy Eckardt and John Pawlikowski demanded that Christians renounce supersessionism and anti-Judaism as roots of the Shoah.
  • Karl Barth — the paradox: traditional Christocentric formulations that appear to denigrate the synagogue, joined to a strong affirmation of the irrevocable election of the Jewish people. His students (Friedrich-Wilhelm Marquardt, Peter von der Osten-Sacken) built post-Barthian, non-supersessionist Christologies on that foundation.
  • R. Kendall Soulen, The God of Israel and Christian Theology — attacks the "semi-gnostic" Christian habit of making creation and consummation subservient to redemption-from-sin. God's identity is inseparable from Israel's election; Christ is the climactic recapitulation of God's work for the mutual blessing of Jew and Gentile.
  • Scriptural Reasoning — postliberal non-supersessionist work led Daniel Hardy and David Ford to co-found the Society for Scriptural Reasoning: Jewish, Christian and Muslim scholars read their scriptures together to discover shared rules of cooperative reasoning. (This is Ford's own eleventh thesis in practice — see 12 - Ford's Twelve Theses and Theology Today.)

C. Islam and Christian theology (Ataullah Siddiqui)

  • Samuel Zwemer — the older adversarial missiology.
  • Kenneth Cragg, The Call of the Minaret — broke new ground by appreciating the Prophet Muhammad's religious and political achievement; the touchstone for a fairer Christian assessment of Islam.
  • Hans Küng — Islam strikes the West as foreign and politically threatening, and precisely therefore ecumenical theology must face the challenge; Christians and Muslims believe in the same Creator God; hence the Global Ethic project.
  • Keith Ward — an "open theology": Islam offers a "unique witness to the nature and purpose of God"; God's revelation is not exhausted by one's own tradition.

D. Buddhism and Christian theology (Paul O. Ingram)

Three forms of dialogue: conceptual, socially engaged, interior. - John B. Cobb Jr (conceptual) — "creative transformation": incorporate Mahāyāna śūnyatā (emptying) and non-self into Christian theology, showing the affinities between these, Whitehead's process philosophy, and the Pauline understanding of God and selfhood. - Paul Knitter (socially engaged) — conceptual dialogue is elitist unless rooted in a preferential option for the poor; Christians and Buddhists find common ground by turning dialogue into a shared struggle against oppression.

E. Theology in many media (Parts VIII, chs 40–42)

Visual arts (John W. de Gruchy)

The arts are intrinsic to theology, not decoration. John Dillenberger mapped the theological approaches; P. T. Forsyth is the early prophet of the theme. Barth was cautious about images (fear of idolatry); Balthasar restored beauty through an incarnational lens. Neo-Calvinists (Wolterstorff, Seerveld) treat art as an instrument of action for God's glory and social transformation. The bridging concept is embodiment: the artist communicates through material reality as God encounters humanity through the Incarnation.

Music (Jeremy Begbie)

Albert Blackwell — music's "sacramental potential" through the Pythagorean (mathematical cosmic order) and Incarnational (sensed materiality) traditions. Begbie situates music within Christology and trinitarian theology: musical tension and resolution school believers in biblical patience; polyphony — hearing several notes simultaneously without their crowding each other out — supplies a non-competitive spatial model for the hypostatic union and the mutual indwelling of the Trinity. Barth let Mozart bear theological witness to grace; Bonhoeffer used polyphony as a governing metaphor in prison.

Film (Jolyon Mitchell)

From moral discrimination (censorship-era) to aesthetic and theological dialogue. John May and Rob Johnston built taxonomies from avoidance to divine encounter; Hurley, May and Wall treat the director's vision (auteur theory) as a channel of revelation; Larry Kreitzer uses film in a triadic approach to illuminate biblical texts; Peter Malone links films to the Catholic lectionary for worship; filmmakers themselves (Lucas) embed spirituality to awaken audiences to "the mystery."

12 — Ford's Twelve Theses, and What All This Is For

A. The Epilogue: "Twelve Theses for Christian Theology in the Twenty-first Century"

How Ford offers them

Twelve maxims or slogans, given as a personal postscript and deliberately without commentary or explanation — "a stimulus provoking readers in the twenty-first century to think, talk, and write Christian theology." Do not treat them as a system; treat them as a set of provocations to argue with.

  1. God is the One who blesses and loves in wisdom.
  2. Theology is done for the sake of God and the Kingdom of God.
  3. The cry of prayer is the beginning, accompaniment and end of theology — Come, Holy Spirit! Hallelujah! Maranatha!
  4. Study of scripture is at the heart of theology.
  5. Describing reality in the light of God is a basic theological discipline.
  6. Theology hopes in and seeks God's purposes while immersed in the contingencies, complexities and ambiguities of creation and history.
  7. Theological wisdom seeks to do justice to many contexts, levels, voices, moods, genres, systems and responsibilities.
  8. Theology is practised collegially, in conversation and, best of all, in friendship; and through the communion of saints it is simultaneously premodern, modern and postmodern.
  9. Theology is a broker of the arts, humanities, sciences and common sense — for the sake of a wisdom that affirms, critiques and transforms each of them.
  10. Our religious and secular world needs theology with religious studies in its schools and universities.
  11. Conversation around scriptures is at the heart of interfaith relations.
  12. Theology is for all who desire to think about God and about reality in relation to God.

How to read the twelve as a summary of the whole book

Thesis 3 answers Part IV (prayer and practice). Thesis 4 answers Part II-E (scripture). Theses 6–7 answer Part V (particularizing). Thesis 8 answers Part II-D (retrieval — premodern, modern and postmodern at once). Thesis 9 answers Part III (the sciences) and Part VIII (the arts). Theses 10 and 12 answer the introduction's anxiety about theology's three publics. Thesis 11 is Scriptural Reasoning, and answers Part VII. Ford's epilogue is the book's own table of contents turned into imperatives.

C. Application — how I actually use this

(This section is my own synthesis, not the book's. Keep the citation streams separate.)

1. For teaching (BTh, MSRS)

  • Ford's five types are a grading tool for essays. When a student writes on any modern figure, ask: where on the spectrum does this theologian start, and does the student see why? It exposes the commonest student error — treating a theologian's conclusions without their starting point.
  • The Part V critiques are the best available antidote to a decontextualized dogmatics course. Teach the womanist critique of black theology, the Third World critique of white feminism, and the postcolonial critique of liberation theology together: they teach students that a liberating theology can itself exclude.
  • Use the flashcard blocks in notes 02–11 directly for spaced repetition; the exam questions in 14 - Glossary, Revision and Exam Questions are built to match.

2. For preaching and pastoral work in India

  • Kitamori, Koyama, Ahn Byung Mu, Pieris and Sobrino give a usable theology of suffering that does not explain pain away: the pain of God, the crucified mind, the ochlos, the two poles of religiosity and poverty, the crucified people. That is a homiletic vocabulary for Indian congregations that patristic impassibility cannot supply.
  • The Dalit critique of inculturation is a permanent check on my own liturgical and theological instincts. Every time an Indian-Christian symbol is proposed, ask whose Hinduism it comes from and who it costs.
  • Pentecostalism is a pastoral fact, not a topic. Chapter 34 explains why the poor leave for churches of healing and power. Any Catholic pastoral plan in Bengal that ignores this is answering a question nobody is asking.

3. For interreligious work

  • D'Costa's move — refuse the threefold typology — is the honest position. Neither the imperial "anonymous Christian" nor the flattening pluralism that decides in advance that all traditions say the same thing.
  • Ford's thesis 11 plus Scriptural Reasoning gives the method: read the neighbour's scripture with the neighbour, without a prior theory of what the reading must yield. In India that is scripturally reasoning with the Gita, the Qur'an and the Dhammapada — the "dialogue of everyday life" (Wilfred) at the textual level.

4. For writing

  • Thesis 8 — theology practised in friendship, simultaneously premodern, modern and postmodern — is the licence for the way this vault is built: patristic sources, modern systematics and postcolonial criticism in the same note, cross-linked rather than sequenced.
  • Thesis 9 — theology as broker of arts, humanities, sciences and common sense — is the standard against which to test whether a piece of writing is doing theology or just reporting it.

A. Glossary (from the book's own glossary)

Term Definition
A priori Reasoning from causes to effects or from abstract notions; knowledge held independent of evidence or warrant
Analogy of being Aquinas: "being" is predicated analogously of God and creatures, because God causes being in creatures and because of the similarity-in-greater-dissimilarity between divine and creaturely existence
Analogy of faith Barth: though God is unknowable to us, God grants believers a share in his own self-knowledge through faith, so that scripture becomes an adequate witness
Apophatic Negative theology: stressing God's transcendence over human language and preferring to state what God is not
Creation ex nihilo God created the world out of nothing, with no pre-existing material
Deconstruction A destabilizing analysis exposing arbitrariness or bias in texts and showing how their logic invites its own refutation
Demythologization Interpreting mythological texts so as to show their continuing existential and practical relevance despite an outmoded worldview
Dialectical theology Post-1918 theology radically negating human ways of knowing God (centred on the crucifixion) and stressing the necessity of God's initiative in revelation
Eschatology The doctrine of the ultimate destiny of the world
Existentialism A movement focused on individual subjectivity: existence precedes essence, worked out in decision, and in anxiety, alienation and despair
Foundationalism The thesis that valid philosophical or theological truth-claims must rest on self-evident propositions or truths of experience
Kenosis Self-emptying: Christ laying aside divine attributes to become fully human
Kerygma Proclamation — the existential message preached by Jesus and the early church
Liberation theology A school originating in Latin America in the 1960s applying Christian faith from the standpoint of the needs of the poor and exploited
Metanarrative An overarching narrative that attempts to comprehend, include or explain all other narratives
Ontology The branch of philosophy concerned with being, or reality in its most fundamental forms
Panentheism The world exists in God without negating God's transcendence; God is both in and beyond the world
Postliberalism A movement (Yale, Duke) affirming orthodoxy, criticizing liberal reliance on human experience, and reclaiming community tradition and biblical narrative as controlling
Postmodernism The position that the present is discontinuous with modernity, sceptical of universal rational principles, objective truth and progress
Soteriology The doctrine of salvation, reconciliation, atonement or redemption
Transcendental theology Kant-influenced theology understanding God as the ultimate horizon and term of the subject's self-transcending movement in experience

B. One-page revision sheet

The whole book in ten lines

  1. 1918: liberal culture-theology dies; Barth reasserts God's Word against religion.
  2. Ford's five types measure how a theologian relates Christian identity to modernity — 2 (self-description), 3 (correlation), 4 (integration) are where the real work happens.
  3. Part I classics split two ways: Barth/Bonhoeffer/de Lubac/Balthasar start from the object; Tillich and Rahner start from the question in the subject.
  4. Post-war systematics — Pannenberg bets on history and public reason; Moltmann on eschatology and the suffering God; Torrance on scientific realism.
  5. American theology turns to ethics and the public square (the Niebuhrs) and to revisionist correlation (Tracy, Ogden, Kaufman).
  6. 1984, Lindbeck: doctrine is a rule of communal grammar, not a proposition or a feeling — the postliberal turn to retrieval.
  7. The sciences return as a theological topic once reductionism collapses; divine action and panentheism are the arguments.
  8. Part V: theology is particularized — feminist, black, Latin American, African, South Asian, East Asian, postcolonial — and these movements then criticize each other.
  9. Part VI: the actual global Church — ecumenical convergence, Orthodox neo-patristic synthesis, the Pentecostal explosion, evangelical resurgence.
  10. Ford's epilogue: theology as prayer, scripture, wisdom, friendship, and the brokering of the disciplines — for the academy, the churches and society.

The five arguments to be able to reconstruct

  1. Rahner vs Balthasar — start from the hearer or from the form?
  2. Lindbeck vs Tracy — intratextual retrieval or correlational mediation?
  3. Milbank vs the social sciences — is secular reason a rival theology?
  4. Postcolonial vs liberation hermeneutics — is the Bible a liberating text or a tainted one?
  5. Dalit critique vs inculturation — whose culture is being inculturated, and at whose cost?
