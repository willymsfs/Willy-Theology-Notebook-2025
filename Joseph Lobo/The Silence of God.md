**The Silence of God**

by[**Joseph Lobo, SJ**](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/author/no-reply-editor-144/)

 [April 19, 2023](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/)
 

_The context_

Many people are overwhelmed by what is going on in our times: the rise of fundamentalism, deceitful populism, malicious majoritarianism, violent right-wing politics with their jingoistic policies, the abject poverty of the masses, forced displacement of people, misery unleashed by the COVID pandemic, the “almighty” corporates building their empires on the skeletons of the exploited, the expanding ecological disasters. Above all, people are dumbfounded by the oft-perceived and dreadfully deadening “silence” of God, and a resulting human cry of anguish that finds a resonance with the cry of the prophet Habakkuk: “Oh Lord, how long shall I cry for help and you will not hear? Why are you silent when the wicked man swallows up the one more righteous than he?” (_Hab_ 1:2, 13). One wonders what all this means.

In this article, I do not strictly intend to address the typical, traditional question of theodicy that rather fruitlessly shuttles among three poles: 1) God is good, but not all-powerful; therefore, evil exists; 2) God is all-powerful, but not good; therefore, evil exists; 3) objective evil does not exist; it is either the privation of good or only a subjective perception.

A fourth position would be to hold that God allows the misuse of human freedom, which causes evil and suffering. There could be even a fifth position, based on texts such as _Deut_ 11:13-17, and interpreting evil and suffering as retributive actions of God against human disobedience. However, _John_ 9:2-3 decisively argues against such interpretations. Keeping all of these in mind, I intend to reflect on the felt “silence of God” in our times, not so much as to speculate regarding it, as to suggest some fruitful ways of responding to it.

_The felt silence of God_

The acute experience of the silence of God in some of the most brutal and violent events of history leads to a human outcry similar to: “My God, My God, why have you forsaken me?” (_Psalm_ 22:1). In fact, the Son of God himself was not an exception to this experience. What, then, of lesser mortals? Hence, we have Job lamenting: “I cry to you for help and you do not answer me; I stand, and you only look at me” (_Job_ 30:20), and the Psalmist crying out: “Why are you so far from saving me, from the words of my groaning? O my God, I cry by day, but you do not answer, and by night, but I find no rest” (_Psalm_ 22:1–2).

In all this, the worst situation would be to envisage God as capricious with no concern for his suffering creatures. The compatibility of divine love with divine silence thus becomes a crucial issue here.

_Some responses_

In the Christian tradition there are two major approaches to understand Divine-human relations: 1) the one where God is totally the OTHER, absolutely sovereign, immutable and impassible; 2) the typically Biblical one, especially in the lamentation Psalms, where human beings grapple with God: plead with God for mercy while acknowledging themselves to be guilty, but demand an explanation when the pain and suffering seem undeserved. Although there is protest against God in such texts, that protest co-exists with a deep trust in God’s providence.[1](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn1) While the scholastic approach does not really do full justice to the incarnation, some Biblical depictions risk over-anthropomorphizing God.

According to the Jewish philosopher Martin Buber, the Absolute (God) does not communicate any specific word or law to humans. The events of the ordinary course of one’s life in which one has to make decisions, constitute the actual communication of God; and the human decisions in such events are their response to God. “The events that occur to human beings are the… untranslatable but unmistakable signs of their being addressed; what they do and fail to do can be an answer or a failure to answer.”[2](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn2)

In this scheme of things, God’s revelation actually shows human beings to themselves, in and through the way they respond to the events of their lives. “Revelation does not deal with the mystery of God but with the life of man. And it deals with the life of man as that which can and should be lived in the face of the mystery of God.”[3](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn3) According to Buber, however, God as such seems to be eternally silent and there is no scope for the revelation of anything that is actually true of God. Hence the silence of God is to be expected or more positively, it creates a space for human response.

Clearly, anyone who believes in Biblical revelation in general and in incarnation in particular, may find it difficult to accept Buber’s position. From a theistic perspective, God’s silence may appear as an opportunity for the human person to remain committed to God in true love and freedom. For, if God were to speak definitively, humans would feel compelled to respond to God out of fear of punishment or desire for reward, but not out of genuine love and freedom. Alternatively, the sense of divine silence can produce endurance in the sufferer and solidarity with others who suffer.[4](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn4) It may be that we still need a profound purification in order to share God’s holiness (cf. _Heb_ 12:10).

However, none of these explanations may lift or lighten the existential burden on believers created by God’s silence. And so, “[I]t is not clear how one can understand the love of a God who permits demoralizing experiences of divine silence.”[5](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn5) Among varied responses, the emergence of an apocalyptic mindset is quite common. We shall attempt to outline a few significant features of it.

_Helplessness and apocalypse_

In the Judeo-Christian tradition, many a seemingly hopeless situation of calamities and persecution has been interpreted through apocalyptic worldviews. Although in the Oxford English Dictionary one meaning of the word “apocalypse” is “the complete final destruction of the world,” its etymology interestingly points toward “uncovering” (Gk: _apokalypsis_) and “revelation.” This paves the way to interpret the tragedies of our times as occasions of hope-inspiring revelation. The Bible has a multitude of such references.

_A critical appraisal of the apocalyptic worldview_

The circumstance of the emergence of Apocalyptic literature is broadly identified as a time of persecution and suffering, combined with utter helplessness to overcome the same. It is then that the persecuted ones cry out for divine intervention to put an end to the persecution and bring the persecutors to justice. While a vengeful attitude in terms of retribution may not always be a part of that cry, yet, in certain texts we do find it.

Commonly known reasons for the inclusion of vengeance-seeking texts in the scriptures range from merely reproducing the available source material to scaring the readers into living morally upright lives; from providing a catharsis for a helplessly suffering audience[6](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn6) to hoping for an end-time setting-right of wrongs through a violent intervention of God; from genuinely desiring a change of heart in the tormentor through God’s chastisement[7](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn7) to a natural outcry for justice. Hence an apocalyptic worldview can be researched from moral, socio-psychological, theological and political perspectives.

It may be inferred that a major factor at play in the emergence of an apocalyptic worldview, is the apparent “silence of God” in the context of suffering and persecution, where the perpetrators of calamities go scot-free. These observations become significant at a time when many Christian groups have developed apocalyptic mindsets that have made them passive spectators of the current negativities. Some, on the other hand, may have begun an ultra-aggressive “evangelization” if it can be genuinely called evangelization. Such attitudes are also to be found in the adherents of other religions with their respective specificities. None of these seem to make a fruitful response to the current Asian, and comparable global, scenarios.

_Silence of God in the Bible_

In the Old Testament, God is more a speaking God than a silent God. While about 1882 verbs and nouns point to God’s speech, only 29 verbs and nouns explicitly denote God’s silence.[8](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn8) Yet, the silence of God and the resulting human lamentation is a major aspect of Judeo-Christian tradition. Psalms 94:17 and 115:17 in their understanding of the Hebrew word _dumah_ (silence) closely associate it with death. Such a state indicates a complete breakdown of communication between God and human beings.[9](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn9)

In a similar line, a lack of prophecy is lamented by Israel, although prophecy often was a harsh critique of their infidelity to God (cf. _Mic_ 3:6-7). _1 Kings_ 19 reports an encounter of prophet Elijah with God. He finds God in a “still small voice” (_qol demamah daqqa_) (v.12) which is almost a silence in comparison with the strong wind, raging fire and noisy earthquake that preceded it. This silence contains or is followed by a theophany.

Thomas Martin, relying on several scholarly studies, presents an interesting insight about the contrast between “loud voices” and the “low voice” referred to in the book of Revelation.[10](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn10) Accordingly, the “still small voice” of God (_1 Kings_ 19 esp. v. 12f), if interpreted as God speaking apparently in a “noiseless voice” in _Rev_ 1:1-8 or 21:5f, shows a deconstruction of “loud voices” heard in heaven by the narrator that are mentioned in many places in the book of Revelation. The narrator has mistaken them for the voice of God; and the implied author offers a critique of the narrator.[11](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn11) The narrator’s mistake lies in associating the loud imperial noises (publicity and public image-building that was usual practice in the Roman empire) with that of heaven. Indeed, John the narrator is asked not to write down anything when he hears the voice of the “seven thunders” (10:2f), indicating the absence of revelation in it. In other words, the narrator wrongly expects God to act “noisily” like a Roman emperor in hope of ending the suffering of the Christians. But the implied author points to a different God, one who speaks in a low voice, almost in silence. What we gather here, is that “the speaking” of God need not be loud and noisy; it can be as soft as silence itself.

In almost a mystical vein, _Rom_ 8:26 speaks of “sighs too deep for words” with which the Spirit of God “intercedes” from within us, because “we do not know how to pray as we ought.” These sighs of the Spirit are the communication bridge between God and us, and are inexpressible in any human language. They indicate a silence that is paradoxically pregnant with communication. It is in and through such ‘silent’ sighs that the unsayable is communicated. Indeed, the Bible treats the theme of “the silence of God” in a variety of ways. It is on this foundation that various shades of Christian understanding of such silence develop.

_Contours of Christian hope_

Amidst all the affliction and potential despair resulting from the silence of God, hope is given a prime place in the Christian tradition.

What is hope? Hope cannot be restricted to mere desires and beliefs. Hope is expressed in activity and in the case of an unfulfilled goal, can even reconfigure such a goal while remaining faithful to the good desired in it.[12](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn12) For instance, the hope of the first century A.D. Israelites of being freed from the Roman bondage is reconfigured in the light of Christ-Event into a more profound hope for freedom from sin and death – far beyond political freedom. Further, all tendencies of idle hope and consequent passivity were squarely critiqued (“Anyone unwilling to work should not eat!” _2 Thess_ 3:10), and an active engagement with the world and the powers that be became a prominent teaching in the early Church. Thus, the reconfigured hope made them transformed agents of change.

These considerations lead us to enquire whether the person on the borderlines of total despair, while experiencing God’s silence, has truly exhausted all the possible ways to respond to the current crisis constructively. A possible failure to do so needs to be acknowledged.[13](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn13) The door that was knocked on may not have opened, but several other doors may have opened without even being knocked on (cf _Matt_ 7:7).

Further, the Christian tradition makes an important and useful distinction between “natural hope” and “hope as a theological virtue.”[14](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn14) This can shed some light on the issue of the “silence of God.” The “natural hope” is human passion toward a perceived good that is difficult to attain. The “theological virtue” of hope refers to the human desire for ultimate union with God.[15](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn15) When a case is made regarding the possible loss of hope due to the silence of God in the midst of human distress, it is not clear if the natural hope is referred to or the theological virtue of hope, or both.

Going by Karl Rahner’s concept of “supernatural existential,”[16](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn16) which is ultimately an existential desire in the human being for any communication and communion with God, it seems impossible to lose the theological virtue of hope, since it is the gift of God to every human being, and it remains in the human subject even if one were to consciously reject God. “Supernatural Existential” is the inseparable part of human existence (therefore “existential”). As Rahner makes it clear, it need not be always at the conscious level, since it is a condition of our existence. Hence there is a strong reason to believe that even in the case of a total loss of “natural hope” the “theological virtue of hope” could still continue to exist in us without our explicit awareness of it.

Finally, it is true that not all objects of “natural hope” (finite goals) may be helpful for arriving at the goal of the theological virtue of hope (communion with God). Hence when “natural hope” is frustrated by the silence of God, it can also be beneficial for the ultimate good of the person.[17](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn17)

_Heaven’s silence as a prelude to an important revelation?_

Utterances make sense only when the words in them are interspaced by silence. Words and interspacing silence make the warp and woof of an integral speech. Therefore, silence is not a lack of speech, but that which makes speech possible, as much as words do. _Rev_ 8:1 speaks of half an hour’s silence upon opening the seventh seal by the Lamb. Falling back on _Zech_ 2:13; _Hab_ 2:20; _Ezek_ 3:15-16 this silence can be interpreted as a prelude to God’s revelation.[18](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn18) Although, attributing literally such a reason for God’s silence in the face of human affliction could amount to over-anthropomorphizing of God, nevertheless, it does make sense to think of God as hearing creatures prior to revelatory speech.

In Rahner’s terms, we are “hearers of the Word”[19](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn19) and are restless because at times we do not hear it historically in a crisis. Unheard melodies may be sweeter than those that are heard; but in this case the unheard melodies are longed for because their primal reality has left an unerasable etching in the very structure of our existence. Therefore, there is more reason to believe that God in God’s very silence cannot but be revealing.

_Can the Triune God be silent?_

The eternal Trinitarian relational processes of the Father begetting the Son, the Son’s active reception of His being from the Father, the breathing out (_spiration_) of the Spirit by the Father and the Son and the Spirit’s active reception of His being from the Father and the Son, can be conceived as essential “speech acts” in the Immanent Trinity. Although Karl Rahner, fearing a possible tritheistic interpretation, hesitates to posit an “I-Thou” in the Immanent Trinity,[20](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn20) so long as the Christian faith affirms personal distinctions in the Trinity, one can certainly speak of the Son as the Thou of the Father. And so, a truly “silent Triune God” is a contradiction in terms; for, the Trinity is fundamentally “communication.” This intra-Trinitarian “communication” overflows into speaking creation, into being in time and space out of an overflowing love and freedom of God. The idols indeed cannot speak, but the living God speaks. God’s speech is that which brings into being and sustains all that exists. Hence, seen from the Trinitarian angle, the silence of God is a matter of human perception and cannot be real on the part of God.

_Silence of God and Incarnation_

The incarnational view of God’s silence has given rise to different interpretations. For instance, Simone Weil (1909-1943), the French philosopher-mystic and political activist, held that in creating the space-time-matter universe, God withdrew from the universe. For, God the infinite cannot in any way be subject to spatio-temporality. Most fundamentally, it is this withdrawal that results in all the human pain regarding the “silence of God.”

In Weil’s view, by withdrawing from the world, God gave the world over to mechanistic forces, due to which there is a stable order in creation. In the routine operation of these forces there is not any special divine providence.[21](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn21) That is to say, God does not intervene and violate the mechanistic order, for instance, by miraculously destroying a despot, or neutralizing a devastating hurricane. God’s response to human cries about affliction is silence. Hence the ideal human attitude toward suffering, according to Weil, is gratitude despite the pain.[22](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn22)

Weil clearly goes against the Biblical depiction of God, who actively and passionately becomes involved in human affairs, and more radically, in Incarnation. There is no question of God’s withdrawing from creation here. God is “God with us” (Emmanuel). The enfleshed speech in incarnation indicates God’s most radical involvement in God’s historico-c deeds.

St. Ignatius of Loyola has another way of making sense of God’s silence. In his _Autobiography_, several times we find an Ignatius left high and dry, not knowing how to proceed. Four types of response to such situations can be traced: 1) waiting patiently and then prayerfully deciding at the proper moment, thereby giving himself time with patience and trust in God; 2) taking everything to God and earnestly seeking God’s will with prayers, masses, penance and fasting; 3) seeking help from “spiritual persons” – a practice that significantly declined in the post-Holy Land visit phase of his life, and later almost replaced by “spiritual conversation” with his companions; 4) surrendering to God and, specifically, to the Roman pontiff.

It becomes clear that one can actively seek the will of God in the signs of the times despite all odds. Indeed, St. Ignatius was gripped by the vision of a “larger picture” in his “pilgrim life” that would enable him to strive forward despite all the hurdles. This leads us to inquire about the possible revelation of a “larger picture” in the face of the felt silence of God.

_Revelation of a ‘larger picture’ in God’s silence_

Although whatever Job lost was restored to him twice over, yet Job’s queries were not answered by God, at least in a direct and easily comprehensible fashion. But in that very ambiguity, a “larger picture” was manifested to Job. In that picture, his own existence appeared insignificantly tiny. His consolation came “in the form of an overwhelming sense of finitude in the face of the vast power and activity of God.”[23](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn23) The larger picture would not have been visible if not for his suffering and God’s silence in the face of it.

The same is true in the case of the Son of God. In the Garden of Olives, the cup was not taken away but an angel was sent to strengthen him (_Luke_ 22:43). The coming of the angel marked the opening of a larger picture. In _Luke_ 22:32 Jesus had advised those who did not have a sword to buy one, even by selling their cloaks. However, the angel’s consolation seemed to have opened up a larger picture that made the sword redundant (_Luke_ 22:51; _John_ 18:11). The sword belongs to a small picture, a narrow world. That world is now transcended in the light of the larger picture. The larger picture ensures that there is no cry of anguish on the cross, but only a submission to God (_Luke_ 23:46).

Incidentally, in the accounts of Matthew and Mark there is no reference to the angel’s consolation, thereby opening up the larger picture. Here the cry of anguish is heard from the cross: “…why have you abandoned me?” (_Matt_ 27:46; _Mark_ 15:34). In these texts, the silence of God opens up the larger picture _postmortem_: the resurrection, revealed not only in the obedient and risen Son but also in the testimony of all those who witnessed him. That is how Paul could say “whether we live or die, we are the Lord’s” (_Rom_ 14:8). That is the larger picture. That is the source of our ultimate hope, which provides the necessary horizon to look at all the independent events of “the silence of God” with courage, confidence and commitment.

_Solidarity as a way forward_

In all the discussion about anguish experienced in the face of the silence of God, one often forgets the community’s responsibility toward accompanying an anguished individual. The attitude of Job’s friends is a classic example. Their preconceived theology of retribution hardly enabled them to achieve a meaningful solidarity with Job. A theology without a praxis of solidarity is thus shown as being sterile. A theology of solidarity, on the other hand, sees the foundations for the praxis of solidarity in the experience of God’s revelation. God’s personified solidarity with humans is the incarnation. This invites the faithful to follow a similar path of incarnating themselves into the anguish of others. Every case of despair and suicide, most fundamentally, is a societal/communitarian failure as much as an individual’s disaster. The community/society can be held responsible for such tragedies in two ways: 1) for pushing the individual into a state of despair through its despair-producing structures and systems in the first place, and 2) for abandoning the individual in his or her moment of anguish.

An honest evaluation of the failure of numerous world summits addressing grave ecological issues, can never hold God’s silence responsible for the ecological disasters of our times and of the future. “The steps world leaders have taken toward addressing the climate emergency that threatens the future of some of the world’s poorest communities are akin to pouring a glass of water on a raging house fire,” exclaims Tomás Insua.[24](https://laciviltacattolica-jnanadeepa.remoteauth.refread.com/the-silence-of-god/#_ftn24)

The silence of an invisible God may be a subjective experience of a sufferer, but the sufferer would not be deaf and blind if the visible community were to speak and act with empathy. Just as the communitarian Triune God is the model for human communitarian living, so does it make sense to say that such a communitarian God makes God’s Word heard in and through the community’s liberating and empowering words and deeds for the individual. God has deigned to speak through God’s humanity which in every way, except sin, is our own humanity.

_Conclusion_

The felt silence of God while suffering a crisis is a harrowing experience for any believer. It is true that no amount of explanation may lessen the anguish of the sufferer. However, by that fact it does not mean that such situations are totally meaningless. Every word God speaks is for a purpose and every moment of silence is equally purposeful. In our reflection we have unearthed multiple possibilities of making good sense of such situations. What we experience as God’s silence is how we perceive it; how at some point it looks and feels, but it is not how it is. We can experience God as absent or silent, even as “in him we live and move and have our being” (_Acts_ 17:28). By trusting we can affirm that all is not lost. Indeed, God’s silence needs to be heard in the community’s words and deeds of solidarity with anguished human beings.
