**Synodality through the Middle Ages to modern times:**

**1. Development and decline of synodal practices**

Synodal ways developed richly in the Middle Ages yet later declined in participatory breadth.

Monks of Cluny led revitalisation, cathedral chapters sustained it, and new religious communities—especially mendicant orders like Franciscans and Dominicans—kept communal discernment alive (para 33).

Local synods shifted toward royal or national forms under kingly presidency, moving away from purely ecclesial gatherings (para 33).

After Trent, diocesan and provincial synods focused on implementing norms rather than involving the whole People of God actively; cultural accents on hierarchy portrayed bishops and pope as the teaching Church (Ecclesia docens) distinct from the learning Church (Ecclesia discens) (para 35).

Example: Medieval councils included bishops, abbots, religious superiors, and even civil rulers, with the pope or legate presiding (para 32).

Decline showed in reduced lay and communal input at local levels.

**2. Centralization, reform movements, and challenges**

Central authority grew while reforms and crises posed challenges.

The Gregorian reform asserted papal primacy to free the Church from imperial control, yet risked weakening local Church identity (para 32).

The Western Schism (1378–1417) with multiple papal claimants led to the Council of Konstanz (1414–1418), which resolved the crisis but sparked conciliarism—later rejected as incompatible with Tradition (para 34).

The Council of Trent responded to the Protestant Reformation by mandating annual diocesan and triennial provincial synods (para 35).

Examples: Saint Charles Borromeo held eleven diocesan and five provincial synods in Milan; Saint Turibius of Mogrovejo convoked thirteen diocesan and three provincial in Lima (para 35).

Challenge: Post-Trent synods remained hierarchical and apologetic, limiting broader participation.

Vatican I (1869–1870) defined papal primacy and infallibility to ensure episcopal unity (para 37).

**3. Recovery of synodal practices before Vatican II**

From the nineteenth century, prophetic voices and new structures began recovering communal dimensions.

Thinkers like Johann Adam Möhler, Antonio Rosmini, and John Henry Newman urged return to Scripture and Tradition, stressing ecclesiology of communion and ordered synodality involving the sensus fidei of all faithful (para 38).

Episcopal conferences emerged mid-century as collegial gatherings in nations or regions, responding to modern contexts (para 39).

Example: Leo XIII convoked the Plenary Council of Latin America (1899), recognising the whole Church as subject of Tradition with active lay role (para 39).

**These steps prepared the fuller renewal of Vatican II.**

This period reveals adaptation amid centralisation and crises, with monastic revivals, conciliar responses, and nineteenth-century impulses preserving and rediscovering the synodal calling.

**SYNODALITY THROUGH THE MIDDLE AGES TO MODERN TIMES**

**INTRODUCTION**

**Setting the Context**

**Where We've Been:**

- **Early Church (1st-8th centuries)**: Synodality was the normal way the Church functioned
- Cyprian's formula: bishop, council, consent of people
- Clear structures at all levels

**Now We Face a Difficult Reality:**

- Beginning of **Second Millennium** (11th century onward)
- Synodality **gradually declined** in the Catholic Church
- Different developments in East and West

**Key Theme:** The document traces how synodality was **lost, maintained in some places, and eventually began to be recovered**.

**PART 1: THE BREAKDOWN - EAST AND WEST DIVIDE**

**A. The Great Schism of 1054**

**The Document States:**

“Since the beginning of the Second Millennium, synodal procedure gradually took on different forms in the East and in the West, particularly after the **breakdown of communion** between the Church of Constantinople and the Church of Rome (11th century).”

**What Happened:**

**1054 AD** - Mutual excommunications between:

- Patriarch Michael Cerularius of Constantinople
- Cardinal Humbert, representing Pope Leo IX

**Result**: Official separation between East and West

**Impact on Synodality:**

The two traditions developed **separately**:

- East maintained traditional synodal structures
- West developed increasingly centralized structures

**B. Additional Factors in the East**

**Political Changes:**

“When ecclesiastical territories belonging to the Patriarchates of Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem came under the **political control of Islam**.”

**Impact:**

- These ancient patriarchates weakened
- Couldn't function as before
- Constantinople and Rome became more dominant

**C. Eastern Churches - Continuity of Synodal Practice**

**The Document States:**

“In the Eastern Churches, synodal procedure continued to follow the **tradition of the Fathers**, particularly on the level of patriarchal and metropolitan Synods.”

**Types of Synods in the East:**

**1. Patriarchal Synods**

- Patriarch with bishops of his patriarchate
- Continued First Millennium tradition

**2. Metropolitan Synods**

- Metropolitan with bishops of his province
- Regional level

**3. Special Synods**

- “In which Patriarchs and Metropolitans took part”
- For broader questions

**The Permanent Synod (Σύνοδος ἐνδημοῦσα - Synodos Endemousa):**

**Definition**: A permanent synod (literally “synod of those dwelling [in the city]”)

**History:** “The activity of a permanent Synod... had also been known since the fourth century in Alexandria and Antioch.”

**In Constantinople:**

- Became more strongly established
- Regular assemblies
- Examined liturgical, canonical, and practical questions

**Different Forms:**

- Byzantine period (330-1453)
- After 1454 - Ottoman period

**Today**: “Permanent Synods live on today in the Orthodox Churches.”

**Key Point**: The East **maintained** synodal structures throughout the Second Millennium.

**PART 2: THE WEST - CENTRALIZATION AND DECLINE**

**A. The Gregorian Reform (11th Century)**

**Background:**

The reform movement associated with **Pope Gregory VII (1073-1085)**.

**The Document's Assessment:**

“In the Catholic Church, the Gregorian reform and the struggle for the _libertas Ecclesiae_ [freedom of the Church] contributed to the **affirmation of the Pope's authority as primate**.”

**The Two-Sided Effect:**

**Positive Side:** “On the one hand, this **freed Bishops from subordination to the Emperor**.”

**Before**: Secular rulers (emperors, kings) heavily controlled Church **After**: Church gained independence from secular power

**Negative Side:** “But, on the other hand, **if not properly understood, it ran the risk of weakening the identity of local Churches**.”

**The Problem:**

- Freed bishops from emperor
- But made them more dependent on pope
- Local churches' autonomy weakened
- Synodal structures declined

**B. The Roman Synod as Medieval Council Model**

**Evolution of the Roman Synod:**

**Since the 5th Century**: The Roman Synod functioned as the Bishop of Rome's Council.

**Who Attended:**

- Bishops of the Roman province
- Bishops present in Rome when it took place
- Presbyters and Deacons

**This Became the Model for Medieval Councils**

**Characteristics of Medieval Western Councils:**

The document describes a major change:

“The Pope or his Legate presided at these, but they were **not assemblies involving only Bishops and ecclesiastics**; they were also an **expression of western Christendom** which involved, in their various roles:”

**1. Ecclesiastical Authorities:**

- Bishops
- Abbots
- Superiors of Religious Orders

**2. Civil Authorities:**

- Representatives of the Emperor
- Representatives of Kings
- High dignitaries

**3. Experts:**

- Theological experts (_periti_)
- Canonical experts

**Key Change**: Councils became **political-ecclesiastical assemblies**, not purely Church synods.

**C. Decline of Local Church Synods**

**The Document's Observation:**

“On the level of local Churches, partly continuing the vast synodal procedure exercised in the Western Roman Empire introduced by Charlemagne, **Synods lost their simply ecclesiastical character** and took on the form of **royal or national Synods**, at which Bishops participated **under the presidency of the King**.”

**What This Means:**

**Before (Early Church):**

- Bishop convoked local synod
- Church matters decided by Church

**Now (Medieval Period):**

- King often presided
- Synods became state-church assemblies
- Bishops under royal authority
- Ecclesiastical independence compromised

**The Problem**: Synods no longer functioned as true Church assemblies but as instruments of royal control.

**D. Exceptions: Attempts at Revitalization**

**The Document Notes Positive Examples:**

“Throughout the Middle Ages, there were examples of **revitalisation** of Synodal procedure in the widest sense of the term.”

**Three Examples Given:**

**1. The Monks of Cluny**

- Cluniac reform movement (10th-12th centuries)
- Maintained communal discernment
- Preserved synodal spirit

**2. Cathedral Chapters**

- “Helped to keep synodal procedure alive”
- Communities of clergy advising bishops
- Elected bishops in many places

**3. The Mendicant Orders**

- “The new communities of religious life, particularly the Mendicant Orders”
- Franciscans, Dominicans (13th century)
- Maintained internal synodal structures
- Elected superiors
- Had provincial and general chapters

**Key Point**: While broader Church structures declined, these communities **preserved** synodal practices.

**PART 3: THE WESTERN SCHISM AND CONCILIARISM**

**A. The Western Schism (1378-1417)**

**The Crisis:**

“At the end of the Middle Ages, a unique situation came about in the **Western Schism** (1378-1417), when there were simultaneously **two, and later three, people claiming the title of Pope**.”

**What Happened:**

**1378**: After Pope Gregory XI's death:

- Cardinals elected Urban VI
- Same cardinals later declared election invalid
- Elected Clement VII
- Result: Two popes!

**1409**: Council of Pisa tried to solve it:

- Elected a third pope (Alexander V)
- Now **three** claimants!

**The Chaos**: Nobody knew who the legitimate pope was.

**B. The Council of Konstanz (1414-1418)**

**The Solution:**

The document describes how the crisis was resolved:

“The **Council of Konstanz** (1414-1418) solved this intricate question by applying **emergency canon law** foreseen in medieval canonical thinking, and went on to elect the legitimate Pope.”

**What the Council Did:**

1. **Deposed** or obtained resignations of all claimants
2. **Elected** Martin V as undisputed pope (1417)
3. **Ended** the Western Schism

**The Legal Basis**: “Emergency canon law” - extraordinary measures for extraordinary crisis.

**C. The Rise of Conciliarism**

**Definition**: The theory that an ecumenical council has authority **over** the pope.

**The Document States:**

“In this situation, however, the **conciliarist idea** developed, whose aim was to impose a **permanent council over and above the primatial authority of the Pope**.”

**What Conciliarists Wanted:**

- Councils to meet regularly
- Councils to have supreme authority
- Pope subordinate to councils
- Permanent check on papal power

**The Church's Judgment:**

“The theological justification and practical application of conciliarism would be judged **not to be in conformity with Tradition**.”

**Why It Was Rejected:**

While councils have great authority, conciliarism went too far:

- Made councils superior to pope
- Contradicted papal primacy
- Not consistent with tradition

**D. The Lesson of the Western Schism**

**Despite Rejecting Conciliarism, There's a Lesson:**

The document states:

“It does, however, leave a lesson for Church history: there is always a **danger of schism** lying in wait, which cannot be shrugged off, and the continual **reform of the Church _in capite et membris_** [in head and members] cannot happen without making **correct use of the synodal procedure** that follows in the wake of Tradition and looks to the **primatial authority of the Pope as its own guarantee**.”

**The Lesson:**

**Avoiding Future Schisms Requires:**

1. Reform of whole Church (head and members)
2. Correct use of synodal procedure
3. Following Tradition
4. With papal primacy as guarantee

**Balance Needed**: Neither papal absolutism nor conciliarism, but proper synodality with papal primacy.

**PART 4: THE COUNCIL OF TRENT AND ITS AFTERMATH**

**A. The Protestant Reformation Crisis (16th Century)**

**The Context:**

- Martin Luther (1517) and Protestant Reformation
- Church split in Western Europe
- Need for Catholic reform and response

**B. The Council of Trent (1545-1563)**

**Convocation and Nature:**

The document describes Trent's characteristics:

“A century later, in response to the crisis triggered off by the Protestant Reformation, the Catholic Church held the **Council of Trent**. It is the **first Council in modern times** with certain characteristics:”

**New Characteristics:**

**1. “No longer a Council of Christendom as in the Middle Ages”**

- Not mixing church and state
- More purely ecclesiastical

**2. Participants:**

- “Bishops as well as Superiors of religious Orders and monastic Congregations”
- More clerical, less political

**3. Civil Representatives:**

- “While the legates of Princes who attend do not have the right to vote”
- Civil authorities present but not voting
- Church decisions made by Church

**Significance**: Trent was more purely ecclesiastical than medieval councils.

**C. Trent's Mandate for Synods**

**The Decree:**

“The Council established the norm that **diocesan Synods should take place annually**, **provincial Synods every three years**, as a way of passing on the impetus of Trent's reforms to the whole Church.”

**Purpose**: Regular synods to implement Tridentine reforms.

**D. Model Implementations: Two Saints**

**The Document Provides Two Examples:**

**1. Saint Charles Borromeo - Archbishop of Milan**

“An example and model of this is to be found in what **Saint Charles Borromeo** did as Archbishop of Milan.”

**His Record:**

- “In his long ministry, he convoked **five provincial Synods**”
- And “**eleven diocesan ones**”

**Significance**: Exemplary implementation of Trent's directive.

**2. Saint Turibius of Mogrovejo - Archbishop of Lima**

“In America, **Saint Turibius of Mogrovejo** undertook something similar:”

**His Record:**

- “He convoked **three provincial Synods**”
- And “**thirteen diocesan ones**”

**Additional Example:** “There were also **three provincial Councils in Mexico** in the same century.”

**Key Point**: Some bishops faithfully implemented Trent's synodal mandates, especially in reform-minded dioceses.

**E. The Problem: Post-Tridentine Synodal Practice**

**Despite the Mandate, a Problem Developed:**

The document makes a crucial observation:

“In keeping with the **culture of the time**, the diocesan and provincial Synods celebrated following the Council of Trent were **not meant to involve the active participation of the whole People of God** - the _congregatio fidelium_ - but to **pass on and enact** the Council's norms and dispositions.”

**What This Means:**

**Before Trent (Early Church):**

- Whole community participated in local synods
- Real discernment and decision-making
- Cyprian's principle: consent of the people

**After Trent:**

- Synods became transmission mechanisms
- Top-down communication
- Little real participation of laity
- Implementing decisions made elsewhere

**The Culture:**

Post-Trent synods reflected the culture of the time:

- Hierarchical society
- Authority from above
- Passive reception below

**F. The Apologetic Reaction**

**Why This Happened:**

“The **apologetic reaction** to the Protestant Reformation's criticism of ecclesiastical authority and to similar responses in a number of modern schools of thought has **accentuated the hierarchological vision** of the Church.”

**What “Apologetic Reaction” Means:**

In defending against Protestant attacks on Church authority:

- Catholic theology emphasized hierarchy
- Stressed distinction between clergy and laity
- Reinforced clerical authority
- Defensive posture

**The Result:**

“To the point of Seeing Bishops, and above them the Pope, as the **_Ecclesia docens_** [teaching Church], with the rest of the People of God as the **_Ecclesia discens_** [learning Church].”

**This Division:**

- Clergy = those who teach
- Laity = those who learn
- Sharp separation
- Passive role for laity
- Loss of synodal participation

**The Vision That Emerged:**

Church as **_societas perfecta et inaequalium_** - “a perfect society of unequals”

**Contrast with Early Church:**

- Early Church: All are _synodoi_ (companions)
- Post-Trent: Sharp division between clergy and laity

**PART 5: PROTESTANT AND ANGLICAN SYNODAL PRACTICES**

**A. Introduction**

**The Document Notes:**

“The ecclesial communities born of the Protestant Reformation promote a certain kind of synodal approach, in the context of an **ecclesiology and a sacramental and ministerial doctrine and practice which depart from Catholic Tradition**.”

**Important Qualification**: They practice synodality, but based on different theological foundations.

**B. Lutheran Tradition**

**Theological Basis:**

The document describes Lutheran synodal government as rooted in:

“The **common priesthood involved in baptism**” - all baptized share priesthood equally.

**Structure:**

“The synodal government of the ecclesial community, in which a **certain number of the faithful take part** by reason of the common priesthood involved in baptism, is held to be the structure that is most in tune with the life of the Christian community according to the Lutheran confession.”

**Participation:**

“**All the faithful** are called to take part in:

- The **election of ministers**
- Ensuring **fidelity to the teaching of the Gospel**
- Ensuring **ecclesiastical order**”

**Historical Development:**

“This prerogative is generally exercised by **civil rulers**, which has led in the past to a regime very **closely linked to the State**.”

**Problem**: In practice, often led to state control of church (especially in Germany and Scandinavia).

**C. Reformed (Calvinist) Tradition**

**Theological Foundation:**

Based on “**Jean Calvin's doctrine of the four ministries**”:

**The Four Ministries:**

1. **Pastors** - preach and administer sacraments
2. **Teachers** - instruct in doctrine
3. **Presbyters** (Elders) - exercise discipline
4. **Deacons** - care for the poor

**Role of Presbyters:**

“The presbyter represents the **dignity and powers** conferred on all the faithful with baptism.”

**Governance:**

“Presbyters, along with Pastors, are thus **responsible for the local community**.”

**Synodal Structure:**

“While synodal procedure foresees the presence in the assembly of teachers and the other ministries, with a **majority of lay faithful**.”

**Key Difference from Catholic**: Majority lay governance, not clerical.

**D. Anglican Communion**

**Formula:**

The document quotes: “She is **synodically governed, but episcopally led**”

**What This Means:**

**Not Just Division of Powers:**

“Not meant simply to indicate a division between:

- The **legislative power** (which belongs to Synods, in which all sectors of the People of God take part)
- And the **executive power** (specific to Bishops)”

**But Rather Synergy:**

“But rather the **synergy** between:

- The **charism and personal authority of the Bishop**, on the one hand
- And, on the other, the **gift of the Holy Spirit poured out on the whole community**”

**Synodal Features:**

“Synods have always been part of the life of the Anglican Communion on all levels:

- **Local**
- **National**
- **International**”

**Comprehensive Participation:**

In Anglican synods: “All sectors of the People of God take part” - bishops, clergy, and laity represented.

**E. Catholic Assessment**

**What Can Be Learned:**

While their theological foundations differ, these traditions:

- Maintained synodal practices
- Valued participation of all baptized
- Developed consultation structures
- Can contribute to ecumenical dialogue

**What Differs:**

- Understanding of ordained ministry
- Sacramental theology
- Role of bishops
- Relationship to apostolic succession

**PART 6: VATICAN COUNCIL I (1869-1870) (8 minutes)**

**A. The Context**

**19th Century Challenges:**

- Rise of nationalism
- Liberal revolutions
- Attacks on Church authority
- Modernist philosophy
- Loss of Papal States

**B. Vatican I's Teaching**

**The Document States:**

“**Vatican Council I** (1869-1870) endorsed the doctrine of the **primacy** and **infallibility** of the Pope.”

**The Two Definitions:**

**1. Papal Primacy:**

“The primacy of the Bishop of Rome, for whom '**in blessed Peter is established the principle and the perpetual and visible foundation of the unity of faith and communion**'.”

**How It's Presented:**

“Presented by the Council as the **ministry** set to guarantee the **unity and indivisibility of the episcopate** at the service of the faith of the People of God.”

**Key Point**: Primacy is a **ministry** (service), not domination.

**2. Papal Infallibility:**

**The Formula**: _Ex cathedra_ definitions are irreformable “**in themselves and not in virtue of the consensus of the Church**” (_ex sese, non autem ex consensu Ecclesiae_).

**C. Common Misunderstanding**

**What the Formula Does NOT Mean:**

The document clarifies:

This formula “**does not make the _consensus Ecclesiae_ superfluous**”

**Rather**: “It affirms the **exercise of authority** which belongs to the Pope **by virtue of his specific ministry**.”

**The Correct Understanding:**

The pope's infallible teaching:

- Is not separate from the Church
- Doesn't bypass the Church
- But exercises authority proper to his office

**D. Evidence: Marian Dogmas**

**Two Examples Show Consultation Happened:**

**1. Immaculate Conception (1854):**

“This is borne out by **consultation carried out through the Bishops** among the whole People of God, which is what **Blessed Pius IX wanted** in view of the definition of the dogma of the Immaculate Conception.”

**Before Defining**: Pius IX consulted bishops worldwide about the faith of their people.

**2. Assumption of Mary (1950):**

“The approach taken by **Pius XII** in regard to the definition of the dogma of the Assumption of Mary.”

**Again**: Consultation of bishops about the faith of the faithful.

**The Lesson:**

Even when exercising supreme authority:

- Popes consulted the Church
- Through the bishops
- About the faith of the People of God
- This is synodal practice!

**E. The Risk of Misunderstanding**

**The Document Acknowledges:**

Vatican I's definitions, “**if not properly understood**,” could:

- Weaken local church identity
- Lead to excessive centralization
- Reduce synodality
- Make consultation seem unnecessary

**But Properly Understood:**

- Papal primacy serves communion
- Guarantees unity of episcopate
- Doesn't eliminate synodal structures
- Should be exercised synodally

**PART 7: 19TH CENTURY PROPHETIC VOICES**

**A. The Growing Awareness**

**Before Vatican II, Seeds Were Planted:**

“The need for a pertinent and consistent re-launch of Synodal practice in the Catholic Church became clear as early as the **nineteenth century**.”

**B. Three Prophetic Writers**

**The Document Names Three Key Figures:**

**1. Johann Adam Möhler (1796-1838)**

- German Catholic theologian
- Tübingen School

**2. Antonio Rosmini (1797-1855)**

- Italian priest and philosopher
- Founded religious order

**3. John Henry Newman (1801-1890)**

- English cardinal
- Convert from Anglicanism
- Major theologian

**What They Did:**

These “**prophetic writers**”:

- “Returned to the **normative sources** of Scripture and Tradition”
- “Heralding the **providential renewal** that came with the biblical, liturgical and patristic movements”

**The Movements They Inspired:**

- **Biblical movement** - return to Scripture
- **Liturgical movement** - renewal of worship
- **Patristic movement** - study of Church Fathers

**C. Their Key Insights**

**What They Stressed:**

“They stressed that a **primary and fundamental element** in the life of the Church is the **dimension of communion**, which implies:

1. An **ordered synodal practice** on every level
2. Giving due importance to the **_sensus fidei fidelium_** [sense of faith of the faithful]
3. **Intrinsically related** to the specific ministry of the Bishops and the Pope”

**The Recovery They Called For:**

- **Communion** as primary ecclesiology (not just hierarchy)
- **_Sensus fidei_** - all faithful have sense of faith
- **Synodal practice** ordered at every level
- Proper relationship between people, bishops, and pope

**D. Contextual Factors Requiring Response**

**The Document Identifies Two New Factors:**

**1. Ecumenical Relationships:**

“The emergence of a new climate in **ecumenical relationships** with other Churches and ecclesial communities”

**Need**: Better experience and presentation of Church mystery.

**2. Modern Consciousness:**

“A more careful discernment of the advanced demands of **modern consciousness** concerning the **participation of every citizen** in running society”

**Challenge**: Church must respond to democratic expectations without losing her identity.

**The Call:**

These factors “call for a **new and deeper experience and presentation** of the mystery of the Church as **intrinsically synodal**.”

**PART 8: LATE 19TH AND EARLY 20TH CENTURY DEVELOPMENTS**

**A. Episcopal Conferences Emerge**

**A New Institution:**

“From the second half of the **nineteenth century** onward, a new institution came into being and acquired a certain significance: **episcopal conferences**.”

**Initial Status:**

“May not have had a **precise canonical profile**”

**But They Were:**

“A **gathering of the Bishops of a single country**.”

**Significance:**

“A sign of a **rediscovery of a collegial interpretation** of the exercise of the episcopal ministry in a **specific territory** and in view of changing **geopolitical circumstances**.”

**What This Means:**

Bishops began meeting regularly as conferences:

- To discuss common issues
- To coordinate pastoral action
- To speak with one voice
- Rediscovery of collegiality

**B. Plenary Latin American Council (1899)**

**Pope Leo XIII's Initiative:**

“On the eve of the twentieth century, **Leo XIII** convoked a **plenary Latin American Council**, which brought together the **Metropolitans** of the ecclesiastical province of the continent (1899).”

**Significance:**

- Continental level gathering
- Showed possibility of regional synodality
- Prepared for future developments

**C. Growing Theological Awareness**

**The Document States:**

“In terms of **theology and ecclesiological experience**, there was a growing awareness that:”

**Three Key Insights:**

**1. “The Church is not identical with her pastors”**

**Meaning**: Church = whole People of God, not just hierarchy

**2. “The whole Church, by the action of the Holy Spirit, is the subject or 'organ' of Tradition”**

**Meaning**: Whole Church (not just hierarchy) transmits and interprets Tradition

**3. “Lay people have an active role in the transmission of the apostolic faith”**

**Meaning**: Laity not passive but active in handing on faith

**The Shift:**

From seeing Church as = hierarchy

To seeing Church as = whole People of God with hierarchy serving within it

**PART 9: THE SECOND VATICAN COUNCIL (1962-1965) - THE GREAT RECOVERY**

**A. Vatican II's Program**

**The Document States:**

“The **Second Ecumenical Vatican Council** continued along the lines of Vatican I and made it part of a programme of **_aggiornamento_** [updating].”

**What Aggiornamento Meant:**

Not changing doctrine but:

- Taking account of modern developments
- Incorporating insights from renewal movements
- Including gains of intervening years
- “Rich synthesis in the light of Tradition”

**B. The Dogmatic Constitution Lumen Gentium**

**The Foundational Document:**

“The dogmatic Constitution **_Lumen Gentium_** sets out a **vision of the nature and mission of the Church as communion**.”

**With the Theological Presuppositions:**

For “a suitable re-launch of synodality”:

**1. Mystical and Sacramental Conception**

- Church as mystery and sacrament
- Not just organization

**2. Nature as People of God**

- Pilgrimage through history
- Toward heavenly homeland

**3. Common Dignity and Mission**

- “All her members are by virtue of baptism honoured with the **same dignity** as children of God”
- “Appointed to the **same mission**”

**4. Variety and Richness**

- “Variety and ordered richness of their charisms, their vocations and their ministries”

**5. Sacramentality of Episcopate**

- Bishops ordained, not just appointed
- Sacramental basis for authority

**6. Collegiality**

- “Collegiality in **hierarchical communion** with the Bishop of Rome”

**C. The Revolutionary Order of Chapters**

**The Document Emphasizes This Crucial Point:**

“The order of its first chapters expresses an **important  forward** in the way the Church understands herself.”

**The Sequence:**

**Chapter 1**: The Mystery of the Church

**Chapter 2**: The People of God

**Chapter 3**: The Hierarchical Constitution of the Church

**Why This Order Matters:**

The document explains:

“Stresses that the **ecclesiastical hierarchy is at the service of the People of God** in order that the Church may carry out her mission in conformity with God's plan of salvation, in the logic of:

- The **priority of the whole over its parts**
- The **priority of the end over the means**”

**The Revolutionary Point:**

**Old View**:

- Hierarchy first
- People defined in relation to hierarchy
- Hierarchy = active, people = passive

**New View**:

- People of God first (Chapter 2)
- Hierarchy second (Chapter 3)
- Hierarchy exists to serve the People
- All have dignity and mission

**This Changes Everything!**

**D. Christus Dominus - Decree on Bishops**

**Key Teachings:**

**1. Local Church as Subject:**

“The decree **_Christus Dominus_** emphasises that the **local Church is a subject**.”

**Meaning**: Each diocese is not just an administrative unit but a true subject—a real Church.

**2. Bishops to Exercise Pastoral Care in Communion:**

“Encourages Bishops to exercise the pastoral care of the Church entrusted to them **in communion with their presbyterate**.”

**3. Senate/Council of Presbyters:**

“Taking advantage of the help of a specific **senate or council of presbyters**.”

**4. Pastoral Council:**

“Formulating the invitation to establish a **pastoral Council** in every Diocese, in which **presbyters, religious and lay people** should take part.”

**Key Point**: Laity to participate in pastoral councils!

**5. Re-invigorate Synods and Councils:**

“The wish that, on the level of communion between Churches in a region, the venerable institution of **Synods and Councils should be re-invigorated**.”

**6. Episcopal Conferences:**

“Invites the promotion of **Episcopal Conferences**.”

**E. Orientalium Ecclesiarum - Decree on Eastern Catholic Churches**

**Recognition of Eastern Traditions:**

“In the Decree **_Orientalium Ecclesiarum_** the institution of the **Patriarchate** and its **synodal form** are put forward in relation to Eastern Catholic Churches.”

**Significance**: Vatican II recognized and valued the synodal structures preserved in Eastern Churches.

**F. Paul VI Institutes the Synod of Bishops (1965)**

**The Major Structural Innovation:**

“In terms of re-vitalising synodal practice on the level of the universal Church, **Blessed Paul VI instituted the Synod of Bishops**.”

**Its Definition:**

“A '**permanent Council of Bishops for the universal Church**', directly and immediately subject to the power of the Pope.”

**Its Function:**

“'**Providing information and offering advice**', which '**can also enjoy the power of making decisions** when such power is conferred upon it by the Roman Pontiff'.”

**The Purpose:**

“This institution aims to **continue to extend to the People of God the benefits of communion lived during the Council**.”

**Key Point**: Keep the conciliar spirit alive through regular synods.

**PART 10: FROM VATICAN II TO PRESENT - ONGOING JOURNEY**

**A. John Paul II's Assessment (Year 2000)**

**On the Jubilee of the Year 2000:**

Saint John Paul II “offered an **assessment** of the path that had been travelled to incarnate - in conformity with the teaching of Vatican II - the very essence of the mystery of the Church through the various **structures of communion**.”

**His Judgment:**

The document quotes him: “**Much has been done**”

**But Also:**

“'But there is certainly **much more to be done**, in order to realise all the potential of these **instruments of communion**... (and) to **respond promptly and effectively to the issues** which the Church must face in these **rapidly changing times**.'“

**The Assessment:**

**Progress Made:**

- Structures created
- Some experience gained
- Awareness grown

**Work Remaining:**

- Full potential not realized
- Need better response to challenges
- Rapid changes require adaptation

**B. Concrete Developments Since Vatican II**

**The Document Lists Achievements:**

“In the more than **fifty years** that have passed since the last Council until today:”

**1. Growth in Awareness:**

“The **awareness of the Church as communion** has grown in broad sectors of the People of God.”

**2. Positive Experiences:**

“There have been **positive experiences of synodality** at diocesan, regional and universal levels.”

**3. Specific Achievements:**

“In particular, there have been:

- **Fourteen general ordinary assemblies** of the Synod of Bishops
- The **experience and activity of Episcopal Conferences** have developed
- Everywhere there have been **synodal assemblies**
- **Councils** have also come into being which foster **communion and co-operation** between local Churches and Bishops to develop pastoral approaches on the level of regions and continents”

**C. The Ongoing Challenge**

**Despite Progress:**

The document acknowledges earlier: “There is still a long way to go... the drive to find appropriate form for a synodal Church... seems to be in need of **clear theological principles** and **decisive pastoral orientations**.”

**Why the Document Was Written (2018):**

To provide:

1. Theological clarity
2. Pastoral orientations
3. Spiritual foundations
4. Practical guidance

**D. Pope Francis's Decisive Push**

**The 50th Anniversary Address (2015):**

Pope Francis at the 50th anniversary of the Synod of Bishops gave programmatic direction.

**Key Statements:**

**1. God's Expectation:**

“It is precisely this **path of synodality** which **God expects** of the Church of the third millennium.”

**2. Essential Dimension:**

Synodality is “an **essential dimension** of the Church.”

**Already Present:**

“What the Lord is asking of us is already in some sense **present in the very word 'synod'**.”

**4. The Inverted Pyramid:**

His image of the Church: “In this Church, as in an **inverted pyramid**, the top is located **below the base**.”

**5. Connected to the Base:**

“Only to the extent that these organizations **keep connected to the 'base'** and start from people and their daily problems, can a synodal Church begin to take shape.”

**E. The 2021-2024 Synod on Synodality**

**Though Not in the 2018 Document:**

Pope Francis called for a **universal Synod on Synodality itself** (2021-2024):

- Unprecedented consultation of entire Church
- Three phases: diocesan, continental, universal
- Topic: synodality itself
- Practice synodality while studying it

**SYNTHESIS AND SUMMARY**

**The Arc of History - Three Phases**

**PHASE 1: DECLINE (11th-18th Centuries)**

**What Was Lost:**

- Active participation of whole People of God
- Regular functioning of local and provincial synods
- Balance between papal primacy and synodal practice
- Communion ecclesiology

**Why It Happened:**

- East-West schism
- Gregorian reform (overcorrection)
- Royal/national synods replacing church synods
- Protestant Reformation (apologetic reaction)
- Emphasis on hierarchy over communion

**Result:**

- Church seen as _societas perfecta et inaequalium_
- _Ecclesia docens_ vs. _Ecclesia discens_
- Synods became transmission mechanisms
- Laity passive

**Bright Spots:**

- Eastern Churches maintained synodal structures
- Some religious orders (Cluny, Mendicants)
- Cathedral chapters
- Some exemplary bishops (Borromeo, Turibius)

**PHASE 2: SEEDS OF RECOVERY (19th-early 20th Centuries)**

**Prophetic Voices:**

- Möhler, Rosmini, Newman
- Return to Scripture, Fathers, Liturgy
- Rediscovery of communion

**Structural Developments:**

- Episcopal conferences begin
- Latin American Council (1899)
- Growing lay involvement

**Theological Awareness:**

- Church ≠ hierarchy alone
- Whole Church is subject of Tradition
- Laity have active role

**Challenges:**

- Vatican I definitions misunderstood
- Risk of excessive centralization
- Need to balance primacy and synodality

**PHASE 3: VATICAN II AND RECOVERY (1962-present)**

**Vatican II's Achievement:**

**Theological:**

- Communion ecclesiology
- People of God primary
- Hierarchy at service
- All baptized have dignity and mission
- Sacramentality of episcopate
- Collegiality

**Structural:**

- Synod of Bishops (1965)
- Episcopal conferences promoted
- Pastoral councils mandated
- Diocesan and parish councils
- Priests' councils

**Order of Lumen Gentium:**

- Mystery → People of God → Hierarchy
- Revolutionary reordering

**Since Vatican II:**

**Progress:**

- 14 Synods of Bishops
- Episcopal conferences functioning
- Many diocesan synods
- Growing awareness of communion

**Ongoing Work:**

- Full potential not realized
- Need better theological clarity (2018 document)
- Need decisive pastoral orientations
- Formation required
- Conversion from clericalism needed

**Francis's Push:**

- “Path God expects”
- Essential dimension
- Inverted pyramid
- Connected to the base
- Synod on Synodality (2021-2024)

**Key Lessons from History**

**1. SYNODALITY CAN BE LOST**

Not automatic—requires:

- Vigilance
- Formation
- Structures
- Conversion

**2. MULTIPLE FACTORS CAUSED DECLINE**

- Political (royal control)
- Theological (apologetic reaction)
- Cultural (hierarchical society)
- Ecclesiological (loss of communion vision)

**3. RECOVERY IS GRADUAL**

- Prophetic voices first
- Theological renewal
- Structural changes
- Cultural shift needed

**4. STRUCTURES MATTER**

Without structures:

- Spirit alone not enough
- Good intentions insufficient
- Slide back to clericalism

**5. BOTH/AND NOT EITHER/OR**

Need:

- Papal primacy AND synodality
- Hierarchy AND People of God
- Authority AND participation
- Unity AND diversity

**6. TRADITION GUIDES RECOVERY**

Return to:

- Scripture (Acts 15)
- Fathers (Ignatius, Cyprian)
- First Millennium practice
- Not innovation but recovery

**7. CONVERSION REQUIRED**

Structural change insufficient:

- Mentality must change
- Clericalism must die
- Formation essential
- Spirituality of communion needed

**TEACHING POINTS FOR STUDENTS**

**Critical Understanding**

**1. THE STORY IS COMPLEX**

Not simple: “we had it, we lost it, now we're getting it back”

Rather: gradual decline, multiple causes, slow recovery, ongoing struggle

**2. EAST AND WEST DIVERGED**

- East maintained more synodal structures
- West became more centralized
- Both traditions have gifts to offer
- Ecumenical dialogue important

**3. PROTESTANTISM'S CHALLENGE**

- Led to apologetic overreaction
- But Protestant/Anglican synodal practices also offer insights
- Ecumenical learning possible while maintaining Catholic doctrine

**4. TRENT'S AMBIGUITY**

- Mandated synods (good)
- But synods became top-down (problem)
- Shows structure insufficient without right spirit

**5. VATICAN I PROPERLY UNDERSTOOD**

- Papal primacy necessary
- But not opposed to synodality
- Popes consulted Church (Marian dogmas)
- Balance needed

**6. VATICAN II'S REVOLUTION**

- Not creating something new
- Recovering something old
- But expressing it in new way
- People of God before hierarchy

**7. THE WORK CONTINUES**

- 50+ years since Vatican II
- Much done, much remains
- Need ongoing conversion
- Structural and spiritual renewal

**CONCLUSION**

The history from the Middle Ages to modern times shows:

**A Sobering Reality**: What the early Church practiced normally can be lost gradually through:

- Political pressures
- Theological overreactions
- Cultural influences
- Loss of vision

**A Hopeful Reality**: What was lost can be recovered through:

- Return to sources (Scripture, Fathers)
- Theological renewal
- Structural reform
- Spiritual conversion

**The Current Moment**: We are in Phase 3 - recovery and implementation:

- Vatican II provided vision
- Structures created
- Experience gained
- But work continues

**The Challenge**: To fully realize synodality requires:

- Clear theological principles (2018 ITC document provides)
- Decisive pastoral orientations (being developed)
- Formation of all (clergy and laity)
- Conversion from clericalism
- Spirituality of communion
- Patience and perseverance

**The Promise**: God expects this path of the Church in the third millennium.
