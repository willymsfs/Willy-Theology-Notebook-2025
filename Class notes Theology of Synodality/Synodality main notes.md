Synodality is described as an **“essential dimension”** of the Church and its specific **“modus vivendi et operandi”** (way of living and operating). Drawing from the Greek roots _συν_ (with) and _όδός_ (path), the term indicates the **path along which the People of God walk together**. It is not merely a bureaucratic process but a theological reality where the Church reveals itself as a pilgrim assembly convoked by the Risen Lord.

**1. Conceptual and Etymological Foundations**

The word “synod” draws on the deepest themes of Revelation. The sources provide several historical and scriptural examples to illustrate its meaning:

• **Followers of the Way:** In the New Testament, Jesus presents Himself as “the way, the truth and the life,” and his original followers were called **“followers of the Way”**.

• **The Church as a Choir:** Saint John Chrysostom famously wrote that the Church is a “name standing for ‘walking together’”. He illustrated this by comparing the Church to a **choir**—a harmonic reality where members converge in a “common mind” (_homonoia_) through reciprocal and ordered relations.

• **The Emmaus Icon:** The account of the disciples at Emmaus serves as a **“living icon”** of the Church, where the Risen Lord acts as a fellow traveler who illuminates the path with His Word and feeds the community with the Bread of Life.

**2. The Dynamic of “All, Some, and One”**

To prevent synodality from being misunderstood as mere parliamentarianism, the sources articulate it through the relationship between the whole community and its leaders. This is structured around three subjects:

• **The “All” (Universitas fidelium):** Refers to the entire body of the faithful who, through the _sensus fidei_ (instinct of faith), are all agents of evangelisation.

• **The “Some” (College of Bishops):** Refers to the ministry of leadership exercised by bishops in communion with their priests.

• **The “One” (Bishop of Rome):** Refers to the primatial ministry of unity exercised by the Successor of Peter.

This dynamic ensures a **circular relationship** where the “all” are consulted, the “some” provide leadership and discernment, and the “one” guarantees the unity of the whole.

**3. Key Illustrations of Synodal Authority**

The sources offer powerful metaphors to explain how authority functions within a synodal framework:

• **The Inverted Pyramid:** Pope Francis describes the synodal Church as an **“inverted pyramid”**. In this model, the **summit is located below the base**, meaning that the hierarchical ministry exists solely to serve the People of God. Those who exercise authority are called “ministers” because, in the original meaning of the word, they are the “least of all”.

• **The Council of Jerusalem:** This New Testament event (Acts 15) is the **paradigm for all Synods**. It demonstrates a community facing a controversial challenge (the mission to the Gentiles) and resolving it through a process of “lively and open discussion,” listening to the Holy Spirit, and reaching a consensus that involves the “whole Church”.

**4. The Goal: Missionary Conversion**

Ultimately, the “need” for synodality arises from a desire for **missionary conversion**. It is intended to quash paradigms that concentrate the responsibility for mission exclusively in the hands of the clergy. By recognising that every baptised person is a **“missionary disciple,”** the Church activates the variety of charisms given by the Spirit to build up the Body of Christ and proclaim the Gospel to the world.

**Analogy for Understanding**

To visualise the **Introduction to Synodality**, consider the functioning of a **mountain climbing expedition**.

• **Synodality** is the “walking together” of the entire team (the All).

• The **guides** (the Some) provide technical expertise and ensure the path is safe, but they cannot reach the summit without the active participation of every climber.

• The **lead climber** (the One) ensures the rope remains connected and the team stays unified. If the guides try to climb alone, the mission fails; if the climbers ignore the guides, they lose the way. The success of the journey depends on the **mutual trust and shared pace** of everyone on the mountain.

According to the sources, the **Old Testament** provides the primary narrative and theological framework for synodality, establishing the Church’s identity as a **People of God on a journey** towards unity and communion.

**I. People of God on a Journey**

The sources define “synod” etymologically as the “path along which the People of God walk together”. This journey is a central motif in the Old Testament:

• **The Original Vocation:** God created humanity as social beings intended to work with Him in communion. Though sin fractured these relationships, God initiated a plan of salvation to bring the “scattered back to the path of unity”.

• **The Exodus Journey:** The journey of the people freed from slavery is the foundational experience of this synodal vocation,. In the desert, God ordered the tribes of Israel, giving each its place while remaining their only guide and shepherd.

• **The New Exodus:** The Prophets later promised a “new Exodus,” a journey that would open out to universal horizons and lead to a new covenant.

**II. Covenantal Assemblies and Communal Discernment**

The sources highlight the Hebrew term **_qahal_** (assembly)—translated into Greek as **_ekklesia_**—as the first form of the People’s synodal vocation.

• **Convocation and Ratification:** This assembly was ratified in the **Sinai covenant**, where the People were made “important and worthy to speak to God”. The assembly acted as the **partner convoked by the Lord** every time He renewed His covenant.

• **Inclusivity of the Assembly:** This synodal “partner” was remarkably inclusive, consisting of not only men but also **women, children, and even foreigners**.

• **Ordered Participation:** While the Lord guided the assembly through Moses, authority was exercised in a **subordinate and “collegial” way** involving Judges, Elders, and Levites. This demonstrates that the People’s movement was “orderly and well thought out,” with specific roles for different members.

**III. Prophetic Listening and Obedience**

The message of the Prophets was essential in teaching the People how to maintain their synodal identity through the “hardships of history”.

• **Conversion and Justice:** The Prophets invited the People to a **conversion of heart** and to practice justice, specifically toward the poorest, the oppressed, and foreigners. This serves as a tangible witness to God’s mercy and is a requirement for remaining faithful to the covenant journey.

• **Internalisation of the Covenant:** The Prophets heralded a transition from a law engraved on stone tablets to a **new covenant engraved on hearts**.

• **Outpouring of the Spirit:** This prophetic tradition points toward an eschatological fulfilment where the **Spirit of the Lord is poured out on all members** of His People, enabling a deeper level of obedience and communal listening to God’s will.

The Old Testament thus establishes synodality as the “guarantee and incarnation” of God’s plan to lead all of humanity into a single, harmonic reality—much like a **choir**—converging in a common mind and love.

  

In the **New Testament**, synodality is presented as a fundamental way of being for the People of God, rooted in the life of Jesus and the guidance of the Holy Spirit in the early Church. The following points detail how the sources describe this biblical foundation.

**Jesus Forming a Community of Disciples**

The term “synod” itself draws on the deepest themes of Revelation, meaning “walking together” along a path. In the New Testament, Jesus is revealed as **“the way, the truth and the life”** (Jn 14:6), and his early followers were appropriately called “followers of the Way”.

• **Communion with the Father:** Jesus never acts alone but always does the will of the Father, who dwells in Him and carries out His work. This **Trinitarian communion** is the source and model for the Church’s synodal life.

• **The Pilgrim Image:** Jesus is depicted as a **pilgrim** who proclaims the Good News, points the way to the Kingdom, and shares the truth and love of communion through the Holy Spirit.

• **The Emmaus Icon:** The account of the disciples on the road to Emmaus is described as a **living icon of the Church**, where the Risen Lord guides, illuminates, and feeds the community with His Word and the Bread of Life.

• **Authority and Charisms:** Jesus conferred **authority** (_exousia_) on the Apostles to teach and baptise, but by virtue of baptism, **every member** of the People of God receives a share in this authority through the “anointing of the Holy Spirit”. The Spirit distributes various charisms for the upbuilding of the one Body of Christ, requiring mutual submission and service.

**The Early Christian Community**

Following the outpouring of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost, the early Church lived out its vocation through communal discernment.

• **The Guide:** The **Holy Spirit** is the leading figure who guides the way and gives direction to the Apostolic Church.

• **Communal Responsibility:** Disciples were tasked with listening to the Spirit’s voice to discern the path forward. The sources cite the choice of the “seven men of good reputation” in Acts 6 and the mission to the Gentiles in Acts 10 as key examples of this community-wide discernment process.

• **Shared Life:** The life of the early community (Acts 2–4) is characterised by an ordered movement where members, despite varied roles, are equally responsible for the mission and life of the community.

**The Council of Jerusalem as a Synodal Model**

The **Council of Jerusalem (Acts 15)** is identified as the **paradigm for Synods** celebrated by the Church across the centuries. It demonstrates how the Church resolves controversial questions through orderly, communal discernment.

• **The Process of Discernment:** The community at Antioch consulted the “Apostles and Elders” in Jerusalem. This led to a **lively and open discussion** followed by the authoritative witness of Peter.

• **Scriptural Interpretation:** James interpreted the situation in the light of prophecy and Scripture, formulating a decision grounded in God’s universal saving plan.

• **Participation of the “All”:** The sources emphasise that **everyone played an active part**. The entire Church in Jerusalem (_pan to plēthos_) was present throughout and involved in the final decision.

• **Consensus and Unanimity:** Through listening to the Spirit and God’s action, initially divergent opinions moved towards **consensus and unanimity** (_homothymadon_).

• **The Trinitarian Formula:** The authority of the decision was expressed in the formula: **“It has been decided by the Holy Spirit and by ourselves”** (Acts 15:28). This reflects a synergy between the community’s discernment and the ministerial responsibility of the leaders.

The sources suggest that the Church is like a **choir**, a harmonic reality where reciprocal and ordered relations allow everyone to converge in “common mind” and love. Just as a choir requires different voices to create a single, beautiful harmony, the synodal Church requires the participation of all its members, each in their specific role, to walk together in fidelity to the Gospel.

The Early Church understood synodality as the **“guarantee and incarnation”** of its fidelity to apostolic origins, manifesting as a “harmonic reality” where the People of God walked together.

**1. Local Synods and Ecumenical Councils**

In the first centuries, the Church applied the term “synod” to assemblies at various levels—diocesan, provincial, regional, patriarchal, and universal—to discern doctrinal, liturgical, and pastoral questions.

• **Provincial and Regional Synods:** By the fourth century, ecclesiastical provinces were established under a **Metropolitan**, using provincial synods as specific instruments to exercise ecclesial synodality. Decisions beyond the competency of a local Bishop were mandated to be taken by a synod, ensuring that the “first” (_protos_) among bishops acted only with the consensus of all.

• **Ecumenical Councils:** The **Council of Nicaea (325)** stands as the first universal institutional expression of the authority (_exousia_) of the Risen Lord guiding the Church through the Holy Spirit. Such councils required a specific harmony (_symphōnía_) between the heads of various Churches and the agreement of the Bishop of Rome.

**2. Role of Bishops, Deacons, and Local Communities**

The sources describe a tripartite dynamic of participation in the early Church’s life:

• **The Bishops:** As successors of the Apostles, they presided over the **Eucharistic synaxis** and exercised authority in service of communion.

• **Presbyters and Deacons:** St Ignatius of Antioch (2nd century) described the college of Presbyters as the **“Bishop’s council”**. St Cyprian of Carthage further solidified the role of the clergy, stating that he would do nothing without the advice of the presbyters and deacons (_nihil sine consilio vestro_).

• **Local Communities:** In the first millennium, the “whole community” generally took part in local synods. Cyprian famously argued that decisions also required the **“consensus of the people”** (_et sine consensu plebis_), ensuring that the entire “plebs” was present and involved in the Church’s discernment.

3. Monastic and Communal Discernment Traditions

While participation in higher-level councils eventually became restricted to bishops, monastic communities preserved and revitalised synodal procedures.

• **Contributions to Synods:** In the first millennium, though only bishops voted in Ecumenical Councils, **monks and presbyters** were frequently invited to provincial synods to contribute to the discernment process.

• **Communal Governance:** Later traditions, such as the **Monks of Cluny** and **Mendicant Orders**, kept synodal procedures alive through chapters and councils. In these communities, superiors were elected by representatives and were required to exercise their authority assisted by a council, reflecting an ecclesiology of communion.

**Example and Illustration**

**Example: The Council of Nicaea (325 AD)** The sources highlight the **Council of Nicaea** as the primary historical example of universal synodality. It brought together bishops from across the East and legates of the Bishop of Rome to establish normative canonical and doctrinal decisions, such as the profession of faith, which directed the way forward for the entire People of God.

**Illustration: The Church as a Choir** To illustrate the synodal nature of the early Church, **Saint John Chrysostom** used the image of a **choir**. He explained that the Church is a “harmonic reality” where different members, through their reciprocal and ordered relations, converge in a “common mind” (_homonoia_) and love. Just as a choir requires distinct voices to pull together into a single song of praise, the early Church required the distinct roles of the bishop, the clergy, and the laity to walk together in unity.

**4 – Synodality in the Early Church**

·         Local synods and ecumenical councils

·         Role of bishops, deacons, and local communities

·         Monastic and communal discernment traditions

The Early Church understood synodality as the **“guarantee and incarnation”** of its fidelity to apostolic origins, manifesting as a “harmonic reality” where the People of God walked together.

As a student of theology, you must first master the vocabulary. The word **synod** comes from the Greek _synodos_ (_syn_ meaning “with” and _hodos_ meaning “path”), literally indicating “the path along which the People of God walk together”. Historically, “synod” and **council** (from the Latin _concilium_) were used as synonyms to describe a meeting, principally of bishops, gathered in Christ’s name to make decisions binding on the Church.

**Local Synods and Ecumenical Councils**

The history of the Church reveals a constant interplay between local and universal gatherings:

• **The Apostolic Prototype:** The “cornerstone” for all synods is the **Council of Jerusalem** (Acts 15). Here, the “apostles and elders” met to discern the Holy Spirit’s will regarding Gentile converts, eventually reaching a consensus that they described as being decided “by the Holy Spirit and by ourselves”.

• **Local Synods:** In the first few centuries, these were remarkably frequent. By the 2nd century, at least 50 gatherings occurred in places like Palestine and North Africa, with at least 400 such meetings documented between the 2nd and 7th centuries. These local synods dealt with disciplinary and doctrinal issues, and while their decisions were primarily for their own regions, there were “striking instances”—such as North African councils regarding Donatism—where their rulings became binding on the whole Church.

• **Ecumenical (Universal) Councils:** These are “church-wide” meetings. The first was the **Council of Nicaea** in 325 AD, convoked by Emperor Constantine. These councils represent the “fullest and most solemn” expression of the Church’s synodal nature at a universal level.

**Roles of Bishops, Deacons, and Local Communities**

In the early Church, governance functioned through two modes: **hierarchical** and **collegial**.

• **Bishops:** By the 2nd century, bishops emerged as the “overseers, guardians and leaders” and successors to the Apostles. In synodal gatherings, they traditionally hold the “deciding vote”. However, early tradition, such as that of Cyprian of Carthage, insisted on the principle of _nihil sine episcopo_ (nothing without the bishop) alongside _nihil sine consilio vestro_ (nothing without the council of presbyters and deacons) and _sine consensu plebis_ (without the consensus of the people).

• **Deacons and Presbyters:** In the Roman Synods from the 5th century, the Bishop of Rome’s council included not just bishops but also **presbyters** (priests) and **deacons**. These figures were essential collaborators, ensuring that the bishop did not act in isolation.

• **The Local Community (Sensus Fidelium):** This Latin term means the “faithful’s sense of the faith”. It is the conviction that the entire “People of God” has an “instinct of faith” that makes them “infallible in credendo” (in believing). In the first millennium, the “whole community” typically took part in local synods, with everyone playing an active role according to their specific position.

**Monastic and Communal Discernment Traditions**

**Discernment** is the process of listening to the Holy Spirit through the Word of God and the “signs of the times” to discover God’s call in a specific situation.

• **Monastic Influence:** Throughout the Middle Ages, synodal procedures were kept alive by monastic communities like the **Monks of Cluny** and the **Mendicant Orders**. At the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215, **800 abbots** attended, actually outnumbering the bishops two to one. These communities offered significant experiences of “communal discernment”.

• **Theologians:** From the 13th century, theologians emerged as a distinct class of teachers who became “indispensable” in formulating council decrees. They serve the Church by helping to understand faith in “sapiential, scientific and prophetic ways”.

• **Circular Dynamics:** Modern synodality seeks to revive this ancient communal spirit through a “circularity” between the **“all”** (the whole People of God), the **“some”** (the College of Bishops), and the **“one”** (the Bishop of Rome).

**Analogy for Understanding:** Think of the early Church as a **harmonic choir**. The **Bishop** may be the conductor who ensures the group stays in time and follows the score (the _depositum fidei_ or deposit of faith), but the “music” of the Church’s life cannot exist without the **voices of the community** and the **harmony of the ministers** (deacons and priests). Synodality is the practice of ensuring all these different voices “converge in _agapé_ (love) and _homonoia_ (common mind)” to sing a single song of faith.

**5 – Synodality Through the Middle Ages to Modern Times**

·         Development and decline of synodal practices

·         Centralization, reform movements, and challenges

·         Recovery of synodal practices before Vatican II

**The history of synodality from the Middle Ages to the modern era reveals a trajectory of institutional adaptation, a period of significant centralisation, and a theological recovery that restored the active role of all the faithful.**

**1. Development and Decline of Synodal Practices**

During the Second Millennium, synodal procedures in the **Eastern Churches** remained relatively consistent with the tradition of the Fathers, maintaining patriarchal and metropolitan synods. In the **West**, however, the **Gregorian reform** (11th century) shifted the focus towards affirming the Pope’s authority as primate to secure the Church’s freedom (_libertas Ecclesiae_).

While synodal life was kept alive through the Middle Ages by the **Monks of Cluny**, **Cathedral Chapters**, and **Mendicant Orders**, the nature of these assemblies changed. In the Western Roman Empire, for instance, synods often lost their purely ecclesiastical character, becoming **royal or national synods** presided over by kings. By the post-Reformation era, synodal practices declined into administrative tools; the **Council of Trent** mandated annual diocesan synods, but these were used primarily to **pass on and enact hierarchical norms** rather than to foster communal participation.

**2. Centralisation, Reform Movements, and Challenges**

A major challenge arose during the Western Schism with the emergence of **Conciliarism**, a movement that sought to place a permanent council above the authority of the Pope. The Church eventually judged this idea to be out of step with Tradition, reaffirming that reform must respect the primatial authority of the Pope.

The subsequent “apologetic reaction” to the Protestant Reformation led to an intensified **“hierarchological vision”** of the Church. The Church was described as a _societas perfecta et inaequalium_ (a perfect and unequal society), which created a sharp division between:

• **The _Ecclesia docens_:** The Pope and Bishops who teach and govern.

• **The _Ecclesia discens_:** The rest of the People of God who simply receive instruction.

This centralisation reached its peak at **Vatican Council I**, which endorsed the primacy and infallibility of the Pope as the guarantor of the unity of faith.

**3. Recovery of Synodal Practices Before Vatican II**

The nineteenth century saw a providential return to the sources of Scripture and Tradition. Prophetic writers like **Johann Adam Möhler, Antonio Rosmini, and John Henry Newman** began to stress the dimension of **communion** and the _sensus fidei fidelium_ (the sense of faith of the faithful).

By the late 1800s, new institutional forms of collegiality began to emerge, such as **Episcopal Conferences**, which allowed bishops of a country to act together in response to changing geopolitical circumstances. There was a growing theological awareness that the **“Church is not identical with her pastors”** and that the laity have an active, not passive, role in transmitting the apostolic faith.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Example and Illustration**

**Example: The Council of Trent (1545–1563)** The sources highlight the Council of Trent as an example of a “Council of modern times” that moved away from the medieval model. While it successfully revitalised diocesan life—for example, through **Saint Charles Borromeo**, who convoked eleven diocesan synods—the focus remained on **passing on norms from the top down** rather than active participation by the whole People of God.

**Illustration: The “Inverted Pyramid”** The sources provide a powerful illustration of the shift from a centralised to a synodal mindset through the image of the **“inverted pyramid”**. In this model:

• The **base** (the People of God) is at the top.

• The **summit** (the hierarchical ministry) is located **below the base**. This illustrates that authority in a synodal Church is a form of service; those at the “top” are actually the “least of all,” existing to support the mission of the entire community.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To better understand this historical shift, consider the image of a **sailing ship**. In the period of decline and centralisation, the ship was viewed as having a single Captain who made every decision while the crew merely followed orders. In the synodal recovery, the Church remembers that for the ship to reach its destination, every member of the crew must use their specific gifts to read the wind and pull the ropes together, though they still look to the Captain to coordinate their efforts.

**6 – Vatican II and the Renewal of Synodal Vision**

·         Lumen Gentium: People of God and collegiality

·         Gaudium et Spes: dialogue with the world

·         Restoring participatory structures in the Church

The term “**synodality**” does not appear explicitly in the texts of **Vatican II**, it lies at the heart of the Council’s work for ecclesial renewal. The Council shifted the Church’s self-understanding toward an ecclesiology of **communion**, which provides the theological basis for a synodal Church.

**I. Lumen Gentium: The People of God and Collegiality**

_Lumen Gentium_ introduced a vision of the Church as the **pilgrim People of God**, emphasizing that all the baptised share in a **common dignity and mission**.

- **Common Dignity:** Every member of the Church, by virtue of their baptism, receives an “**anointing of the Holy Spirit**” and a share in the priestly, prophetic, and royal office of Christ. This implies that all the faithful are active agents of evangelisation, not merely passive recipients of instruction.
- **Episcopal Collegiality:** The Council taught that Jesus appointed the Apostles as a “**stable group**” or college, with Peter at the head. Collegiality is the specific way synodality is manifested through the ministry of Bishops, who exercise their authority in **hierarchical communion** with the Bishop of Rome.
- **Service of the Hierarchy:** _Lumen Gentium_ reordered the Church’s self-understanding by placing the **Mystery of the Church** and the **People of God** (Chapters 1 and 2) before the **Hierarchical Constitution** (Chapter 3). This structure demonstrates that the hierarchy is not an end in itself but is **at the service of the People of God** to fulfill the divine plan of salvation.

**II. Gaudium et Spes: Dialogue with the World**

_Gaudium et Spes_ reflects the Church’s “pilgrim” and “missionary” character, necessitating a **dialogue with the modern world**.

- **Signs of the Times:** A synodal Church is called to read and interpret the “**signs of the times**” in the light of the Gospel. This involves a communal discernment to discover God’s call within specific historical situations.
- **Social Diakonia:** The Council encouraged a “**social diakonia**” where the Church engages in constructive dialogue with different religions, cultures, and world-views. This dialogue aims to build a social ethos of **fraternity, solidarity, and inclusion**, specifically by hearing the “cry of the poor” and the “cry of the earth”.

**III. Restoring Participatory Structures**

To make the synodal vision a reality, the sources note that the Council invigorated ancient structures and created new ones to promote **participation and co-responsibility**.

- **Universal Level:** Blessed Paul VI instituted the **Synod of Bishops** in 1965 as a permanent council to extend the benefits of the communion lived during the Council to the entire People of God.
- **Local and Regional Levels:** The Council (specifically through _Christus Dominus_) invited the establishment of **Diocesan Pastoral Councils** and **Councils of Priests** to assist bishops in their governance. It also reinvigorated **Episcopal Conferences** and provincial synods to enhance collegiality across different regions.
- **Parish Level:** Participatory structures were extended to the immediate community through **Parish Pastoral Councils** and **Financial Councils**, which allow the lay faithful to participate in pastoral planning and consultation.

**Example and Illustration**

**Example: The Synod of Bishops** The institution of the **Synod of Bishops** serves as a primary example of restoring participatory structures. Established as a “permanent Council of Bishops for the universal Church,” it represents the entire Catholic episcopacy in hierarchical communion with the Pope. Today, this structure is increasingly used as a **point of convergence** for listening to the _sensus fidei_ of the entire People of God through broad preparatory consultations.

**Illustration: The Inverted Pyramid** The sources illustrate this Vatican II shift through the image of the **“inverted pyramid”**. In a traditional hierarchical model, the “top” commands the “bottom.” However, in a synodal Church, the **summit (the hierarchy) is located below the base (the People of God)**. This illustrates that those in authority are truly “**ministers**” (the “least of all”), because their role is to support and serve the baptised faithful in their common mission to the world.

To understand the modern “synodal turn” in the Church, we must look at the **Second Vatican Council** (1962–1965). While the Council did not use the specific word “synodality,” it provided the theological “soil” in which this ancient practice was replanted and renewed.

As a student of theology, you should view this as a programme of **_aggiornamento_**—a term meaning “bringing up to date”—whereby the Church looked back at its ancient traditions to find better ways to serve the modern world.

**1. _Lumen Gentium_: The People of God and Collegiality**

The Dogmatic Constitution on the Church, **_Lumen Gentium_** (meaning “Light of the Nations”), shifted the Church’s focus from a purely hierarchical structure to one based on **communion**.

• **The People of God:** Before discussing the hierarchy, the Council described the Church as the **People of God**. This emphasizes the **common dignity** of all the baptised. Every Christian, by virtue of their baptism, shares in the **priestly, prophetic, and royal office** of Jesus Christ.

• **Sensus Fidei:** This term refers to the **“instinct of faith”** or “the faithful’s sense of the faith”. The Council taught that the entire body of the faithful, from the bishops to the last of the lay faithful, cannot err in matters of belief when they manifest a universal consent. Therefore, the Church is **“infallible in credendo”** (in believing) because the Holy Spirit guides all members, not just the leaders.

• **Collegiality:** This defines how the **Bishops** work together. Just as the Apostles formed a “college” with Peter at the head, the Bishops form a **College of Bishops** with the Pope as the “visible principle and foundation of unity”. **Synodality** is the broad “walking together” of the whole People of God, while **collegiality** is the specific way bishops exercise this synodal nature at the leadership level.

**Illustration:** Pope Francis describes this as an **“inverted pyramid”**. In a traditional pyramid, the leaders are at the top and the people are at the bottom. In a synodal Church, the “top” (the hierarchy) is located **below the base** because their role is to be **ministers** (servants) to the People of God.

**2. _Gaudium et Spes_: Dialogue with the World**

The Pastoral Constitution **_Gaudium et Spes_** (“Joy and Hope”) focused on the Church’s relationship with the modern world.

• **Signs of the Times:** This concept refers to discerning God’s will by looking at the events, needs, and desires of the current historical moment. The Church must listen to the “cry of the poor” and the “cry of the earth” to understand its mission today.

• **Dialogue:** The Council moved away from a “fortress” mentality to one of **dialogue**. This involves a “reciprocal exchange of gifts” with other Christians, other religions, and the secular world.

• **Social Diakonia:** The term **_diakonia_** means “service.” The Church’s synodal life is expressed through its commitment to justice, peace, and the common good in society.

**3. Restoring Participatory Structures**

To make this vision a reality, the Church restored or created structures that allow for **participation and co-responsibility**.

• **Universal Level:** In 1965, Pope Paul VI instituted the **Synod of Bishops**. This is a permanent council of bishops from around the world who advise the Pope on major issues. While traditionally consultative (offering advice) rather than deliberative (making final laws), it is the primary tool for universal collegiality.

• **Local (Diocesan) Level:** The Council encouraged **Diocesan Synods**, where the Bishop gathers his clergy, religious, and laypeople to discern the local Church’s path. It also established **Pastoral Councils** and **Councils of Priests** (the “senate of priests”) to assist the Bishop in governance.

• **Parish Level:** Synodal life is most visible in the **Parish Pastoral Council** and **Finance Council**. These allow laypeople to play an active role in planning the mission of their local community.

**Definitions for the Student:**

• **Kairós:** A Greek word for a “favourable time” or a “moment of grace”.

• **Parrhesía:** The Greek term for “frankness” or “courageous speech” needed for honest dialogue in a synod.

• **Communion:** The deep “union with God and unity between persons” that forms the substance of the Church’s mystery.

**Analogy for Understanding:** Think of the Church as a **large family estate**. Before Vatican II, the estate was managed solely by the **Head of the House** (the Pope) and the **Estate Managers** (Bishops). Following the Council’s “renewal,” the family realised that the estate belongs to the **entire Family** (the People of God). Now, the managers hold regular **family meetings** (synods). While the Head of the House still makes final decisions, he does so only after listening to the **wisdom and experience** (sensus fidei) of all the family members, ensuring the estate truly reflects the needs of everyone living there.

To understand the modern **Synod of Bishops**, we must view it as a “new thing” brought forth from the “ancient treasure” of the Church’s tradition. While synods have existed since the second century, the specific institution we use today was born from the renewal of the Second Vatican Council.

We should first distinguish between **synodality** (the broad “walking together” of the whole People of God) and **collegiality** (the specific way bishops exercise authority in communion with the Pope).

**1. Paul VI’s _Motu Proprio: Apostolica Sollicitudo_**

On 15 September 1965, as Vatican II was concluding, **Blessed Pope Paul VI** issued a document called a **_Motu Proprio_**—a Latin term meaning “of his own accord,” indicating a decree issued on the Pope’s personal initiative. This document, titled **_Apostolica Sollicitudo_**, officially instituted the **Synod of Bishops**.

• **Definition:** The Synod of Bishops is a “permanent Council of Bishops for the universal Church”. Unlike an Ecumenical Council (like Vatican II), which is a rare and solemn event, this is a permanent structure designed to provide ongoing support to the Pope.

Paul VI’s establishment of the Synod of Bishops in 1965 created a mechanism for ongoing dialogue between the Pope and the bishops of the world. Since then, numerous assemblies have been held, each addressing specific themes relevant to the Church’s life and mission at that time.

Here are key examples of Synods of Bishops, categorized by their type (Ordinary General, Extraordinary General, and Special), along with their purposes and themes.

These are held at fixed intervals (usually every 3–4 years) to discuss matters of major importance to the universal Church.

**The Current Synod on Synodality (2021–2024)**

This is arguably the most significant since the institution’s founding because its _topic_ is the Synod itself.

- **Theme:** “For a Synodal Church: Communion, Participation, and Mission.”
- **Purpose:** Pope Francis initiated this multi-year process not just to discuss a single topic, but to change _how_ the Church functions—moving toward a “listening Church” where all members (laity, clergy, religious) journey together.
- **Structure:** Unlike previous synods held only in Rome, this one began with a “listening phase” in every local diocese worldwide, followed by continental assemblies, before culminating in sessions in Rome (2023 and 2024).

• **The Consultative Nature:** A key theological distinction introduced by Paul VI was that this Synod is primarily **consultative** rather than **deliberative**.

    ◦ **Consultative (_Votum tantum consultivum_):** This means the members provide information and offer advice to the Pope.

    ◦ **Deliberative:** This refers to the power to make binding laws. The Synod only has this power when the Pope specifically confers it upon them.

• **Purpose:** Its goal was to continue the “benefits of communion” experienced during the Council and to assist the Pope in the care of the universal Church.

**2. Evolution of Synodal Processes**

The “evolution” of these processes is a journey from the **Apostolic Age** to the **Third Millennium**.

• **Ancient Roots:** The **Council of Jerusalem** (Acts 15) serves as the “cornerstone” and “paradigm”. Here, the Apostles and Elders reached a consensus they described as being decided “by the Holy Spirit and by ourselves”.

• **Shift in the Second Millennium:** For much of the first millennium, synods were **decision-making bodies** that made “decisions binding on the church”. However, after the 11th century in the West, the hierarchical mode of governance became more central, and the Pope assumed the exclusive right to convoke these gatherings.

• **Vatican II Restoration:** The Council shifted the focus back to an **ecclesiology of communion**, moving away from a “hierarchological” vision where the Church was seen only as a “perfect society” of unequals.

• **Modern Phases:** Today, the synodal process has evolved into a **three-phase cycle**: **Preparation** (consulting the faithful), **Celebration** (the meeting of bishops), and **Implementation** (acting on the decisions in local churches).

**3. Bishops in Communion with the Pope**

The theology of the Synod is rooted in the relationship between the **“One,” the “Some,” and the “All”**.

• **The “One” (The Primacy):** The Bishop of Rome (the Pope) is the “visible principle and foundation of unity”. He is not “above” the Church, but “within it as one of the baptised”.

• **The “Some” (Collegiality):** This refers to the **College of Bishops**. Just as Peter and the Apostles formed a “stable group” (_cœtus_), the Pope and the Bishops form a college. They exercise “supreme and full power over the universal Church,” but only when they act **“cum Petro et sub Petro”** (with Peter and under Peter).

• **The “All” (Sensus Fidei):** This is the “faithful’s sense of the faith”. It is the conviction that because of their baptism, the entire People of God has an “instinct of faith” that makes them **“infallible in credendo”** (infallible in believing).

**Illustration: The Inverted Pyramid** To understand how these roles fit together, Pope Francis uses the illustration of an **inverted pyramid**. In a normal pyramid, the leaders are at the top and the people are at the base. In a synodal Church, the **top is located below the base**. This means that the Bishops and the Pope are “ministers” (which literally means “the least of all”) who are placed at the bottom to serve the People of God.

**Analogy for Understanding:** Think of the Synod of Bishops as a **Global Satellite System (GPS)** for a massive fleet of ships (the local churches). The **Pope** is the fleet commander who decides the ultimate destination. The **Bishops** are the captains who meet in the “Synod” to compare charts and signal the commander about the conditions of the sea. However, they don’t just look at their own maps; they listen to the **crew and passengers** (the People of God) who are actually living on the water. By “walking together,” the commander ensures the whole fleet reaches the same port safely, guided by the “wind” of the Holy Spirit.

**8 – Pope Francis and the Synodal Church**

·         The 50th Anniversary Address (2015)

·         Key themes: listening, discernment, encounter

·         Clericalism and the need for co-responsibility

To understand the vision of Pope Francis regarding the **Synodal Church**, we must view it as a “new threshold” in the wake of the Second Vatican Council. For Francis, synodality is not merely an occasional event but the “constitutive way of the Church”.

We should recognise that while the term **synodality** is a recent neologism, its practice is the revival of the Church’s oldest tradition of governance.

**The 50th Anniversary Address (2015)**

The most significant “programmatic commitment” to this vision was made by Pope Francis on 17 October 2015, during the commemoration of the **50th anniversary of the institution of the Synod of Bishops**.

• **The Core Message:** Francis stated, “It is precisely this path of synodality **which God expects of the Church of the third millennium**”.

• **Constitutive Dimension:** He emphasised that synodality is an “essential dimension” of the Church; it is what the Church _is_.

• **The Inverted Pyramid:** In this address, he sketched an image of the Church as an **“inverted pyramid”**. In this theological illustration, the “summit” (the hierarchy) is located below the base (the People of God) because those in authority are called to be **ministers**—a term which originally means “the least of all”.

**Key Themes: Listening, Discernment, and Encounter**

Pope Francis’s vision is built on a specific _modus vivendi et operandi_ (way of living and working) characterized by three pillars:

• **Listening:** A synodal Church is, first and foremost, a **“Church which listens”**. This is not a passive hearing but a mutual process: the faithful, the College of Bishops, and the Bishop of Rome all listen to each other, and all listen to the **Holy Spirit**. It requires **_parrhesía_**—the courage and frankness to speak the truth in love.

• **Discernment:** This is the process of discovering God’s call in a specific historical situation. It involves interpreting the **“signs of the times”** through prayer, meditation, and dialogue. Communal discernment allows the Church to move from divergent opinions toward **consensus and unanimity**, which are the fruits of the Spirit.

• **Encounter:** This theme relates to the Church’s **missionary “moving outwards”**. It involves a “culture of encounter” where the Church engages in **social _diakonia_** (service) and dialogue with the world, specifically hearing the “cry of the poor” and the “cry of the earth”.

**Clericalism and the Need for Co-responsibility**

For synodality to become a reality, Pope Francis identifies specific obstacles that must be overcome through a “pastoral and missionary conversion”.

• **Clericalism:** This is described as an “excessive” mindset that treats the clergy as a superior class and “keeps the lay people away from decision-making”. It views the Church through a “hierarchological” lens as a society of unequals, which Francis seeks to quash.

• **Co-responsibility:** This is the theological antidote to clericalism. It is based on the **common dignity** of all the baptised. Every member of the Church is a “missionary disciple” and a “protagonist of mission”.

• **The “One, Some, and All”:** Synodal co-responsibility is expressed through a circular relationship between the **“All”** (the _sensus fidei_ of the entire People of God), the **“Some”** (the College of Bishops), and the **“One”** (the Petrine ministry of the Pope).

**Example for the Student:** Consider a **Parish Pastoral Council**. If the priest makes all decisions alone without consulting the community, he is practicing **clericalism**. If he gathers the council merely to tell them his plans, he is missing the “listening” phase. In a **synodal** approach, the priest (the “one”) listens to the diverse experiences of the lay faithful (the “some”) who represent the whole parish (the “all”). Together, they **discern** how to help the local poor (the **encounter**), and then the priest takes a decision that is “informed by the consensus” of his people.

**Analogy for Understanding:** Think of the Church as a **massive expedition** through a mountain range. **Clericalism** is like a guide who ignores the hikers and demands they follow him blindly. **Synodality** is the guide sitting down with the whole group at a campfire to **listen** to their struggles and **discern** the best path forward based on everyone’s observations of the terrain. The guide still leads the way, but the “path” is chosen together, ensuring the whole **People of God** reach the peak in unity.

**9-Synodality and the Sensus Fidei**

·         Meaning of sensus fidei and sensus fidelium

·         The role of the whole People of God

·         Discerning the voice of the Spirit in the community

Welcome to this exploration of how the Church understands the “voice” of her members. To grasp **synodality**, one must understand the relationship between the Holy Spirit and the baptised. We are looking at what Pope Francis calls a “treasure” that brings forth both “new things and old”.

**1. Meaning of _Sensus Fidei_ and _Sensus Fidelium_**

As a student of theology, you will encounter these Latin terms frequently. They are the theological “heart” of a synodal Church.

• **Sensus Fidei (Sense of Faith):** This refers to an **“instinct of faith”**. It is a spiritual capacity given by God to every baptised person, creating a certain “connaturality” with divine realities. This means a Christian can often grasp the truth of the faith intuitively, even if they cannot explain it in complex theological words.

• **Sensus Fidelium (Sense of the Faithful):** This is the collective manifestation of that instinct. When the whole body of the faithful—from the Bishops to the last of the lay people—manifests a universal consent in matters of faith and morals, they are said to be **“infallible in credendo”** (infallible in believing). They do not err because the Spirit guides the totality of the Church.

• **Sentire cum Ecclesia:** This means **“to feel, sense and perceive in harmony with the Church”**. It is the key to “walking together,” ensuring that our personal “instinct” is tuned to the “heartbeat” of the whole community.

**2. The Role of the Whole People of God**

In a synodal Church, the “subject” of the Church is not just the hierarchy, but **everyone**.

• **Common Dignity:** By virtue of baptism, every member shares in the **priestly, prophetic, and royal office** of Christ. All have the same dignity and are called to the same mission.

• **Missionary Disciples:** The sources move away from a “hierarchological” view where the clergy do all the teaching and the laity only listen. Instead, every baptised person is a **“protagonist of mission”**.

• **Co-responsibility:** This is the principle that the “All” (the whole people) must participate in the life of the Church according to their specific roles. This includes the lay faithful’s competency in social, cultural, and professional sectors, which is considered **indispensable** for the Church’s discernment.

• **The Inverted Pyramid:** Pope Francis illustrates this role by sketching the Church as an **“inverted pyramid”**. In this model, the “top” (the hierarchy) is located below the “base” (the People of God) because authority is defined as being the “least of all” in service to the community.

**3. Discerning the Voice of the Spirit in the Community**

How do we actually “hear” God in a group of thousands of people? This requires **communal discernment**.

• **Listening:** A synodal Church is a **“Church which listens”**. This is a circular process: the faithful listen to the Pastors, the Pastors listen to the faithful, and all listen to the Holy Spirit.

• **Dialogue and Parrhesía:** True synodal dialogue requires **courage and frankness** (_parrhesía_) in speaking, and **humility** in listening. It is not a debate to win an argument, but a spiritual communication intended to build communion amid disagreement.

• **Interpreting the “Signs of the Times”:** Discernment involves looking at the real experiences and challenges of the community to discover God’s call in a particular historical moment.

• **The Three-Phase Cycle:** In practice, this happens through **preparation** (consulting the base), **celebration** (the meeting of leaders), and **implementation** (returning the decisions to the people).

**Example for the Student:** Imagine a parish trying to decide how to respond to a local rise in poverty. Instead of the Priest simply announcing a plan, the synodal approach begins with **consultation**. He listens to the “sense of faith” of the parishioners who live in those neighbourhoods. He dialogues with social experts in the pews. They pray and reflect on the Word of God together. Through this **communal discernment**, they discover a path that no one individual could have seen alone—this is the Holy Spirit speaking through the “we” of the community.

**Analogy for Understanding:** Think of the _sensus fidei_ like the **musical ear** of a member of a vast choir. Each singer has a personal “instinct” for the melody. **Synodality** is the act of the whole choir singing together. If one person sings off-key (ignores the _sentire cum Ecclesia_), the harmony is broken. But when they all listen to the **Conductor** (the Holy Spirit) and to **one another**, they produce a single, beautiful sound that is greater than any individual voice. The **Bishop** ensures everyone stays on the correct sheet music (the _depositum fidei_), but the “song” belongs to the whole choir.

**10 – The Synod on Synodality (2021–2024)**

·         Global listening process

·         Themes: communion, participation, mission

·         Insights from diocesan, continental, and universal stages

Welcome to our final lecture on the current “living history” of the Church. We are moving from the ancient roots of synodality into its most ambitious modern expression: the **Synod on Synodality (2021–2024)**.

To study this as a theologian, you must understand that this is not just a meeting but a **Kairós**—a Greek term meaning a “moment of grace” or an opportune time in God’s plan. It represents a “new threshold” in the wake of Vatican II, moving the Church from a “hierarchological” vision toward a “missionary energy” that involves the entire People of God.

The Global Listening Process

The defining feature of this Synod is its “breathtaking inclusiveness”. Unlike historical synods which were restricted to a small number of participants, Pope Francis has called for a “massive consultation”.

• **Sensus Fidei and Sensus Fidelium:** As we discussed previously, these terms refer to the “instinct of faith” given to all the baptised. The global listening process is designed to activate this instinct, as the Church believes the “totality of the faithful” cannot err in matters of belief when they are in agreement.

• **Parrhesía:** This is the Greek word for **frankness** or **courageous speech**. In the listening sessions, the faithful were encouraged to speak with _parrhesía_ and listen with humility, creating a “circularity” of communication.

• **The Inverted Pyramid:** To explain the role of leadership in this process, the sources use the image of an **inverted pyramid**. Here, the “summit” (the Pope and Bishops) is located **below the base** because their primary role is to be “ministers” (servants) who listen to the needs and faith of the people.

The Three Themes: Communion, Participation, Mission

These three pillars are the theological “engine” of the synodal path.

1. **Communion:** This is the “profound substance” of the Church’s mystery. It is not just social togetherness but a reflection of the **Trinitarian life**—the unity of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. It expresses the “ecclesial ‘we’” where different cultural forms are bonded in mutual dependence.

2. **Participation:** This is based on the **common dignity** of all the baptised. In a synodal Church, everyone is a **“subject”**—a protagonist who plays an active role according to their specific charism. It moves away from the idea that only the clergy are responsible for the Church.

3. **Mission:** The Church exists in order to **evangelise**. Synodality is the _modus vivendi et operandi_ (way of living and working) that empowers the Church to “go outside the camp” and bring the Gospel to the modern world.

Insights from the Three Stages

The Synod was designed to evolve through three distinct phases: **Preparation, Celebration, and Implementation**.

• **The Diocesan Stage (The “All”):** This was the “point of departure”. By starting at the “base,” the Church ensured that the process remained connected to people’s daily problems. The goal was to reach “all the voices” that express the People of God in the local Church.

• **The Continental Stage:** This stage allowed for **inculturation**—the process of making the Gospel “at home” in different socio-cultural regions. It encouraged local Churches to share their unique theological and spiritual gifts in a “reciprocal exchange”.

• **The Universal Stage (The “Some” and “One”):** This is the “point of convergence” for the listening process conducted at every other level. Here, the **College of Bishops** (the “some”) meet in hierarchical communion with the **Pope** (the “one”) to discern the way forward for the entire universal Church.

**Summary of Terms for the Student:**

• **Modus vivendi et operandi:** A Latin phrase meaning “way of living and working.” It describes how synodality should be the Church’s ordinary style.

• **Consensus and Unanimity:** The “fruit of communal discernment” where initially different opinions move toward a common mind through the Holy Spirit.

• **Co-responsibility:** The idea that all members, not just the clergy, are responsible for the Church’s life and mission.

**Analogy for Understanding:** Think of the Synod on Synodality as a **global research expedition**. In the **Diocesan stage**, every single explorer (the baptised) is asked to report on the terrain they see right in front of them. In the **Continental stage**, regional leaders gather these reports to map out the mountain ranges and rivers of their specific continents. Finally, in the **Universal stage**, the Head of the Expedition (the Pope) and the Lead Navigators (the Bishops) look at all the maps together. They don’t replace the explorers’ reports; they use that collective wisdom to decide the best path for the whole group to reach its final destination.

**11 – Theological Principles of Discernment**

·         Ignatian and biblical roots of communal discernment

·         Spiritual conversation method

·         Listening to the marginalized and “the least”

In this lesson, we will examine the **Theological Principles of Discernment**, which serve as the internal “compass” of a synodal Church. To understand these, a student of theology must move beyond viewing church governance as mere administration and see it as a spiritual activity.

1. Biblical and Theological Roots of Communal Discernment

**Discernment** is defined as the **theologal interpretation of the “signs of the times”** under the guidance of the Holy Spirit. It is the process of traveling a path that follows God’s plan as it is fulfilled in every **Kairós** (a specific “favourable time” or moment of grace) throughout history.

• **The Biblical Paradigm:** The **Council of Jerusalem** (Acts 15) provides the “normative” model for discernment. This event shows the early Church facing a decisive challenge and resolving it through a **“lively and open discussion”**. The community listened to the Holy Spirit’s voice through the witness of God’s actions, eventually moving from divergent opinions toward **consensus and unanimity** (_homothymadon_).

• **The Trinity as Model:** Discernment is rooted in the life of the **Blessed Trinity**. Just as the Divine Persons live in a “communion of love,” the Church is called to be a **“harmonic reality”**—like a choir—where members converge in a **“common mind”** (_homonoia_).

• **Ignatian Influence:** While your request mentions “Ignatian” roots, the sources specifically highlight the broader **spirituality of communion** that informs this practice. This requires a **“pastoral and missionary conversion”** of heart and mind, moving from an “I” centered focus to an **“ecclesial ‘we’”**.

2. The Spiritual Conversation Method

Synodal discernment does not happen through parliamentary debate or political negotiation; it happens through **spiritual communication**. This method requires two essential “spiritual muscles”:

• **Parrhesía (Courageous Frankness):** This is the **“trust, frankness and courage”** to speak what one believes the Spirit is suggesting for the general good. It is an “outward-moving” energy that prevents the community from remaining stagnant.

• **Humility (_Tapeinophrosyne_):** Dialogue fails if it is undermined by “jealousy” or “vanity”. True discernment requires the **“mind of Christ,”** which is characterized by obedience to God’s will and seeing others as more important than oneself.

The goal of this method is to **“build communion amid disagreement”**. Instead of one side “winning” a debate, the community adopts a **“relational way of viewing the world,”** seeing through the eyes of others to reach a shared vision.

3. Listening to the Marginalised and “the Least”

A central principle of synodal discernment is that the Holy Spirit often speaks through those on the edges of society.

• **The Prophetic Witness:** The message of the Prophets teaches that true conversion is found in **justice for the poorest, the oppressed, and foreigners**. This is not just a social duty but a “tangible witness to God’s mercy”.

• **Hearing the “Groans” of the Spirit:** Discernment requires carefully listening to the **“silent cry”** of the People of God. The Church is specifically called to hear both **“the cry of the poor and the cry of the earth”**.

• **The Inverted Pyramid:** In a synodal Church, the hierarchy is an **“inverted pyramid”** where the “top” is located below the base. Consequently, leaders are called **“ministers”** because they are, in the original meaning of the word, the **“least of all”**. By listening to the marginalized, the Church ensures it is not acting out of “arbitrariness” but is staying faithful to its mission to “proclaim the Gospel to the poorest”.

**Example for the Student:** Imagine a local parish discerning how to use its building. A **debate** would involve different groups arguing for their own interests (e.g., a youth club vs. a senior social). A **spiritual conversation** begins with prayer and **listening**. The community specifically seeks out the “least”—perhaps the homeless individuals near the church or those who feel excluded. By listening with **parrhesía** and **humility**, they might discern a third option: a project that serves the whole community’s spiritual hunger and physical needs, reaching a **consensus** that feels like the work of the Holy Spirit rather than a human compromise.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Analogy for Understanding:** Think of communal discernment like a **mountain rescue team** navigating a thick fog. If the leader (the hierarchy) ignores the radio reports from the team members (the laity) who are standing in different parts of the valley, the whole group stays lost. However, if the team members only shout over each other to be right, they cannot hear the faint whistle of the person they are trying to save (the marginalized). Only by **silencing their own voices** to listen for that faint whistle, and then **communicating clearly** with one another, can they discern the one true path to safety.

**12 – Participation and Co-Responsibility**

·         Baptismal dignity and mission

·         Role of laity, women, youth, families, religious, clergy

·         Building participatory pastoral structures

Welcome to our exploration of how the Church functions as a living body. Today, we will examine **Participation and Co-Responsibility**—two pillars that define the “synodal path”. In a synodal Church, every member is called to play an active role, moving away from a model where only a few “leaders” carry the weight of the mission.

**1. Baptismal Dignity and Mission**

To understand participation, we must first look at **Baptism**. In theology, we speak of the **common dignity** of the baptised. By virtue of this sacrament, all members of the Church—regardless of their specific role—share the same status as “children of God” and are appointed to the same mission.

• **Sensus Fidei:** This is a vital term for your studies. It refers to the **“instinct of faith”** given by the Holy Spirit to all the baptised. Because of this anointing, the People of God are considered “infallible in believing” (_infallible in credendo_), meaning that as a whole, the community cannot err in matters of faith.

• **Missionary Discipleship:** In the past, people often viewed the clergy as the “teachers” (_Ecclesia docens_) and the laity as the “learners” (_Ecclesia discens_). A synodal vision quashes this; every baptised person is a **“protagonist of mission”** because they are “missionary disciples”.

**Illustration:** Think of the Church not as a train where the clergy are the engine and the laity are the carriages being pulled along, but as a **single body** where every cell and organ—through the “anointing” of Baptism—is alive and contributes to the movement of the whole.

**2. The Roles of Various Members**

Participation is not a “one-size-fits-all” concept; it respects a variety of **charisms** (spiritual gifts) and **ministries** (specific roles).

• **The Laity:** They constitute the “immense majority” of the People of God. Their role is essential because they bring a “specific competency” from their lives in cultural and social sectors. Consulting them is **indispensable** for the Church to truly discern God’s will.

• **The Clergy (Pastors):** Their authority is a specific gift of the Spirit for the “upbuilding of the entire Body”. However, their role is to be **ministers**—a word that, in its original sense, means “the least of all”—serving the community.

• **Women and Religious:** The sources highlight a need to better appreciate the “consecrated life” and the “qualified contribution” of women. This involves the principle of **“co-essentiality,”** where the hierarchical gifts of the clergy and the charismatic gifts of religious communities work together for renewal.

• **Theologians:** Theology is a communal and collegial service. Theologians help the Church understand the faith in “prophetic ways” and are “indispensable” in formulating Church documents.

**3. Building Participatory Pastoral Structures**

For participation to be more than just a nice idea, it must be built into the **structures** of the Church. This is the difference between “thinking synodally” and “making synodality happen”.

• **Local Structures:** In your own parish, you see this in the **Parish Pastoral Council** and the **Financial Council**. At a higher level, the **Diocesan Synod** is a “grace-filled event” where the Bishop gathers all voices to discern the way forward for the local Church.

• **The Circular Dynamic:** Synodal life works through a relationship between the **“One”** (the Bishop or Pope), the **“Some”** (the councils or College of Bishops), and the **“All”** (the whole People of God).

• **Overcoming Clericalism:** A major obstacle to these structures is **clericalism**—a mindset that concentrates all responsibility in the hands of the clergy and keeps the laity away from decision-making. Co-responsibility means the clergy must **listen carefully** to the wishes (_vota_) of the faithful before making decisions.

**Definition for the Student:** **Co-responsibility** means that the life and mission of the Church do not belong solely to the hierarchy, but are a shared duty for all, according to each person’s specific calling.

**Illustration: The Inverted Pyramid** Pope Francis uses the image of an **“inverted pyramid”** to explain these structures. In a normal pyramid, the leaders are at the top. In a synodal Church, the **summit is below the base**. This means the hierarchy (the summit) exists to support and serve the People of God (the base).

**Analogy for Understanding:** Think of the Church as a **large family estate**. In the past, it might have been run like a business where only the “Manager” made decisions. In a synodal vision, it is run like a **home**. While the “Head of the House” holds final responsibility, they do not act without a **family meeting**. Every child, parent, and grandparent contributes their unique perspective. The house only thrives when everyone feels **co-responsible** for its warmth, its budget, and its welcome to the neighbours. Without the manager, there is no order; but without the family, there is no home.

**13 – Synodal Leadership and Ministry**

·         Servant leadership

·         Humility, dialogue, and accountability

·         Leading parishes through discernment and shared mission

**The Heart of Synodal Ministry: Leadership as Service and Discernment**

To understand **synodal leadership**, one must first grasp the etymology of the word “synod.” It is derived from the Greek _συν_ (with) and _όδός_ (path), signifying the **“path along which the People of God walk together”**. In a theological sense, synodality is the **“modus vivendi et operandi”** (way of living and working) of the Church, where leadership is not a detached power but a service exercised within a community.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**I. Servant Leadership: The “Inverted Pyramid”**

The sources describe a profound shift in how authority is understood, moving away from a “hierarchological” vision to one of **servant leadership**.

• **The Concept of the Inverted Pyramid:** **Pope Francis**, in his 2015 address commemorating the 50th anniversary of the Synod of Bishops, famously illustrated the Church as an **“inverted pyramid”**. In this model, the “top” (the hierarchy) is located **below the base** (the People of God).

• **The Meaning of “Minister”:** The sources explain that those who exercise authority are called “ministers” because, in the original meaning of the word, they are **“the least of all”**. Their authority—referred to in the New Testament as **_exousia_**—is a gift for the “upbuilding of the entire Body” rather than a representative function of the people.

• **Historical Context of Authority:**

    ◦ **Constantine:** In 325, **Emperor Constantine** convoked the first Ecumenical Council in Nicaea. He served as the honorary chair, demonstrating that leadership in synods has historically involved a complex relationship between secular and sacred authorities.

    ◦ **Pope Innocent III:** In 1215, he convoked the **Fourth Lateran Council**, ordering kings and dukes to attend alongside 800 abbots, who actually outnumbered the bishops two to one.

    ◦ **Centralisation:** After the 11th century, papal claims became more peremptory, and popes assumed the exclusive right to convoke councils, marking a period of high **hierarchical centralisation**.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**II. Humility, Dialogue, and Accountability**

Synodal leadership requires a specific **spiritual ethos** characterised by several key virtues and methods of communication.

• **Humility (_Tapeinophrosyne_):** This is the essential attitude for synodal dialogue. It involves seeing others as **more important than oneself** and putting the common good first. It is modeled on the **_kenosis_** (emptying) of Christ, who was obedient to the Father.

• **Dialogue as Spiritual Communication:** **Blessed Paul VI** taught that true dialogue is **spiritual communication** that requires love, respect, and trust. **Benedict XVI** added that truth is _lógos_ (word) which creates **_diá-logos_** (dialogue), leading to communion.

• **Parrhesía:** This refers to the **“trust, frankness and courage”** to speak one’s mind while being open to what the Spirit says through others.

• **Accountability (_Quod omnes tangit_):** The sources highlight a principle of Roman law used in the Medieval Church: **_“Quod omnes tangit, ab omnibus tractari et approbari debet”_** (what affects everyone should be discussed and approved by all). This ensures that leaders are accountable to the community they serve.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**III. Leading Parishes Through Discernment and Shared Mission**

In the parish—the visible, immediate form of the Church—leadership is exercised through **communal discernment** and **co-responsibility**.

• **Communal Discernment:** This is the process of interpreting the **“signs of the times”** under the guidance of the Holy Spirit to discover God’s call in a particular historical situation.

    ◦ **The Apostolic Paradigm:** The **Council of Jerusalem** (Acts 15) is the primary example. The Apostles and Elders, led by **James** and **Peter**, listened to the Spirit and moved from “initially divergent opinions” towards **consensus and unanimity** (_homothymadón_).

• **Co-responsibility and the _Sensus Fidei_:** Leaders must recognize the **_sensus fidei_** (the faithful’s sense of the faith). **Saint John Henry Newman** famously argued for “consulting the faithful in matters of doctrine,” a principle that underscores that all the baptised are “agents of evangelisation”.

• **Participatory Structures:**

    ◦ **Pastoral Councils:** In the local parish, synodality is institutionalised through the **Parish Pastoral Council** and the **Financial Council**.

    ◦ **Historical Example:** In the 19th-century United States, the seven **Councils of Baltimore** shaped the young American Church, making decisions on discipline and doctrine locally while remaining in communion with Rome.

    ◦ **The Rule of Cyprian:** In the 3rd century, **Cyprian of Carthage** established that while nothing should be done without the Bishop (_nihil sine episcopo_), it is equally true that nothing should be done without the counsel of the presbyters or the **consensus of the people** (_sine consensu plebis_).

**14 – Challenges and Opportunities**

·         Fear of change, polarization, and resistance

·         Misunderstanding Synodality as parliamentary democracy

·         Opportunities for evangelization, unity, and renewal

**Navigating the Path of the Third Millennium: Challenges and Opportunities in a Synodal Church**

Welcome to this theological exploration. As we study the “Synodal Church,” we are looking at what **Pope Francis** calls a **_Kairós_**—a Greek term meaning a providential moment of grace or a “favourable time” in God’s plan. While the term “synodality” is a recent neologism coined about 20 years ago, it represents the revival of the Church’s oldest traditions of governance. However, this revival brings specific challenges and profound opportunities for the People of God.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**1. The Obstacles: Fear, Polarization, and Resistance**

The path of synodality is not without its hurdles. These often stem from historical habits and a lack of understanding of the Church’s true nature as communion.

• **Clericalism and Resistance to Change:** One of the greatest challenges is a **“clerical mindset”** that risks keeping the lay faithful on the edges of ecclesial life. This often manifests as **clericalism**, an excessive focus on the ministry of Pastors that fails to appreciate the charisms of the laity, women, and youth.

• **Fear of Plurality:** There is often a fear that “walking together” will lead to **polarization** or conflict. However, the sources suggest that the criterion for dialogue is that **“unity prevails over conflict”**. We must avoid the temptations of “jealousy” (_eri-theía_) and “vanity” (_kenodoxía_), which undermine communal life.

• **Historical Context of Diminishment:** After the **First Vatican Council (1869–1870)** defined papal primacy and infallibility, synodal practices in the West significantly diminished, leading to a “hierarchological” vision where the clergy were the teachers (_Ecclesia docens_) and the laity were merely learners (_Ecclesia discens_). Breaking this long-standing habit of passive participation is a major modern challenge.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**2. The Great Misunderstanding: Synodality vs. Parliamentary Democracy**

A frequent point of confusion for theology students—and even for seasoned practitioners—is the tendency to view a synod as a political or parliamentary body.

• **Not a Parliament:** The sources explicitly state that synodal procedures should not be understood as **“parliamentarianism on a political level”**. A council or synod is not a “debating society” or a gathering to negotiate power.

• **The Nature of Authority:** In a parliament, authority is delegated by the people. In a synodal Church, the **authority of Pastors** is a specific gift of the Holy Spirit for the upbuilding of the Body, not a representative function of the people.

• **Consultative vs. Deliberative:** Traditionally, councils made decisions “binding on the church”. However, the **Synod of Bishops** instituted by **Pope Paul VI in 1965** was designed as a **consultative body** to provide advice to the Pope, who remains the final decision-maker.

• **The Inverted Pyramid:** Pope Francis explains this unique authority through the image of an **“inverted pyramid”**. In this model, the “summit” (the hierarchy) is located below the base because those in authority are **“ministers”**—literally meaning “the least of all”—who serve the People of God.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**3. Opportunities for Evangelisation, Unity, and Renewal**

Despite the challenges, synodality offers the “most appropriate framework” for the Church to meet the complexities of the modern world.

• **Missionary Renewal:** Synodality is an indispensable precondition for a **“new missionary energy”**. By activating the **_Sensus Fidei_** (the faithful’s sense of the faith), every baptised person becomes a “protagonist of mission” and a “missionary disciple”. This was championed by thinkers like **Saint John Henry Newman**, who argued for “consulting the faithful in matters of doctrine”.

• **Ecumenical Unity:** Synodality is at the heart of the commitment to full Christian unity. It allows for a “reciprocal exchange of gifts” with other traditions. For example, the **Chieti Document (2016)** highlights how the Orthodox and Catholic Churches share a common history of synodal structures linked with primacy from the first millennium.

• **Social Diakonia (Service):** The synodal journey is a school for acquiring the _ethos_ needed for dialogue with the world. It compels the Church to hear **“the cry of the poor and the cry of the earth”** and to promote justice and peace as a “tangible witness to God’s mercy”.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Historical Examples and Key Figures**

To understand these concepts logically, we can look at specific historical moments:

1. **Emperor Constantine (325 AD):** He convoked the **Council of Nicaea**, the first ecumenical council, and even served as its honorary chair, setting an early precedent for the “Some” (Bishops) and “One” (Authority) working together.

2. **Pope Innocent III (1215 AD):** At the **Fourth Lateran Council**, he ensured wide participation, including kings, dukes, and 800 abbots, showing that synods have historically been more inclusive than we might imagine.

3. **The Councils of Baltimore (19th Century):** These seven local councils in the **United States** shaped the young American Church, making binding decisions on discipline and doctrine long before the modern centralisation of the 20th century.

4. **Saint Charles Borromeo & Saint Turibius of Mogrovejo (16th Century):** Following the **Council of Trent**, these bishops used frequent diocesan synods as the primary tool to implement reform and renewal in their respective regions (Milan and the Americas).

**Summary of Key Terms**

• **_Parrhesía:_** The “trust, frankness and courage” required to speak the truth in love during synodal discernment.

• **_Sensus Fidei:_** The “instinct of faith” given to all the baptised, enabling them to grasp divine realities intuitively.

• **_Sentire cum Ecclesia:_** To “feel, sense and perceive in harmony with the Church,” which is the key to walking together.

• **_Diakonia:_** The service the Church provides to society, particularly in the areas of justice and solidarity.

**15 – Synodality and the Mission of the Church**

·         Synodal Church as a missionary Church

·         Synodality in ecumenism and interreligious dialogue

·         Concluding vision: “A Church that listens, walks, and serves together”

**The Synodal Church: A Missionary Journey of Listening and Service**

To understand the relationship between **synodality** and the **mission** of the Church, we must view it as a providential **kairós**—a Greek term meaning a “moment of grace” or a providential opportunity in history. As a student of theology, you should see synodality not merely as a bureaucratic procedure, but as the Church’s **modus vivendi et operandi** (way of living and operating). It is the specific way the People of God “walk together” to fulfil their calling.

**1. The Synodal Church as a Missionary Church**

The Church does not exist for itself; she exists to **evangelise**. The sources describe a “synodal Church” as inherently a “missionary Church” because synodality provides the “most appropriate framework” for a new missionary energy.

- **Missionary Discipleship:** Following the teachings of **Vatican II**, every baptised person is considered a “protagonist of mission”. This is based on the **sensus fidei** (the faithful’s “instinct of faith”), which helps all Christians discern the Word of Truth.
- **Overcoming Clericalism:** A key challenge to mission is **clericalism**—an “excessive” mindset that keeps the laity away from decision-making. A missionary conversion requires quashing paradigms that concentrate all responsibility for mission only in the hands of the Pastors.
- **The Inverted Pyramid:** **Pope Francis** illustrates this missionary authority as an **“inverted pyramid”**. In this model, the “summit” (the hierarchy) is located below the base because authority is defined as being the “least of all” in service to the People of God.

**Example:** In the 19th century, **Saint John Henry Newman** argued for “Consulting the Faithful in Matters of Doctrine,” a prophetic view that highlighted the laity’s active role in transmitting the apostolic faith—a core element of modern synodal mission.

**2. Synodality in Ecumenism and Interreligious Dialogue**

Synodality is described as being “at the heart of the ecumenical commitment”. It serves as an invitation to all Christians to walk together toward full communion.

- **Reciprocal Exchange of Gifts:** Ecumenism in a synodal key is not about compromise, but a logic of a “reciprocal exchange of gifts” in the light of truth. This allows the Church to enrich herself with the treasures of other Christian traditions.
- **Structures of Primacy:** The **Chieti Document (2016)**, an important reference in Catholic-Orthodox dialogue, notes that in the **First Millennium**, structures of synodality were “inseparably linked with primacy”. Healing modern divisions requires returning to this shared theological and canonical legacy.
- **Social Diakonia:** The term **_diakonia_** means “service”. In interreligious dialogue, synodality manifests as a “culture of encounter”. The Church walks beside members of various religions to build justice and hear the **“cry of the poor and the cry of the earth”**.

**Historical Illustration:** **Emperor Constantine** convoked the **Council of Nicaea (325)** to address doctrinal questions. While the political context was unique, it demonstrated that the Church has always sought “communal discernment” to resolve challenges.

**3. Concluding Vision: A Church that Listens, Walks, and Serves Together**

The final vision of synodality is a “Church that listens,” where every member—the faithful, the College of Bishops, and the Bishop of Rome—listens to each other and, together, to the Holy Spirit.

- **Parrhesía:** This Greek term refers to the **“trust, frankness and courage”** required to speak the truth and enter the “expanse of God’s horizon”. Without _parrhesía_, dialogue becomes a mere debate rather than a spiritual communication.
- **The Trinity as Model:** Synodality reflects the **Trinitarian life**—the unity and love between the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Just as the Divine Persons are in communion, the Church’s members are “companions on the journey” (_sýnodoi_).
- **The Circular Relationship:** Synodal life is lived in the circular relationship between the **“All”** (the whole People of God), the **“Some”** (the College of Bishops), and the **“One”** (the ministry of unity of the Pope).

**Analogy for the Student:** Think of the Church as a **householder** who “brings forth from his treasure both **new things and old**”. The **“old”** is the ancient tradition of synods used by **Pope Innocent III** at the **Fourth Lateran Council (1215)**, which included not just bishops but 800 abbots and secular leaders. The **“new”** is the “breathtaking inclusiveness” of **Pope Francis’s** vision, which invites the entire People of God to participate in the Church’s missionary journey toward the Kingdom of Heaven.
