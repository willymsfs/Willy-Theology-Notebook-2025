To understand **local and provincial synods**, we must first clarify the terminology used in the sources. A **synod** (or council) is defined as a meeting, principally of bishops, gathered in Christ’s name to take action and make decisions that are **binding on the church**. Historically, “synod” and “council” were synonyms; the distinction between them is a relatively recent development in canon law.

**What are Local and Provincial Synods?**

**Local synods** are assemblies convoked at various levels of the Church’s structure—such as a single diocese, a province, or a wider region—to discern doctrinal, liturgical, and pastoral questions.

**Provincial synods** are a specific type of local synod that occur at the level of an **ecclesiastical province**, which is a grouping of several neighbouring local churches (dioceses). These synods were established as early as the fourth century to demonstrate and promote communion between these local churches. Their primary purpose is to allow bishops to pool their abilities and wills for the common good and to harmonise pastoral choices across a specific territory.

**Historical Examples and Explanations**

The sources provide several key examples of these gatherings:

• **The Council of Jerusalem (Acts 15):** This is the “cornerstone” and **apostolic prototype** for all synods. It involved the “apostles and elders” meeting to resolve a controversy regarding Gentile converts.

• **North African Councils (4th and 5th Centuries):** These are “striking instances” where local provincial decisions on doctrinal issues, specifically regarding the heresies of Donatism and Pelagianism, were eventually accepted as binding on the **entire universal Church**.

• **The Councils of Baltimore (19th Century):** In the United States, seven provincial and plenary councils shaped the young American Church, making decisions on discipline and doctrine without immediate recourse to Rome.

• **Post-Trent Reform Synods:** Following the Council of Trent, **Saint Charles Borromeo** convoked five provincial and eleven diocesan synods in Milan to implement reforms, while **Saint Turibius of Mogrovejo** did the same in America with three provincial synods.

**Who Conducted Them? (Convocation and Presidency)**

In the history of the Church, the responsibility for conducting these synods fell to specific leaders:

• **The Metropolitan:** At the provincial level, the **Metropolitan** (the bishop of the principal see in a province) was responsible for convoking and presiding over the synod.

• **The Prōtos:** According to Apostolic Canon 34, the bishops of a nation must recognise the “first among them” (**prōtos**) as their head and do nothing significant without his agreement.

• **Legitimate Authority:** In a broader historical sense, councils were convoked by a “legitimate authority”. In some instances, such as the first Ecumenical Council at Nicaea, this was even a secular authority like **Emperor Constantine**, who served as the honorary chair.

Who were the Participants?

While bishops held the “deciding vote,” the sources emphasise that synods were often **inclusive gatherings**:

• **Bishops:** As successors to the Apostles, they were the primary participants and guardians of the faith.

• **Presbyters and Deacons:** In the early Roman Synods and under leaders like Cyprian of Carthage, the bishop’s council included **priests and deacons**. Cyprian famously stated that he would do nothing without the council of his clergy.

• **Monastic Leaders:** At the Fourth Lateran Council (1215), **800 abbots** attended, actually outnumbering the bishops two to one.

• **Theologians and Experts (Periti):** From the 13th century onwards, theologians became “indispensable” for formulating decrees.

• **The Lay Faithful (Plebs):** Early records show the “consensus of the people” was often sought. Historical local synods frequently took place “in the presence of a great part of the people”.

• **Civil Authorities:** Historically, representatives of kings, emperors, and high dignitaries were often present, especially in the Middle Ages.

**Analogy for Understanding:** Think of a **Provincial Synod** like a **regional summit of mayors**. While each mayor (bishop) is the “boss” of their own city (diocese), they meet regularly at the regional level (province) under a chairperson (Metropolitan). They don’t just bring themselves; they bring their advisors, experts, and community representatives (clergy, theologians, and laity) to ensure that the rules they make for the whole region reflect the needs and the faith of everyone living there.

**Definitions and Canonical Distinctions**

The Code of Canon Law (1983) distinguishes them clearly. A **diocesan synod** occurs within a single diocese, convoked and presided over by the diocesan bishop (paras 4, 9; cf. canons on diocesan synod). Local synods historically refer to these or similar gatherings.

A **provincial synod** (or council) gathers bishops of an ecclesiastical province under the metropolitan archbishop. **Plenary councils** extend to a larger territory, such as a nation or region, requiring Holy See recognition (para 4).

These forms differ from the universal Synod of Bishops or ecumenical councils.

**Historical Development and Examples**

Practice emerged in the third century. Provincial synods addressed disciplinary and doctrinal questions; bishops presided, with decisions circulated via letters and canons to express communion among Churches (para 28). Local (diocesan-level) assemblies involved broader participation in principle, ordered by roles—presbyters, deacons, and laity offering consent—following Cyprian of Carthage's principle: nothing without the bishop, the presbyters' counsel, or the people's consensus (para 25, 30).

In the Middle Ages, local synods often shifted toward royal influence, with kings or princes presiding in some regions (para 33).

The Council of Trent (1545–1563) marked vigorous renewal. It mandated annual diocesan synods and triennial provincial ones to implement reforms universally (para 35).

- **Saint Charles Borromeo**, Archbishop of Milan (1564–1584), convoked eleven diocesan synods and five provincial councils in the province of Milan. He presided as metropolitan; participants included suffragan bishops, clergy, and religious superiors, focusing on clerical reform, catechesis, and sacramental discipline.
- **Saint Turibius of Mogrovejo**, Archbishop of Lima (1581–1606), held thirteen diocesan synods and three provincial councils for the Peruvian province. He conducted them amid evangelisation challenges in the New World; participants comprised missionaries, clergy, and indigenous representatives in limited ways.
- In Mexico that century, three provincial councils applied Trent's decrees continent-wide.

Post-Trent culture emphasised hierarchy, so participation remained largely clerical, with bishops and superiors dominant; lay involvement appeared indirectly through consent or representation (para 35).

In Eastern Catholic Churches, analogous structures persisted as patriarchal or metropolitan synods, preserving ancient collegial patterns (para 31).

**Application in Today's Context**

The Commission insists that authentic synodality today requires renewing these structures to involve the entire People of God more actively (paras 8–9, 54–60 on implementation at local levels).

Diocesan synods now serve as privileged instruments for communal discernment. The bishop convokes them, but preparation includes wide consultation—lay faithful, religious, clergy—through listening sessions reflecting _sensus fidei fidelium_. Outcomes guide local mission in creative fidelity to Tradition.

Provincial or plenary assemblies address regional questions, such as inculturation or social justice.

Recent practice aligns with Pope Francis call for a synodal Church: many dioceses worldwide convoked synods during the 2021–2024 Synod on Synodality process, gathering diverse voices to contribute to universal discernment.

These historical forms thus become vital today for fostering co-responsibility, healing clericalism, and unleashing missionary energy at grassroots levels—precisely what the document urges as precondition for renewed evangelisation (para 9).
