**Magisterial Foundations: Synod of Bishops**

**Collegiality Made Permanent in the Life of the Church**

After the Second Vatican Council, the Church faced a decisive question: how could the synodal and collegial vision of Vatican II remain alive once the Council had ended? The bishops had experienced genuine communion, shared responsibility, and collective discernment during the Council sessions from 1962 to 1965. The danger was that, once the Council closed, the Church would return to a purely centralized mode of governance. The answer to this concern was the creation of the Synod of Bishops, a permanent structure at the service of communion between the Pope and the bishops.

This development belongs to the magisterial reception of Vatican II, not to later pastoral experimentation.

**Paul VI and Apostolica Sollicitudo (1965)**

**From a Council Event to a Permanent Synodal Structure**

The Synod of Bishops was established by Paul VI on 15 September 1965, just weeks before the close of Vatican II, through the motu proprio Apostolica Sollicitudo.

The historical context is crucial. Paul VI had personally guided the second half of Vatican II after the death of John XXIII in 1963. He was deeply convinced that the experience of episcopal collegiality during the Council should not end with the Council itself. Apostolica Sollicitudo therefore established the Synod of Bishops as a stable institution, not an occasional gathering.

In this document, Paul VI clearly states that the Synod of Bishops exists to:

•              assist the Pope in the governance of the universal Church,

•              strengthen unity between the Pope and the bishops,

•              promote shared reflection on matters concerning faith, morals, and pastoral action.

What is important here is the balance. The Synod of Bishops is not a parliament and not a council superior to the Pope. Paul VI explicitly places it under papal authority, while at the same time affirming it as a real expression of episcopal collegiality. This directly reflects Vatican II’s teaching in Lumen Gentium that bishops act with and under Peter, not independently of him.

The creation of the Synod of Bishops can be seen as the institutional answer to the conciliar experience: the Church needed a way to keep walking together after the Council.

**The Evolution of Synodal Processes**

**From Consultation to Discernment**

In its early years, the Synod of Bishops functioned primarily as a consultative body. The first Synod, held in 1967, focused on preserving and strengthening Catholic faith in the modern world. Subsequent synods addressed topics such as priesthood, evangelization, catechesis, and the family.

Under John Paul II (1978–2005), the Synod of Bishops became an important instrument for shaping global Catholic teaching. For example:

•              the 1980 Synod on the Family influenced Familiaris Consortio,

•              the 1985 Extraordinary Synod assessed the reception of Vatican II,

•              the 1997 Synod for America shaped missionary priorities for the continent.

During this period, synods were highly structured and largely centered on bishops’ interventions. The process emphasized consultation, but discernment was still tightly controlled by central structures. This reflects the Church’s cautious approach in the decades immediately following Vatican II, as she sought stability after rapid change.

A further development occurred under Benedict XVI (2005–2013), who emphasized the theological depth of synodal reflection, especially the relationship between Scripture, Tradition, and the Magisterium. While the structure of synods remained largely the same, there was growing awareness that synodality involved not only bishops speaking, but also listening.

A significant shift took place under Pope Francis. Francis explicitly described synodality as “the path which God expects of the Church in the third millennium.” Under his pontificate, synodal processes expanded beyond meetings in Rome to include wide consultation of the People of God, diocesan listening processes, and spiritual conversation. This development did not change the nature of the Synod of Bishops, but deepened its synodal character.

**Bishops in Communion with the Pope**

**Authority as Shared Responsibility**

At the heart of the Synod of Bishops is a specific understanding of authority. Bishops are not representatives of local opinions, nor are they independent leaders acting apart from the universal Church. They are successors of the apostles, entrusted with pastoral responsibility for their local Churches, and at the same time members of the worldwide episcopal college.

This understanding is rooted in Vatican II’s teaching and made concrete through the Synod of Bishops. When bishops gather in synod, they do so in communion with the Pope, who presides over the synodal process. The Pope listens, discerns, and ultimately confirms decisions, safeguarding unity and fidelity to the Gospel.

A concrete example of this communion can be seen in how post-synodal apostolic exhortations are issued. After synodal discussions, the Pope does not simply repeat what was said. He exercises discernment, integrating the synodal process into the Church’s magisterial teaching. This shows that synodality does not weaken papal authority but enriches it through shared listening.

**Historically, this model avoids two extremes:**

•              on one side, conciliarism, which places authority above the Pope,

•              on the other side, isolation, where authority is exercised without consultation.

The Synod of Bishops stands between these extremes as a magisterial expression of communion.

**Concluding Perspective**

The establishment of the Synod of Bishops by Paul VI through Apostolica Sollicitudo represents a decisive moment in the Church’s modern history. It ensured that the collegial and synodal vision of Vatican II would not remain a temporary experience but become a permanent dimension of Church life. Over time, synodal processes have evolved from structured consultation to deeper communal discernment, especially under Pope Francis. Throughout this evolution, one principle has remained constant: bishops exercise their ministry with and under the Pope, in communion, for the good of the whole Church.

This magisterial foundation makes it clear that synodality is not a pastoral fashion. It is a stable and authoritative way in which the Church lives her apostolic responsibility in history.

**Pope Francis and the Synodal Church**

**Synodality as a Way of Being Church in the Third Millennium**

When Pope Francis speaks about synodality, he is not proposing a new programme or an administrative reform. He is describing what he believes the Church is called to be at this moment in history. For Pope Francis, synodality is not simply about synods. It is about how the Church lives, listens, decides, and serves. His teaching emerges from Vatican II, but it responds directly to the pastoral, cultural, and ecclesial challenges of the twenty-first century.

Francis became pope in 2013, at a time when the Church was facing declining credibility, internal polarization, clerical abuse scandals, and growing distance between Church leadership and ordinary believers. Against this background, he proposed synodality as a path of conversion, not merely a method of consultation.

**The 50th Anniversary Address of the Synod of Bishops (2015)**

**Synodality as the Path God Expects of the Church**

A decisive moment in Pope Francis’s teaching on synodality came on 17 October 2015, when he addressed the Synod of Bishops on the 50th anniversary of its institution by Paul VI in 1965.

In this address, Pope Francis made a statement that has since become foundational for contemporary ecclesiology. He said that “synodality is the path which God expects of the Church in the third millennium.” This was not rhetorical language. Francis was making a theological claim: that walking together, listening together, and discerning together belong to the very identity of the Church.

In this address, Francis carefully located synodality within tradition. He recalled that the Synod of Bishops was created as a fruit of Vatican II’s teaching on collegiality, but he also acknowledged that, for many years, synods functioned mainly as consultative meetings of bishops in Rome. What was missing, he argued, was a deeper involvement of the whole People of God.

Francis described the Church as an “inverted pyramid”. At the top, in this image, are the People of God. Below them are bishops, and at the bottom is the Pope himself, whose role is to serve communion. This image directly challenges worldly models of authority without denying hierarchical structure. Authority remains, but it is exercised as service, not domination.

**Listening, Discernment, and Encounter**

**Core Spiritual Themes of Francis’s Synodal Vision**

At the heart of Pope Francis’s understanding of synodality are three closely connected spiritual attitudes: listening, discernment, and encounter.

Listening, for Francis, is not a courtesy or a strategy. It is a theological act. The Church listens first to the Word of God, then to the People of God, and finally to the cries of the poor and the signs of the times. In his synodal teaching, Francis often reminds the Church that the Holy Spirit speaks not only through official documents or leaders, but also through the lived faith of ordinary believers. Listening is described as an **“attitude of listening to the voice of the Spirit”**. It is a foundational practice where the Church seeks to hear all its members—including the laity, youth, and marginalised groups—to understand the challenges they face. It is the prerequisite for a “listening and discerning church”.

This conviction has concrete consequences. Under Francis, synodal processes have expanded beyond bishops’ meetings to include diocesan consultations, listening sessions with lay people, religious, women, young people, and those who feel marginalized or wounded by the Church. The Synod on the Family (2014–2015) and later the Synod on Synodality (2021–2024) illustrate this shift clearly.

Discernment is the second key theme. Francis insists that synodality is not about majority votes or ideological debates. It is about seeking the will of God together. Drawing deeply from Ignatian spirituality, Francis emphasizes patience, prayer, silence, and attentiveness to the movements of the Spirit. He frequently warns that without discernment, synods become parliaments and the Church becomes polarized. **Discernment is seen as “common discernment”** as a process to identify “the paths of evangelisation”. It involves moving away from isolated decision-making toward a collective effort to understand God's will for the community.

Encounter is the third essential dimension. For Francis, synodality cannot be lived from a distance. It requires proximity, dialogue, and a willingness to be changed by meeting others. This includes encounter within the Church, between bishops and people, but also encounter with the world, with other Christian traditions, and with other religions. In this sense, synodality is inseparable from mission. Encounter is viewed as a **“humble and dialogic encounter”** with the modern world. It is the act of “walking together” (the literal meaning of synodality), where differences in culture, race, and language are not barriers but parts of a “sacred journey” toward unity in Christ.

**These Core Spiritual Themes of Francis’s Synodal Vision is r**ather than an “old-fashioned” mission of merely increasing membership, synodality calls for a **“relational witness”** aimed at the renewal and transformation of the world. The practices of listening and discernment are driving the **revision of Canon Law** and the incorporation of women at all levels of Church life. The **“method of spiritual conversation”** is presented as the practical way to facilitate these dynamics in parishes and assemblies, fostering a community spiritual dynamic that leads to consensus building.

**Clericalism and the Need for Co-Responsibility**

**A Conversion of Authority and Participation**

One of the strongest and most consistent themes in Pope Francis’s teaching is his critique of clericalism. He identifies clericalism not simply as moral failure, but as a distortion of ecclesiology. Clericalism occurs when ordained ministry is separated from service, when authority becomes self-referential, and when the laity are treated as passive recipients rather than active subjects of mission.

Francis repeatedly states that clericalism is the enemy of synodality. It blocks listening, silences the Spirit, and creates a Church that speaks but does not hear. This critique is rooted in history. After centuries of centralization, especially following First Vatican Council, the Church developed habits where authority was exercised without sufficient consultation. Francis does not deny the need for authority, but he calls for its conversion.

In response, he emphasizes co-responsibility, grounded in baptism. All the baptized share responsibility for the Church’s life and mission, even though their roles differ. Bishops and priests do not lose authority in a synodal Church. Rather, their authority is exercised in communion, transparency, and accountability.

A concrete example of this is Francis’s encouragement of parish councils, diocesan synods, and episcopal conferences to function meaningfully, not symbolically. Another example is his insistence that women and lay experts be included in synodal processes, not as observers but as contributors to discernment.

**Concluding Perspective**

Pope Francis’s vision of a synodal Church is not an abstract theory. It is a response to concrete historical challenges: loss of trust, cultural change, internal division, and the Church’s mission in a complex world. His 2015 address marks a turning point in the reception of Vatican II, moving synodality from principle to practice.

For Francis, a synodal Church is one that listens attentively, discerns prayerfully, encounters others humbly, rejects clericalism, and embraces shared responsibility. In this way, synodality becomes not merely a structure, but a spiritual style of Church life, faithful to tradition and open to the future.

**Synodality and the Sensus Fidei**

**Listening to the Spirit in the Whole People of God**

When the Church speaks today about synodality, one concept becomes absolutely central: the sensus fidei. Without this concept, synodality risks becoming either a sociological exercise or a parliamentary process. With it, synodality becomes what it truly is: a spiritual process of discerning the voice of the Holy Spirit in the believing community.

The idea of sensus fidei is not modern. It belongs to the Church’s deepest tradition and has been lived long before it was precisely named.

Meaning of Sensus Fidei and Sensus Fidelium

**From Biblical Experience to Theological Language**

The Latin expression sensus fidei means “the sense of faith.” It refers to a spiritual capacity given by the Holy Spirit to believers, enabling them to recognize what truly belongs to the faith handed down from the apostles. Closely related is the expression sensus fidelium, which means “the sense of the faithful,” that is, the shared faith-consciousness of the whole Church.

Although the Latin terms developed later, the reality itself is already present in Scripture. In the New Testament, faith is never presented as a purely private possession. Saint Paul speaks of believers as those who have received the Spirit, who enables them to discern what comes from God. Saint John writes that the faithful have an “anointing” from the Holy One that teaches them. This biblical conviction lies behind the Church’s later reflection on the sensus fidei.

In the early centuries, this reality was lived instinctively. When controversies arose about doctrine or practice, the Church did not rely only on isolated authorities. She looked to the faith that was being lived, prayed, and confessed across the Christian communities. A famous expression of this conviction comes from Augustine of Hippo (4th–5th century), who insisted that bishops must listen to the faith of the Church, because they themselves belong to the believing community. Augustine described himself as “a Christian with you and a bishop for you,” capturing the balance between authority and shared faith.

Later, in the 19th century, the English theologian John Henry Newman played a decisive role in articulating this idea more clearly. Reflecting on the Arian crisis of the 4th century, Newman observed that at certain moments the faithful preserved orthodox belief even when many bishops hesitated. His work helped the Church recognize that the faith of the people is not merely devotional, but theologically significant.

**The Role of the Whole People of God**

**Baptismal Dignity and Shared Responsibility**

The Second Vatican Council brought this long tradition into clear focus. In Lumen Gentium, the Council taught that the whole People of God share in Christ’s prophetic mission. This means that all the baptized, not only clergy or theologians, participate in the Church’s reception and transmission of faith.

This teaching was shaped by the historical context of the mid-20th century. After two World Wars and the collapse of old imperial structures under rulers such as Wilhelm II and Franz Joseph I, authority itself was widely questioned. Vatican II responded not by weakening doctrine, but by rediscovering the Church as a communion, rather than a purely top-down institution.

The Council insisted that the sensus fidei belongs to the whole People of God when they remain faithful to the Gospel, united with their pastors, and attentive to the Spirit. This does not mean that every opinion expressed by believers is an expression of the sensus fidei. Rather, it means that the lived faith of the Church as a whole has a receptive capacity for truth.

In a synodal Church, this role of the faithful becomes visible through consultation, listening processes, and shared discernment. Bishops and pastors are not replaced by the people, but they are called to listen seriously to the faith experience of those entrusted to their care.

**Discerning the Voice of the Spirit in the Community**

**Between Authority and Consensus**

Discerning the voice of the Spirit is the most delicate and demanding aspect of synodality. The Church has always known that not every voice is the voice of the Spirit. Discernment requires prayer, patience, fidelity to Scripture and Tradition, and communion with legitimate authority.

The biblical model for this process remains the Council of Jerusalem described in Acts 15. The apostles and elders listened to lived experience, debated openly, and finally reached a decision that they attributed not to human strategy but to the Holy Spirit: “It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us.” This phrase captures the heart of ecclesial discernment.

Historically, the Church has applied this model in ecumenical councils. At Nicaea in 325, convened by Constantine the Great, the bishops did not simply impose an imperial solution to the Arian controversy. They listened to the faith of the Churches and confessed what had been believed and worshipped, especially in baptism and liturgy. The council’s authority rested not only on episcopal agreement, but on the reception of its teaching by the faithful.

In modern times, this dynamic has been strongly emphasized by Pope Francis. Francis repeatedly insists that synodality begins by listening to the People of God, because the Spirit speaks through their lived faith. At the same time, he warns against confusing discernment with opinion polls or ideological pressure. True discernment takes place when bishops, theologians, and faithful listen together, each according to their vocation, under the guidance of the Spirit.

A concrete example of this process can be seen in the recent synodal consultations, where local Churches gathered experiences from parishes, families, young people, and marginalized groups. These voices were not treated as final judgments, but as essential data for ecclesial discernment, to be weighed prayerfully by pastors in communion with the universal Church.

**Concluding Perspective**

Synodality and the sensus fidei are inseparable. Without the sensus fidei, synodality becomes procedural. Without synodality, the sensus fidei remains unheard. Together, they express a Church that trusts the Spirit at work in the whole People of God, while safeguarding unity through apostolic leadership.

In this vision, the Church does not choose between authority and participation. She lives from their communion. The Spirit who guides the bishops also dwells in the faithful, and synodality is the way the Church learns, again and again, to listen to that Spirit together.
