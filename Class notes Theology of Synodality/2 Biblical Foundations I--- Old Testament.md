**2 – Biblical Foundations I: Old Testament**

·         People of God on a journey

·         Covenantal assemblies and communal discernment

·         Prophetic listening and obedience

According to the sources, the **Old Testament** provides the primary narrative and theological framework for synodality, establishing the Church’s identity as a **People of God on a journey** towards unity and communion.

**Old Testament roots of synodality:**

 **1: Start with the very beginning – creation itself**

God creates the human person (man and woman) in His own image and likeness. From the start, humans are social beings – we are made for relationship, for communion. Our first calling is to cooperate with God, to care for creation together, and to move forward toward the goal He sets (Genesis 1:26-28).

Synodality begins here: the idea that we are not meant to walk alone but together with others and with God.

 **2: Sin breaks the harmony, but God refuses to abandon the plan**

Sin enters and tears apart the beautiful network of relationships that showed truth, goodness, and beauty in creation. Human hearts become blinded to their true calling.

Yet God, rich in mercy, does not give up. Again and again He confirms and renews His covenant in order to gather everything that has been scattered and to set it back on the path of unity. He heals human freedom and invites us to accept the gift of union with Him and with one another in creation, our common home.

Examples of these renewing covenants: Genesis 9:8-17 (after the flood), Genesis 15 and 17 (with Abraham), Exodus 19-24 (at Sinai), 2 Samuel 7:11 (with David).

**The key point:** God’s entire saving plan is about gathering a scattered people and putting them back on a shared path.

 **3: God convokes an assembly – the qahal**

To carry out this plan, God does not deal with isolated individuals. He calls together a people. He convokes Abraham and his descendants (Genesis 12:1-3; 17:1-5; 22:16-18).

This gathered people is called in Hebrew qahal or ‘edah (often translated into Greek as ekklēsia – the same word later used for “church”). The great moment of sealing this assembly is the Sinai covenant (Exodus 24:6-8; 34:20ff).

Freed from slavery in Egypt, Israel now becomes a people worthy to speak directly with God. On the exodus journey they gather around Him, celebrate His worship, and live according to His law, recognising that they belong to Him alone (Deuteronomy 5:1-22; Joshua 8; Nehemiah 8:1-18).

Synodality shows itself here as “assembly” – a people called together by God to walk with Him.

 **4: The Assembly has Order and Includes Everyone**

In the desert God commands a census of the tribes and gives each its proper place (Numbers 1-2). At the very centre stands the Lord Himself – the only true guide and shepherd.

He makes Himself present through the ministry of Moses (Numbers 12; 15-16; Joshua 8:30-35). Moses is helped in a subordinate and collegial way by judges (Exodus 18:25-26), elders (Numbers 11:16-17, 24-30), and Levites (Numbers 1:50-51).

Crucially, the assembly is not just adult men. It includes women, children, and even foreigners living among them (Joshua 8:33, 35).

Every time God renews the covenant, He calls the whole people as His partner (Deuteronomy 27-28; Joshua 24; 2 Kings 23; Nehemiah 8).

So synodality in the Old Testament is inclusive and ordered: everyone has a place, and the people together listen, decide, and commit themselves.

 **5: The prophets keep calling the people to faithful walking together**

The prophets constantly remind Israel to journey through history’s difficulties while staying faithful to the covenant. They summon the people to turn their hearts back to God and to live justice toward neighbours – especially the poor, the oppressed, and strangers – as the visible sign of God’s mercy (Jeremiah 37:21; 38:1).

To make this faithful journey possible, God promises interior renewal: a new heart and a new spirit (Ezekiel 11:19-20), a new exodus (Jeremiah 37-38), and above all a new covenant written not on stone tablets but on human hearts (Jeremiah 31:31-34).

This renewed covenant will reach universal horizons: the Servant of the Lord will gather the nations (Isaiah 53), and the Spirit will be poured out on every member of the people (Joel 3:1-4).

The prophetic voice therefore insists that true synodal walking requires continual listening, heartfelt conversion, and concrete justice.

**Putting it all together**

The Old Testament picture of synodality is this: God calls a people to walk together with Him through history, gathers them regularly in assembly to listen to His Word and renew the covenant, gives them ordered roles so everyone belongs, and – through the prophets – keeps calling them to interior conversion so they can stay faithful on the shared path.

All of this prepares the ground for what will be fully revealed in Jesus Christ: a People of God journeying together in communion, guided by the Spirit.

**3- Biblical Foundations II: New Testament**

·         Jesus forming a community of disciples

·         The early Christian community (Acts 2–4)

·         The Council of Jerusalem (Acts 15) as a synodal model

In the **New Testament**, synodality is presented as a fundamental way of being for the People of God, rooted in the life of Jesus and the guidance of the Holy Spirit in the early Church. The following points detail how the sources describe this biblical foundation.

**Jesus Forming a Community of Disciples**

The term “synod” itself draws on the deepest themes of Revelation, meaning “walking together” along a path. In the New Testament, Jesus is revealed as **“the way, the truth and the life”** (Jn 14:6), and his early followers were appropriately called “followers of the Way”.

• **Communion with the Father:** Jesus never acts alone but always does the will of the Father, who dwells in Him and carries out His work. This **Trinitarian communion** is the source and model for the Church’s synodal life.

• **The Pilgrim Image:** Jesus is depicted as a **pilgrim** who proclaims the Good News, points the way to the Kingdom, and shares the truth and love of communion through the Holy Spirit.

• **The Emmaus Icon:** The account of the disciples on the road to Emmaus is described as a **living icon of the Church**, where the Risen Lord guides, illuminates, and feeds the community with His Word and the Bread of Life.

• **Authority and Charisms:** Jesus conferred **authority** (_exousia_) on the Apostles to teach and baptise, but by virtue of baptism, **every member** of the People of God receives a share in this authority through the “anointing of the Holy Spirit”. The Spirit distributes various charisms for the upbuilding of the one Body of Christ, requiring mutual submission and service.

**The Early Christian Community**

Following the outpouring of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost, the early Church lived out its vocation through communal discernment.

• **The Guide:** The **Holy Spirit** is the leading figure who guides the way and gives direction to the Apostolic Church.

• **Communal Responsibility:** Disciples were tasked with listening to the Spirit’s voice to discern the path forward. The sources cite the choice of the “seven men of good reputation” in Acts 6 and the mission to the Gentiles in Acts 10 as key examples of this community-wide discernment process.

• **Shared Life:** The life of the early community (Acts 2–4) is characterised by an ordered movement where members, despite varied roles, are equally responsible for the mission and life of the community.

**The Council of Jerusalem as a Synodal Model**

The **Council of Jerusalem (Acts 15)** is identified as the **paradigm for Synods** celebrated by the Church across the centuries. It demonstrates how the Church resolves controversial questions through orderly, communal discernment.

• **The Process of Discernment:** The community at Antioch consulted the “Apostles and Elders” in Jerusalem. This led to a **lively and open discussion** followed by the authoritative witness of Peter.

• **Scriptural Interpretation:** James interpreted the situation in the light of prophecy and Scripture, formulating a decision grounded in God’s universal saving plan.

• **Participation of the “All”:** The sources emphasise that **everyone played an active part**. The entire Church in Jerusalem Acts: 15:12 (παν τὸ πληθος: _pan to plēthos_) was present throughout and involved in the final decision.

• **Consensus and Unanimity:** Through listening to the Spirit and God’s action, initially divergent opinions moved towards **consensus and unanimity** (όμοθυμαδόν_: homothumadon)_ cf. Acts 15:25 (“with one accord,” “unanimously,” or “with one mind/passion,”)

• **The Trinitarian Formula:** The authority of the decision was expressed in the formula: **“It has been decided by the Holy Spirit and by ourselves”** (Acts 15:28). This reflects a synergy between the community’s discernment and the ministerial responsibility of the leaders.

The Church is like a **choir**, a harmonic reality where reciprocal and ordered relations allow everyone to converge in “common mind” and love. Just as a choir requires different voices to create a single, beautiful harmony, the synodal Church requires the participation of all its members, each in their specific role, to walk together in fidelity to the Gospel.

**4 – Synodality in the Early Church**

·         Local synods and ecumenical councils

·         Role of bishops, deacons, and local communities

Monastic and communal discernment traditions

The Early Church understood synodality as the **“guarantee and incarnation”** of its fidelity to apostolic origins, manifesting as a “harmonic reality” where the People of God walked together.

As a student of theology, you must first master the vocabulary. The word **synod** comes from the Greek _synodos_ (_syn_ meaning “with” and _hodos_ meaning “path”), literally indicating “the path along which the People of God walk together”. Historically, “synod” and **council** (from the Latin _concilium_) were used as synonyms to describe a meeting, principally of bishops, gathered in Christ’s name to make decisions binding on the Church.

**Local Synods and Ecumenical Councils**

The history of the Church reveals a constant interplay between local and universal gatherings:

• **The Apostolic Prototype:** The “cornerstone” for all synods is the **Council of Jerusalem** (Acts 15). Here, the “apostles and elders” met to discern the Holy Spirit's will regarding Gentile converts, eventually reaching a consensus that they described as being decided “by the Holy Spirit and by ourselves”.

• **Local Synods:** In the first few centuries, these were remarkably frequent. By the 2nd century, at least 50 gatherings occurred in places like Palestine and North Africa, with at least 400 such meetings documented between the 2nd and 7th centuries. These local synods dealt with disciplinary and doctrinal issues, and while their decisions were primarily for their own regions, there were “striking instances”—such as North African councils regarding Donatism—where their rulings became binding on the whole Church.

• **Ecumenical (Universal) Councils:** These are “church-wide” meetings. The first was the **Council of Nicaea** in 325 AD, convoked by Emperor Constantine. These councils represent the “fullest and most solemn” expression of the Church's synodal nature at a universal level.

**Roles of Bishops, Deacons, and Local Communities**

In the early Church, governance functioned through two modes: **hierarchical** and **collegial**.

• **Bishops:** By the 2nd century, bishops emerged as the “overseers, guardians and leaders” and successors to the Apostles. In synodal gatherings, they traditionally hold the “deciding vote”. However, early tradition, such as that of Cyprian of Carthage, insisted on the principle of _nihil sine episcopo_ (nothing without the bishop) alongside _nihil sine consilio vestro_ (nothing without the council of presbyters and deacons) and _sine consensu plebis_ (without the consensus of the people).

• **Deacons and Presbyters:** In the Roman Synods from the 5th century, the Bishop of Rome’s council included not just bishops but also **presbyters** (priests) and **deacons**. These figures were essential collaborators, ensuring that the bishop did not act in isolation.

• **The Local Community (Sensus Fidelium):** This Latin term means the “faithful’s sense of the faith”. It is the conviction that the entire “People of God” has an “instinct of faith” that makes them “infallible in credendo” (in believing). In the first millennium, the “whole community” typically took part in local synods, with everyone playing an active role according to their specific position.

**Monastic and Communal Discernment Traditions**

**Discernment** is the process of listening to the Holy Spirit through the Word of God and the “signs of the times” to discover God’s call in a specific situation.

• **Monastic Influence:** Throughout the Middle Ages, synodal procedures were kept alive by monastic communities like the **Monks of Cluny** and the **Mendicant Orders**. At the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215, **800 abbots** attended, actually outnumbering the bishops two to one. These communities offered significant experiences of “communal discernment”.

• **Theologians:** From the 13th century, theologians emerged as a distinct class of teachers who became “indispensable” in formulating council decrees. They serve the Church by helping to understand faith in “sapiential, scientific and prophetic ways”.

• **Circular Dynamics:** Modern synodality seeks to revive this ancient communal spirit through a “circularity” between the **“all”** (the whole People of God), the **“some”** (the College of Bishops), and the **“one”** (the Bishop of Rome).

**Analogy for Understanding:** Think of the early Church as a **harmonic choir**. The **Bishop** may be the conductor who ensures the group stays in time and follows the score (the _depositum fidei_ or deposit of faith), but the “music” of the Church's life cannot exist without the **voices of the community** and the **harmony of the ministers** (deacons and priests). Synodality is the practice of ensuring all these different voices “converge in _agapé_ (love) and _homonoia_ (common mind)” to sing a single song of faith.
