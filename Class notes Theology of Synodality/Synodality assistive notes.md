**Synodality in the Early Church**

**Walking Together Before It Became a Concept (1st–6th Centuries)**

When we speak about synodality in the early Church, we must remember one basic fact: the early Christians did not first develop a theory of synodality and then practice it. They practiced synodality because they were a community gathered by the Holy Spirit, and only later did theological reflection emerge. Synodality, in this sense, is as old as the Church herself.

From the beginning, the Church understood herself not as a collection of isolated believers, but as a community on a shared journey, discerning together how to remain faithful to the Gospel in changing circumstances.

**Local Synods and Ecumenical Councils**

**From Community Discernment to Universal Communion**

In the apostolic and sub-apostolic periods (1st–2nd centuries), local Churches were led by bishops who were deeply embedded in their communities. When problems arose that affected more than one Church, bishops gathered to discern together. These gatherings were the earliest local synods.

A foundational biblical example is the Council of Jerusalem described in Acts 15, where apostles and elders met to decide whether Gentile converts must follow the Mosaic Law. The decision was not imposed by one leader alone. Instead, it emerged through discussion, listening to experience, and prayer, culminating in the famous formula: “It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us.” This phrase becomes the spiritual model for all later synodal discernment.

By the 2nd and 3rd centuries, regional synods had become a normal part of Church life. Bishops met to address issues such as heresy, discipline, and reconciliation of those who had denied the faith during persecution. For example, Cyprian of Carthage (d. 258) strongly defended the synodal principle. He insisted that no bishop should act alone in matters affecting the whole Church, famously stating that decisions should be taken “with the counsel of the clergy and the consent of the people.”

The ecumenical councils represent the highest expression of synodality in the early Church. These councils were convened when the unity of faith was threatened across the whole Christian world. The first and most famous was the Council of Nicaea, called by the Roman emperor Constantine the Great. Bishops from across the empire gathered to address the Arian controversy and to confess the divinity of Christ.

**Later councils followed, such as:**

•              Council of Constantinople, under Theodosius I,

•              Council of Ephesus,

•              Council of Chalcedon.

These councils show that synodality was not optional. When faith and communion were at stake, the Church gathered, listened, debated, prayed, and decided together.

**Role of Bishops, Deacons, and Local Communities**

**Authority as Service in Communion**

In the early Church, leadership was clearly structured, but it was never isolated from the community. The bishop was the focal point of unity in the local Church. However, bishops did not govern as solitary rulers. They were accountable to the faith of the Church and to one another.

Figures such as Ignatius of Antioch (early 2nd century) emphasized the importance of unity around the bishop, but always in communion with presbyters, deacons, and the faithful. For Ignatius, the bishop presided over the community as a sign of Christ’s presence, not as an authoritarian figure.

Deacons played a crucial synodal role, especially in service and mediation. The Acts of the Apostles (Acts 6) shows the community participating in the selection of deacons to address practical needs. Deacons served at the intersection of liturgy, charity, and administration, ensuring that decisions reflected the lived reality of the people.

The local community itself was not passive. The faithful participated actively in worship, discernment, and even in the recognition of leaders. In many places, bishops were chosen with the consent of clergy and people. This reflects a deep conviction of the early Church: the Holy Spirit speaks through the whole community, not only through office-holders.

This communal dimension is evident in the writings of Augustine of Hippo, who described himself as “a Christian with you and a bishop for you.” His words express the synodal balance between authority and fraternity.

**Monastic and Communal Discernment Traditions**

**Schools of Synodality in Daily Life**

From the 4th century onward, monasticism emerged as a powerful spiritual movement that deeply influenced synodal practice. Monasteries were communities organized around prayer, work, and communal discernment. Though not synods in the formal sense, they embodied the spirit of synodality.

One of the most influential figures was Anthony the Great, often called the father of monasticism. While he lived as a hermit, his followers formed communities where decisions were taken through listening and spiritual guidance.

Even more significant was Basil of Caesarea, who organized monastic life around community rather than isolation. Basil insisted that discernment should be communal and that obedience was not blind submission, but shared listening to God’s will.

In the Western Church, Benedict of Nursia (6th century) gave monastic synodality a lasting structure through the Rule of St. Benedict. One striking instruction in the Rule is that the abbot must consult the whole community, even the youngest monks, because God may speak through anyone. This is a profound expression of the sensus fidelium long before the term existed.

Monastic life thus became a laboratory of synodality, preserving practices of consultation, listening, and discernment even when broader ecclesial structures became more centralized.

**Concluding Perspective**

In the early Church, synodality was not an abstract idea but a lived reality. Local synods and ecumenical councils expressed communion among Churches. Bishops, deacons, and communities shared responsibility for discernment and mission. Monastic traditions safeguarded communal listening and obedience as spiritual practices.

This early experience forms the foundation for all later reflections on synodality. When the Church today speaks about walking together, she is not inventing something new. She is remembering how she once lived, prayed, decided, and believed together under the guidance of the Holy Spirit.

**Synodality Through the Middle Ages to Modern Times**

**Development, Decline, and Recovery of a Living Tradition**

To understand synodality from the Middle Ages to modern times, we must avoid the mistake of thinking in terms of loss and invention. The Church did not abandon synodality and later rediscover it by accident. Rather, synodal practice developed, weakened in certain forms, and was later recovered as historical conditions changed. The Second Vatican Council stands at the end of this long process, not as a rupture, but as a retrieval.

**Development and Gradual Decline of Synodal Practices**

In the first millennium, synods were a normal and frequent way of governing the Church. Bishops gathered regularly at local and regional levels, and ecumenical councils were convened to resolve doctrinal and disciplinary questions. This practice continued into the early Middle Ages, but the conditions that supported it began to change dramatically after the collapse of the Western Roman Empire in 476 AD, when the last Western emperor, Romulus Augustulus, was deposed.

With the fall of imperial administration, Europe became politically fragmented. Roads deteriorated, communication became dangerous, and stable civil structures disappeared. In this context, bishops could no longer meet frequently or easily. Synods did not disappear, but they became irregular and heavily dependent on political support. Under rulers such as Charlemagne, synods were still convoked, often as part of wider imperial reform efforts aimed at disciplining clergy and unifying Christian practice. This shows that synodality was still valued, but it now relied on external stability rather than being an organic and frequent ecclesial practice.

As the Middle Ages progressed, the Church also faced internal challenges that contributed to the gradual decline of synodal life. Local churches were increasingly affected by feudal systems, where bishops were tied to land, power, and noble families. In such a context, communal discernment gave way to governance shaped by hierarchy and privilege. Synodal practices narrowed, not because they were considered wrong, but because the Church was adapting to survive and govern in a fractured world.

**Centralization, Reform Movements, and Major Challenges**

A decisive turning point came in the 11th century with the Gregorian Reform, associated above all with Pope Gregory VII. The Church at this time was deeply compromised by simony, clerical marriage, and especially by the control of Church offices by secular rulers. Emperors such as Henry IV claimed the right to appoint bishops, reducing the Church to a political instrument.

To free the Church from this domination, Gregory VII and his successors deliberately strengthened papal authority and centralized governance. This reform was necessary for the Church’s spiritual freedom, but it had an important side effect. Local synods lost influence, and decision-making increasingly flowed from Rome. Synodality was not rejected, but hierarchy and law became the dominant modes of governance.

This centralizing tendency continued into the 13th century, particularly under Pope Innocent III, one of the most powerful medieval popes. Innocent III convened the Fourth Lateran Council, which remains one of the most significant synodal events in Church history. Bishops from across Christendom gathered to reform discipline, clarify doctrine, and regulate sacramental life. Yet the strong leadership of the papacy during and after this council meant that synodal participation at local levels continued to weaken. Synodality survived at the universal level, while becoming fragile at the grassroots.

The late Middle Ages confronted the Church with an even greater crisis: the Western Schism (1378–1417), when rival popes claimed authority from Rome and Avignon. This crisis shook confidence in papal leadership itself. The Church responded by convening the Council of Constance, which successfully restored unity and elected Martin V. During this period, some theologians developed the theory of conciliarism, arguing that councils should have authority over the pope. While this theory was later rejected, the Church never rejected councils or synods themselves. Instead, she clarified that synodality must function within communion, not in opposition to papal primacy.

The 16th century introduced new challenges with the Protestant Reformation initiated by Martin Luther. Western Christianity fractured, and political rulers asserted control over religion. In response, the Catholic Church convened the Council of Trent. Trent was a synodal act of the highest order, but it operated within a defensive ecclesial mindset. Emphasis was placed on doctrinal clarity, clerical discipline, and centralized oversight. This further reduced space for synodal participation at the local level, as unity and orthodoxy became urgent priorities.

In the 19th century, the Church faced the rise of nationalism, secular ideologies, and the loss of temporal power, especially after the unification of Italy and the fall of the Papal States. The First Vatican Council, convened under Pius IX, responded by defining papal primacy and infallibility. This was a historically conditioned response to political and cultural threats. While it strengthened papal authority, it also deepened the imbalance between authority and participation.

**Recovery of Synodal Practices Before Vatican II**

Despite centuries of centralization, synodality never disappeared from the Church’s life. It survived in monastic communities, where communal discernment and obedience were practiced daily. It survived in reform movements that called the Church back to the Gospel. It survived in ecumenical councils that were convened whenever unity was seriously threatened.

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, a quiet but powerful recovery began. Biblical scholarship rediscovered the Church as the People of God. Patristic scholars returned to the writings of the early Fathers and uncovered the synodal practices of the first centuries. The liturgical movement emphasized active participation of the faithful in worship. These developments reshaped ecclesiology long before Vatican II began.

By the time the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965) was convoked by John XXIII, the theological ground had already been prepared. Vatican II was able to retrieve synodality not as a novelty, but as a dimension of the Church’s tradition that had been overshadowed by historical necessity. The Council rebalanced authority and communion, restored the vision of the People of God, and reopened structures of participation.

**Concluding Insight**

Synodality through the Middle Ages to modern times followed a path shaped by history. It developed naturally in the early Church, declined in practice as the Church faced political collapse, reform struggles, and doctrinal crises, and was gradually recovered as the Church rediscovered her communal identity. The Second Vatican Council stands not as a break with the past, but as the moment when the Church, once again, learned to walk together.

**Vatican II and the Renewal of the Synodal Vision**

**(1962–1965: A Turning Point in the Life of the Church)**

When we speak of Vatican II and synodality, we are not talking about a sudden invention. We are talking about a recovery of balance in the Church’s self-understanding after centuries of strong centralization. The Second Vatican Council did not reject papal authority or hierarchy. Instead, it re-situated authority within communion, rediscovered the People of God, and reopened space for participation, dialogue, and shared responsibility.

The Council took place between 1962 and 1965, in a world marked by the aftermath of two World Wars, the collapse of old empires, the rise of nation-states, decolonization, scientific progress, and deep cultural change. The Church could no longer speak as if she were standing above history. She had to learn again how to walk with humanity, listen, and discern.

**Lumen Gentium: People of God and Collegiality**

The most important document for understanding the synodal vision of Vatican II is Second Vatican Council’s Dogmatic Constitution on the Church, Lumen Gentium.

Before Vatican II, many theology manuals described the Church primarily as a perfect society, structured hierarchically, with authority flowing from the top downward. Vatican II did not deny this structure, but it deliberately changed the starting point. Instead of beginning with hierarchy, Lumen Gentium begins with the mystery of the Church and then introduces the Church as the People of God.

This shift is crucial. By calling the Church the People of God, the Council affirmed that all the baptized share a common dignity, even though they have different roles. Bishops, priests, religious, and laity are not separate classes of Christians. They are members of one pilgrim people journeying through history.

This teaching draws directly from Scripture, especially from 1 Peter 2:9 and 1 Corinthians 12. It also retrieves early Church experience, where faith was lived communally before it was highly institutionalized.

Within this vision, Lumen Gentium also renews the doctrine of episcopal collegiality. The bishops are not isolated rulers of their dioceses, nor are they mere delegates of the pope. Together, as successors of the apostles, they form a college, united with and under the Bishop of Rome.

This teaching corrects an imbalance that had developed after First Vatican Council, which had strongly emphasized papal primacy and infallibility in response to political threats such as nationalism and the loss of the Papal States under Pius IX. Vatican II does not contradict Vatican I. Instead, it completes it by restoring the communal dimension of authority.

A concrete example of this renewal is the way bishops at Vatican II debated, amended, and voted on texts together, something rarely seen since the early councils. Authority was exercised through listening, dialogue, and shared responsibility, which is the heart of synodality.

**Gaudium et Spes: Dialogue with the World**

If Lumen Gentium renews how the Church understands herself, Gaudium et Spes renews how the Church understands her relationship with the world.

This document begins with a sentence that was revolutionary in tone:

“The joys and the hopes, the griefs and the anxieties of the people of this age… are the joys and hopes, the griefs and anxieties of the followers of Christ.”

With this, Vatican II clearly rejects a defensive or fortress mentality. The Church does not stand outside history judging it from above. She stands within history, listening, discerning, and serving.

The historical context matters here. Europe had experienced the horrors of World War I and World War II, the Holocaust, atomic warfare, and the collapse of old imperial structures under rulers such as Franz Joseph I and Wilhelm II. The credibility of authority itself was deeply wounded. In such a world, the Church could not speak credibly without first listening.

Gaudium et Spes introduces the idea of reading the signs of the times. This means discerning where God is already at work in human history, including in social movements, scientific progress, struggles for justice, and even in human questioning and doubt.

This approach is profoundly synodal. Dialogue replaces monologue. Discernment replaces condemnation. The Church learns not only to teach but also to learn.

A concrete example of this synodal attitude is the Church’s engagement with modern issues such as human rights, labor, peace, and development. The Council Fathers, under popes John XXIII and later Paul VI, chose conversation over confrontation.

This dialogical posture becomes foundational for later synodal practices, including ecumenical and interreligious dialogue.

**Restoring Participatory Structures in the Church**

Vatican II did not stop at ideas. It insisted that communion and participation must be institutionally expressed.

One of the most concrete outcomes of Vatican II was the restoration and creation of participatory structures in the Church. These include diocesan synods, episcopal conferences, pastoral councils, and parish councils.

Historically, many of these structures had existed in the early Church but weakened over time due to centralization. Vatican II encouraged their revival to ensure that discernment does not remain abstract.

A clear example is the establishment of the Synod of Bishops by Paul VI in 1965 through Apostolica Sollicitudo. This was meant to make episcopal collegiality a permanent reality, not something that happens only during councils.

At the local level, parish and diocesan councils allowed laity, religious, and clergy to share responsibility for pastoral planning. While these bodies are consultative, Vatican II insisted that consultation is theologically meaningful, not merely administrative.

This restoration of structures expresses a key synodal insight: the Holy Spirit speaks not only through ordained ministers, but through the whole People of God. Listening becomes an ecclesial duty, not a courtesy.

**Concluding Perspective**

Taken together, Lumen Gentium and Gaudium et Spes show that Vatican II renewed the Church’s synodal vision by re-centering her identity as the People of God, rebalancing authority through collegiality, opening dialogue with the modern world, and restoring structures of participation.

Vatican II did not weaken the Church. It made her more faithful to her own tradition by allowing her once again to listen, walk, and serve together.

**Magisterial Foundations: The Synod of Bishops**

**Collegiality Made Permanent in the Life of the Church**

After the Second Vatican Council, the Church faced a decisive question: how could the synodal and collegial vision of Vatican II remain alive once the Council had ended? The bishops had experienced genuine communion, shared responsibility, and collective discernment during the Council sessions from 1962 to 1965. The danger was that, once the Council closed, the Church would return to a purely centralized mode of governance. The answer to this concern was the creation of the Synod of Bishops, a permanent structure at the service of communion between the Pope and the bishops.

This development belongs to the magisterial reception of Vatican II, not to later pastoral experimentation.

**Paul VI and Apostolica Sollicitudo (1965)**

**From a Council Event to a Permanent Synodal Structure**

The Synod of Bishops was established by Paul VI on 15 September 1965, just weeks before the close of Vatican II, through the motu proprio Apostolica Sollicitudo.

The historical context is crucial. Paul VI had personally guided the second half of Vatican II after the death of John XXIII in 1963. He was deeply convinced that the experience of episcopal collegiality during the Council should not end with the Council itself. Apostolica Sollicitudo therefore established the Synod of Bishops as a stable institution, not an occasional gathering.

In this document, Paul VI clearly states that the Synod of Bishops exists to:

•              assist the Pope in the governance of the universal Church,

•              strengthen unity between the Pope and the bishops,

•              promote shared reflection on matters concerning faith, morals, and pastoral action.

What is important here is the balance. The Synod of Bishops is not a parliament and not a council superior to the Pope. Paul VI explicitly places it under papal authority, while at the same time affirming it as a real expression of episcopal collegiality. This directly reflects Vatican II’s teaching in Lumen Gentium that bishops act with and under Peter, not independently of him.

The creation of the Synod of Bishops can be seen as the institutional answer to the conciliar experience: the Church needed a way to keep walking together after the Council.

**The Evolution of Synodal Processes**

**From Consultation to Discernment**

In its early years, the Synod of Bishops functioned primarily as a consultative body. The first Synod, held in 1967, focused on preserving and strengthening Catholic faith in the modern world. Subsequent synods addressed topics such as priesthood, evangelization, catechesis, and the family.

Under John Paul II (1978–2005), the Synod of Bishops became an important instrument for shaping global Catholic teaching. For example:

•              the 1980 Synod on the Family influenced Familiaris Consortio,

•              the 1985 Extraordinary Synod assessed the reception of Vatican II,

•              the 1997 Synod for America shaped missionary priorities for the continent.

During this period, synods were highly structured and largely centered on bishops’ interventions. The process emphasized consultation, but discernment was still tightly controlled by central structures. This reflects the Church’s cautious approach in the decades immediately following Vatican II, as she sought stability after rapid change.

A further development occurred under Benedict XVI (2005–2013), who emphasized the theological depth of synodal reflection, especially the relationship between Scripture, Tradition, and the Magisterium. While the structure of synods remained largely the same, there was growing awareness that synodality involved not only bishops speaking, but also listening.

A significant shift took place under Pope Francis. Francis explicitly described synodality as “the path which God expects of the Church in the third millennium.” Under his pontificate, synodal processes expanded beyond meetings in Rome to include wide consultation of the People of God, diocesan listening processes, and spiritual conversation. This development did not change the nature of the Synod of Bishops, but deepened its synodal character.

**Bishops in Communion with the Pope**

**Authority as Shared Responsibility**

At the heart of the Synod of Bishops is a specific understanding of authority. Bishops are not representatives of local opinions, nor are they independent leaders acting apart from the universal Church. They are successors of the apostles, entrusted with pastoral responsibility for their local Churches, and at the same time members of the worldwide episcopal college.

This understanding is rooted in Vatican II’s teaching and made concrete through the Synod of Bishops. When bishops gather in synod, they do so in communion with the Pope, who presides over the synodal process. The Pope listens, discerns, and ultimately confirms decisions, safeguarding unity and fidelity to the Gospel.

A concrete example of this communion can be seen in how post-synodal apostolic exhortations are issued. After synodal discussions, the Pope does not simply repeat what was said. He exercises discernment, integrating the synodal process into the Church’s magisterial teaching. This shows that synodality does not weaken papal authority, but enriches it through shared listening.

**Historically, this model avoids two extremes:**

•              on one side, conciliarism, which places authority above the Pope,

•              on the other side, isolation, where authority is exercised without consultation.

The Synod of Bishops stands between these extremes as a magisterial expression of communion.

**Concluding Perspective**

The establishment of the Synod of Bishops by Paul VI through Apostolica Sollicitudo represents a decisive moment in the Church’s modern history. It ensured that the collegial and synodal vision of Vatican II would not remain a temporary experience but become a permanent dimension of Church life. Over time, synodal processes have evolved from structured consultation to deeper communal discernment, especially under Pope Francis. Throughout this evolution, one principle has remained constant: bishops exercise their ministry with and under the Pope, in communion, for the good of the whole Church.

This magisterial foundation makes it clear that synodality is not a pastoral fashion. It is a stable and authoritative way in which the Church lives her apostolic responsibility in history.

**Pope Francis and the Synodal Church**

**Synodality as a Way of Being Church in the Third Millennium**

When Pope Francis speaks about synodality, he is not proposing a new programme or an administrative reform. He is describing what he believes the Church is called to be at this moment in history. For Pope Francis, synodality is not simply about synods. It is about how the Church lives, listens, decides, and serves. His teaching emerges from Vatican II, but it responds directly to the pastoral, cultural, and ecclesial challenges of the twenty-first century.

Francis became pope in 2013, at a time when the Church was facing declining credibility, internal polarization, clerical abuse scandals, and growing distance between Church leadership and ordinary believers. Against this background, he proposed synodality as a path of conversion, not merely a method of consultation.

**The 50th Anniversary Address of the Synod of Bishops (2015)**

**Synodality as the Path God Expects of the Church**

A decisive moment in Pope Francis’s teaching on synodality came on 17 October 2015, when he addressed the Synod of Bishops on the 50th anniversary of its institution by Paul VI in 1965.

In this address, Pope Francis made a statement that has since become foundational for contemporary ecclesiology. He said that “synodality is the path which God expects of the Church in the third millennium.” This was not rhetorical language. Francis was making a theological claim: that walking together, listening together, and discerning together belong to the very identity of the Church.

In this address, Francis carefully located synodality within tradition. He recalled that the Synod of Bishops was created as a fruit of Vatican II’s teaching on collegiality, but he also acknowledged that, for many years, synods functioned mainly as consultative meetings of bishops in Rome. What was missing, he argued, was a deeper involvement of the whole People of God.

Francis described the Church as an “inverted pyramid”. At the top, in this image, are the People of God. Below them are bishops, and at the bottom is the Pope himself, whose role is to serve communion. This image directly challenges worldly models of authority without denying hierarchical structure. Authority remains, but it is exercised as service, not domination.

**Listening, Discernment, and Encounter**

**Core Spiritual Themes of Francis’s Synodal Vision**

At the heart of Pope Francis’s understanding of synodality are three closely connected spiritual attitudes: listening, discernment, and encounter.

Listening, for Francis, is not a courtesy or a strategy. It is a theological act. The Church listens first to the Word of God, then to the People of God, and finally to the cries of the poor and the signs of the times. In his synodal teaching, Francis often reminds the Church that the Holy Spirit speaks not only through official documents or leaders, but also through the lived faith of ordinary believers.

This conviction has concrete consequences. Under Francis, synodal processes have expanded beyond bishops’ meetings to include diocesan consultations, listening sessions with lay people, religious, women, young people, and those who feel marginalized or wounded by the Church. The Synod on the Family (2014–2015) and later the Synod on Synodality (2021–2024) illustrate this shift clearly.

Discernment is the second key theme. Francis insists that synodality is not about majority votes or ideological debates. It is about seeking the will of God together. Drawing deeply from Ignatian spirituality, Francis emphasizes patience, prayer, silence, and attentiveness to the movements of the Spirit. He frequently warns that without discernment, synods become parliaments and the Church becomes polarized.

Encounter is the third essential dimension. For Francis, synodality cannot be lived from a distance. It requires proximity, dialogue, and a willingness to be changed by meeting others. This includes encounter within the Church, between bishops and people, but also encounter with the world, with other Christian traditions, and with other religions. In this sense, synodality is inseparable from mission.

**Clericalism and the Need for Co-Responsibility**

**A Conversion of Authority and Participation**

One of the strongest and most consistent themes in Pope Francis’s teaching is his critique of clericalism. He identifies clericalism not simply as moral failure, but as a distortion of ecclesiology. Clericalism occurs when ordained ministry is separated from service, when authority becomes self-referential, and when the laity are treated as passive recipients rather than active subjects of mission.

Francis repeatedly states that clericalism is the enemy of synodality. It blocks listening, silences the Spirit, and creates a Church that speaks but does not hear. This critique is rooted in history. After centuries of centralization, especially following First Vatican Council, the Church developed habits where authority was exercised without sufficient consultation. Francis does not deny the need for authority, but he calls for its conversion.

In response, he emphasizes co-responsibility, grounded in baptism. All the baptized share responsibility for the Church’s life and mission, even though their roles differ. Bishops and priests do not lose authority in a synodal Church. Rather, their authority is exercised in communion, transparency, and accountability.

A concrete example of this is Francis’s encouragement of parish councils, diocesan synods, and episcopal conferences to function meaningfully, not symbolically. Another example is his insistence that women and lay experts be included in synodal processes, not as observers but as contributors to discernment.

**Concluding Perspective**

Pope Francis’s vision of a synodal Church is not an abstract theory. It is a response to concrete historical challenges: loss of trust, cultural change, internal division, and the Church’s mission in a complex world. His 2015 address marks a turning point in the reception of Vatican II, moving synodality from principle to practice.

For Francis, a synodal Church is one that listens attentively, discerns prayerfully, encounters others humbly, rejects clericalism, and embraces shared responsibility. In this way, synodality becomes not merely a structure, but a spiritual style of Church life, faithful to tradition and open to the future.

**Synodality and the Sensus Fidei**

**Listening to the Spirit in the Whole People of God**

When the Church speaks today about synodality, one concept becomes absolutely central: the sensus fidei. Without this concept, synodality risks becoming either a sociological exercise or a parliamentary process. With it, synodality becomes what it truly is: a spiritual process of discerning the voice of the Holy Spirit in the believing community.

The idea of sensus fidei is not modern. It belongs to the Church’s deepest tradition and has been lived long before it was precisely named.

Meaning of Sensus Fidei and Sensus Fidelium

**From Biblical Experience to Theological Language**

The Latin expression sensus fidei means “the sense of faith.” It refers to a spiritual capacity given by the Holy Spirit to believers, enabling them to recognize what truly belongs to the faith handed down from the apostles. Closely related is the expression sensus fidelium, which means “the sense of the faithful,” that is, the shared faith-consciousness of the whole Church.

Although the Latin terms developed later, the reality itself is already present in Scripture. In the New Testament, faith is never presented as a purely private possession. Saint Paul speaks of believers as those who have received the Spirit, who enables them to discern what comes from God. Saint John writes that the faithful have an “anointing” from the Holy One that teaches them. This biblical conviction lies behind the Church’s later reflection on the sensus fidei.

In the early centuries, this reality was lived instinctively. When controversies arose about doctrine or practice, the Church did not rely only on isolated authorities. She looked to the faith that was being lived, prayed, and confessed across the Christian communities. A famous expression of this conviction comes from Augustine of Hippo (4th–5th century), who insisted that bishops must listen to the faith of the Church, because they themselves belong to the believing community. Augustine described himself as “a Christian with you and a bishop for you,” capturing the balance between authority and shared faith.

Later, in the 19th century, the English theologian John Henry Newman played a decisive role in articulating this idea more clearly. Reflecting on the Arian crisis of the 4th century, Newman observed that at certain moments the faithful preserved orthodox belief even when many bishops hesitated. His work helped the Church recognize that the faith of the people is not merely devotional, but theologically significant.

**The Role of the Whole People of God**

**Baptismal Dignity and Shared Responsibility**

The Second Vatican Council brought this long tradition into clear focus. In Lumen Gentium, the Council taught that the whole People of God share in Christ’s prophetic mission. This means that all the baptized, not only clergy or theologians, participate in the Church’s reception and transmission of faith.

This teaching was shaped by the historical context of the mid-20th century. After two World Wars and the collapse of old imperial structures under rulers such as Wilhelm II and Franz Joseph I, authority itself was widely questioned. Vatican II responded not by weakening doctrine, but by rediscovering the Church as a communion, rather than a purely top-down institution.

The Council insisted that the sensus fidei belongs to the whole People of God when they remain faithful to the Gospel, united with their pastors, and attentive to the Spirit. This does not mean that every opinion expressed by believers is an expression of the sensus fidei. Rather, it means that the lived faith of the Church as a whole has a receptive capacity for truth.

In a synodal Church, this role of the faithful becomes visible through consultation, listening processes, and shared discernment. Bishops and pastors are not replaced by the people, but they are called to listen seriously to the faith experience of those entrusted to their care.

**Discerning the Voice of the Spirit in the Community**

**Between Authority and Consensus**

Discerning the voice of the Spirit is the most delicate and demanding aspect of synodality. The Church has always known that not every voice is the voice of the Spirit. Discernment requires prayer, patience, fidelity to Scripture and Tradition, and communion with legitimate authority.

The biblical model for this process remains the Council of Jerusalem described in Acts 15. The apostles and elders listened to lived experience, debated openly, and finally reached a decision that they attributed not to human strategy but to the Holy Spirit: “It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us.” This phrase captures the heart of ecclesial discernment.

Historically, the Church has applied this model in ecumenical councils. At Nicaea in 325, convened by Constantine the Great, the bishops did not simply impose an imperial solution to the Arian controversy. They listened to the faith of the Churches and confessed what had been believed and worshipped, especially in baptism and liturgy. The council’s authority rested not only on episcopal agreement, but on the reception of its teaching by the faithful.

In modern times, this dynamic has been strongly emphasized by Pope Francis. Francis repeatedly insists that synodality begins by listening to the People of God, because the Spirit speaks through their lived faith. At the same time, he warns against confusing discernment with opinion polls or ideological pressure. True discernment takes place when bishops, theologians, and faithful listen together, each according to their vocation, under the guidance of the Spirit.

A concrete example of this process can be seen in the recent synodal consultations, where local Churches gathered experiences from parishes, families, young people, and marginalized groups. These voices were not treated as final judgments, but as essential data for ecclesial discernment, to be weighed prayerfully by pastors in communion with the universal Church.

**Concluding Perspective**

Synodality and the sensus fidei are inseparable. Without the sensus fidei, synodality becomes procedural. Without synodality, the sensus fidei remains unheard. Together, they express a Church that trusts the Spirit at work in the whole People of God, while safeguarding unity through apostolic leadership.

In this vision, the Church does not choose between authority and participation. She lives from their communion. The Spirit who guides the bishops also dwells in the faithful, and synodality is the way the Church learns, again and again, to listen to that Spirit together.

**The Synod on Synodality (2021–2024)**

**A Global Process of Listening, Discernment, and Mission**

The Synod on Synodality, held between 2021 and 2024, marks one of the most significant ecclesial processes since the Second Vatican Council. It was not conceived as a single meeting in Rome, but as a worldwide journey of the whole Church, involving dioceses, episcopal conferences, continental assemblies, and finally the universal Church. Under the leadership of Pope Francis, this synod sought to answer a fundamental question: How can the Church live communion, participation, and mission more faithfully in today’s world?

This synod must be understood as the mature fruit of Vatican II’s ecclesiology and of the Synod of Bishops established in 1965 by Paul VI. What is new is not the idea of synodality, but the scale, depth, and method by which it was practiced.

**The Global Listening Process**

**From Rome to the Peripheries and Back**

The Synod on Synodality began formally in October 2021, but its heart was the global listening process that unfolded at the local level. For the first time in the history of synods, Pope Francis insisted that the synod must begin not with bishops’ discussions in Rome, but with listening to the People of God in their own contexts.

At the diocesan stage, bishops were asked to convene listening sessions in parishes, religious communities, schools, seminaries, and ecclesial movements. Lay faithful, women, youth, clergy, religious, and even those who felt marginalized or distant from the Church were invited to speak. In many places, people shared experiences of joy, wounds, exclusion, hope, and longing for a more listening Church. This stage reflected Pope Francis’s conviction that the Holy Spirit speaks through the lived faith of ordinary believers.

Theologically, this process was rooted in the conviction that the sensus fidei belongs to the whole People of God. Historically, it represented a shift from synods as events to synodality as a process. The listening phase echoed the early Church practice described in Acts 15, where the apostles listened first to experience before discerning together.

**Communion, Participation, and Mission**

**The Three Interconnected Themes**

From the global listening process, three core themes emerged and were explicitly named by Pope Francis as the framework of the synod: communion, participation, and mission. These are not three separate topics, but three dimensions of one ecclesial reality.

Communion refers to the Church as a people united in Christ, rooted in the Trinity. Many participants expressed a deep desire for unity that does not erase diversity. They spoke about polarization within the Church, divisions between clergy and laity, and tensions between different theological or cultural groups. The synod identified these fractures as obstacles to authentic communion, calling the Church to healing, reconciliation, and mutual trust.

Participation emerged as one of the most pressing concerns. Across continents, people spoke about feeling unheard or excluded from decision-making. Particular attention was given to the role of women, youth, and lay people. Pope Francis repeatedly stressed that participation flows from baptism, not from clerical status. This theme directly challenges clericalism and calls for renewed co-responsibility in the life of the Church.

Mission gave direction to the entire process. The synod consistently affirmed that synodality is not inward-looking. A Church that listens and walks together becomes more missionary. Many local Churches emphasized that credibility in evangelization depends on humility, dialogue, and closeness to people’s real lives. In this sense, the synod echoed the missionary vision of Vatican II’s Gaudium et Spes.

**Diocesan, Continental, and Universal Stages**

**Discernment at Multiple Levels**

After the diocesan listening phase, the synodal process moved to the continental stage between 2022 and 2023. Episcopal conferences gathered and synthesized the voices from their regions. Continental assemblies were held in Africa, Asia, Europe, Latin America and the Caribbean, North America, and Oceania.

These assemblies revealed both shared concerns and regional differences. For example, Churches in Africa and Asia emphasized mission, inculturation, and interreligious dialogue, while Churches in Europe and North America spoke more about secularization, trust, and institutional reform. This stage demonstrated that synodality respects diversity while seeking unity.

The process then moved to the universal stage, culminating in two sessions of the Synod of Bishops in Rome in October 2023 and October 2024. What distinguished these sessions from earlier synods was not only the content, but the method. Bishops were joined by non-bishop members, including women and lay men and women, with full speaking rights. Spiritual conversation, silence, and prayer shaped the rhythm of discernment.

Throughout this stage, Pope Francis emphasized that the synod was not about producing quick solutions, but about fostering a culture of synodality that would continue beyond the synod itself.

**Concluding Perspective**

The Synod on Synodality (2021–2024) represents a historic moment in the life of the Church. It translated the vision of Vatican II into a global ecclesial practice. Through a worldwide listening process, it affirmed communion as a gift to be healed, participation as a baptismal responsibility, and mission as the Church’s reason for existing.

Above all, this synod showed that synodality is not a temporary project or a reform strategy. It is a way of being Church, rooted in tradition, attentive to the Spirit, and open to the future. Under the guidance of Pope Francis, the Church learned once again to listen, discern, and walk together.
