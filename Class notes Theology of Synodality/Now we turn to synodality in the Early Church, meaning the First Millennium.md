Now we turn to synodality in the Early Church, meaning the First Millennium:
1. Local synods and ecumenical councils
From the start, synodality guaranteed fidelity to apostolic origins and Catholic calling. It took shape in assemblies at different levels.
Local and provincial synods began in the third century. They dealt with disciplinary, liturgical, and doctrinal matters. Bishops led them, but decisions expressed communion with all Churches through shared letters and canons (para 28).
The first Ecumenical Council met at Nicea in 325, convoked by the Emperor. Bishops from the East attended, along with legates from the Bishop of Rome. Its creed and canons held normative value for the whole Church. Later councils followed this pattern, clarifying authority through agreement among heads of Churches, cooperation of the Bishop of Rome, and harmony with earlier teaching (para 29).
Only bishops participated in ecumenical councils of the First Millennium, while diocesan and provincial synods shaped most procedures (para 30).
Example: Nicea (325) set the model for universal synodal exercise of the Lord’s authority in guiding the People of God.
2. Role of bishops, deacons, and local communities
Bishops held central responsibility. Ignatius of Antioch (early second century) described the college of presbyters as the bishop’s council. The eucharistic assembly under the bishop showed clearest communion (para 25). https://d.docs.live.net/83d933a67ebb0b1a/Documents/To%20understand%20local%20and%20provincial%20synods.docx
Cyprian of Carthage (third century) stated the principle: nothing without the bishop (nihil sine episcopo), nothing without the council of presbyters and deacons (nihil sine consilio vestro), and nothing without the consent of the people (et sine consensu plebis). The episcopate is one, shared undivided among bishops (para 25).this principle
In local synods, the whole community participated in principle, each group according to its role. Provincial synods included bishops mainly, but presbyters could contribute. Deacons appear in Cyprian’s council alongside presbyters (para 30).
Local communities thus shared actively, especially at diocesan level, while bishops carried synodal authority.
3. Monastic and communal discernment traditions
The document notes monastic presence lightly in the First Millennium. Presbyters and monks could be invited to contribute in provincial synods (para 30).
Communal discernment appeared in the whole community gathering for local synods, grouped by roles, following Apostolic Tradition yet influenced by local culture (para 30).
Stronger monastic examples emerge later: monks of Cluny and mendicant orders helped revitalise synodal practice in the Middle Ages (para 33, shifting to Second Millennium).
In the Early Church, discernment remained tied to ordered communal participation under bishops rather than separate monastic traditions.
These practices show how the Early Church lived synodality as ordered communion, with bishops at the centre yet involving presbyters, deacons, monks when invited, and the people’s consent.

Synodality in the Second Millennium.
 1: Different paths emerge in East and West
From the start of the Second Millennium, synodal ways gradually diverged between East and West. This happened especially after the break in communion between Constantinople and Rome (11th century), and when Islamic rule affected patriarchal territories like Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem (para 31).
The Church no longer followed one unified pattern. East and West each adapted synodality to their contexts.
 2: Synodality continues in the Eastern Churches
In the Eastern Churches, practice stayed rooted in the patristic tradition. Patriarchal and metropolitan synods remained active, alongside special synods involving patriarchs and metropolitans.
In Constantinople, a permanent synod (Σύνοδος ένδημούσα) grew stronger – a model known since the fourth century in places like Alexandria and Antioch. These held regular meetings for liturgical, canonical, and practical matters, adapting through Byzantine and later Ottoman times.
Today, permanent synods persist in Orthodox Churches (para 31).
Eastern synodality thus preserved continuity with ancient forms.
 3: Gregorian reform strengthens papal primacy in the Catholic Church
In the Catholic Church, the Gregorian reform and fight for Church freedom (libertas Ecclesiae) affirmed the pope’s primatial authority. This liberated bishops from imperial control but risked weakening local Church identity if misunderstood (para 32).
Roman synods served as the pope’s council since the fifth century, including bishops, presbyters, and deacons. Medieval councils expanded to represent Western Christendom, involving bishops, abbots, religious superiors, civil rulers (emperors, kings), and experts (periti). The pope or legate presided.
 4: Local synods change and monastic orders revitalise practice
At local level, continuing Charlemagne’s influence, synods shifted from purely ecclesiastical to royal or national forms, with bishops under kingly presidency (para 33).
Yet revitalisation occurred throughout the Middle Ages. Monks of Cluny exemplified this; cathedral chapters sustained it; new religious communities, especially mendicant orders, kept synodal ways alive (para 33).
Religious life thus injected fresh energy into communal discernment.
 5: Western Schism leads to Konstanz and rejects conciliarism
At the late Middle Ages’ end, the Western Schism (1378–1417) saw multiple papal claimants. The Council of Konstanz (1414–1418) resolved it using emergency canon law and elected a legitimate pope (para 34).
Conciliarism emerged here, seeking permanent council superiority over papal primacy. The Church later judged this incompatible with Tradition.
It teaches a lesson: schism threats persist; continual reform in head and members requires synodal practice faithful to Tradition, with papal primacy as guarantee (para 34).
 6: Council of Trent responds to Reformation
A century later, facing Protestant Reformation crisis, the Council of Trent gathered. This first modern-type council excluded voting by princes’ legates; participants included bishops and religious superiors (para 35).
Trent mandated annual diocesan synods and triennial provincial ones to implement reforms Church-wide.
Examples shine brightly: Saint Charles Borromeo convoked five provincial and eleven diocesan synods in Milan; Saint Turibius of Mogrovejo held three provincial and thirteen diocesan in America; Mexico saw three provincial councils that century (para 35).
Post-Trent synods, shaped by era’s culture, focused on enacting norms rather than broad People of God participation. Apologetic responses to Reformation accentuated hierarchical views, seeing bishops and pope as teaching Church (Ecclesia docens) and others as learning (Ecclesia discens) (para 35).
 7: Protestant communities develop distinct synodal approaches
Reformation-born communities promote synodal styles, though differing from Catholic sacramental and ministerial Tradition (para 36).
Lutheran: Synodal government suits community life; faithful participate via common priesthood, electing ministers and guarding Gospel fidelity – often exercised by civil rulers historically.
Reformed (Calvin): Four ministries (pastors, teachers, presbyters, deacons); presbyters represent baptismal dignity; assemblies include majority lay faithful.
Anglican: Synods operate at all levels; “synodically governed but episcopally led” expresses synergy between bishop’s charism and community’s Spirit gift (para 36).
 8: Vatican I defines primacy; 19th-century voices call for renewal
Vatican I (1869–1870) taught papal primacy and infallibility, presenting Roman bishop’s ministry as ensuring episcopal unity for People of God’s faith (para 37).
Ex cathedra definitions stand irreformable in themselves, yet consensus Ecclesiae remains vital – shown in consultations like those by Pius IX (Immaculate Conception) and Pius XII (Assumption) (para 37).
From the 19th century, prophetic thinkers – Johann Adam Möhler, Antonio Rosmini, John Henry Newman – urged return to Scripture and Tradition sources, stressing communion and ordered synodality involving sensus fidei fidelium alongside bishops and pope (para 38).
Ecumenical openings and modern participation awareness deepened calls for synodal Church experience (para 38).
 9: New institutions emerge before Vatican II
From mid-19th century, episcopal conferences arose as collegial gatherings in nations or regions, responding to geopolitics (para 39).
Leo XIII convoked a plenary Latin American Council (1899). Growing awareness recognised whole Church (not just pastors) as Tradition’s subject, with lay active role in apostolic faith transmission (para 39).
These prepared Vatican II’s renewal.
Putting it all together
The Second Millennium shows synodality adapting amid divergences, centralisations, crises, and revivals. East preserved ancient continuity; West navigated primacy growth, schisms, reforms, and hierarchical accents, yet monastic, conciliar, and prophetic impulses kept communal walking alive – setting the stage for Vatican II’s synthesis.

SYNODALITY IN THE EARLY CHURCH

INTRODUCTION 
Connecting to Previous Sessions
We’ve seen:
•	Old Testament: Qahal/edah - assembly of God’s people
•	New Testament: Acts 15 as the paradigm for synodal discernment
•	The Need: Recovery of synodality lost in Second Millennium
Now we examine: How the early Church (1st to 8th centuries) actually lived synodality in practice.
Key Theme
Synodality was the normal way the Church functioned in the first centuries. It wasn’t theory—it was lived experience in structures and processes.

PART 1: THE WITNESS OF THE FATHERS (2nd-3rd CENTURIES)
A. Ignatius of Antioch (Early 2nd Century - died c. 107 AD)
Who He Was:
•	Bishop of Antioch
•	Wrote seven letters while being taken to Rome for martyrdom
•	Earliest witness after the Apostles to Church structure
His Synodal Understanding of Local Churches:
1. All Baptized Are “Synodoi” (Companions on the Journey)
From his Letter to the Ephesians (IX, 2):
Ignatius says all members of the community in Ephesus are σύνοδοι (synodoi) - “companions on the journey.”
The Basis:
•	By virtue of the dignity of baptism
•	By their friendship with Christ
Key Point: This is NOT just about bishops or clergy. ALL baptized are walking together!
2. The Divine Order Makes the Church a Single Body
Ignatius stressed:
•	The divine order that makes the Church a single body
•	Called to sing praise of unity with the Father in Christ Jesus
His Vision: The Church is like a choir - many voices singing in harmony.
3. The Structure He Describes:
From various letters:
The Bishop:
•	Presides over the community
•	Center of unity
The College of Presbyters:
•	The Bishop’s council (συμβούλιον - symboulion)
•	Work together with the bishop
The Members:
•	Each according to their role
•	All called to build up the community
Key Teaching: “The college of Presbyters is the Bishop’s council and the members of the community, in their various roles, are all called to build it up.”
4. The Eucharistic Synaxis as Center
Ecclesial community is created and at its clearest in:
•	The eucharistic synaxis (assembly)
•	Presided over by the Bishop
This Feeds:
•	The conviction that at the end of history
•	God will gather in His Kingdom
•	All the communities that now live and celebrate it in faith
Summary of Ignatius:
Distinctive characteristics of the true Church:
1.	Fidelity to the teaching of the Apostles
2.	Celebration of Eucharist under guidance of Bishop (successor of Apostles)
3.	Ordered exercise of ministries
4.	Primacy of communion in mutual service
5.	Praise and glory of God the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit
B. Cyprian of Carthage (Mid-3rd Century - c. 200-258 AD)
Who He Was:
•	Bishop of Carthage (North Africa)
•	Heir and interpreter of Ignatius’s tradition
•	Martyr under Roman persecution
His Episcopal and Synodal Principle:
Cyprian formulated the clearest statement of synodal governance in the early Church.
The Triple Formula:
From his Letter (Epistula 14, 4):
Cyprian states that in the local Church:
1.	“Nihil sine episcopo” - Nothing without the bishop
2.	“Nihil sine consilio vestro” - Nothing without your council (the Presbyters and Deacons)
3.	“Sine consensu plebis” - Without the consent of the people
What This Means:
THREE LEVELS must work together:
•	The Bishop (one)
•	The council of Presbyters and Deacons (some)
•	The consent of the people (all)
All three are necessary!
The Universal Level:
From “De catholicae ecclesiae unitate” (On the Unity of the Catholic Church), 5:
Cyprian wrote: “Episcopatus unus est cuius a singulis in solidum pars tenetur”
Translation: “The episcopate is one, of which each member has an undivided share in it.”
Meaning:
•	There is ONE episcopate
•	Each bishop holds an undivided part of it
•	All bishops together form one college
•	Unity of the episcopate guarantees unity of the Church
Summary of Cyprian’s Principle:
At Local Level:
•	Bishop doesn’t act alone (nihil sine episcopo)
•	Presbyters/Deacons must be consulted (nihil sine consilio vestro)
•	People must give consent (sine consensu plebis)
At Universal Level:
•	Episcopate is one
•	Each bishop shares in it
•	All must work in solidarity (in solidum)
This is the clearest formula for synodality in the early Church!

PART 2: DEVELOPMENT OF SYNODAL STRUCTURES (4th-8th CENTURIES) 
A. Fourth Century Developments
1. Ecclesiastical Provinces Established
What They Were:
•	Groups of dioceses in a region
•	Headed by a Metropolitan (bishop of the main city)
•	Provincial synods held regularly
Purpose:
•	Demonstrate communion between local Churches
•	Promote unity in the region
•	Handle common questions
2. Provincial Synods - Specific Instruments for Synodality
Definition: Assemblies of bishops and other clergy of a province to deliberate on common matters.
Canon 6 of the Council of Nicaea (325):
Recognized the πρεσβεία (presbeia) - pre-eminence and regional primacy of certain sees:
•	Rome
•	Alexandria
•	Antioch
Later Additions:
Council of Constantinople I (381):
•	Canon 3 added Constantinople to the list
•	Gave it “honorary presidency” after the Bishop of Rome
Council of Chalcedon (451):
•	Confirmed Constantinople’s position
•	Added Jerusalem to the principal sees
The Pentarchy:
These five apostolic sees formed what the East called the Pentarchy:
1.	Rome
2.	Constantinople
3.	Alexandria
4.	Antioch
5.	Jerusalem
For the East: This pentarchy is the form and guarantee of synodality and communion.
For the West: Rome is not seen as one patriarchate among others, but has specific primacy at the heart of the universal Church.
B. Apostolic Canon 34 (Late 3rd Century)
This Canon was well-known in the East and established fundamental synodal principles.
The Text (from the Document):
“The Bishops of each nation (ἔθνος - ethnos) must recognise the one who is first (πρῶτος - protos) among them and consider him their head (κεφαλή - kephale), doing nothing significant without his agreement (γνώμη - gnome)... but the first among them (πρῶτος - protos) must do nothing without the consensus of all.”
Key Principles:
3. The Role of the “Protos” (First):
•	Each region has one recognized as “first”
•	He is considered the “head”
•	His agreement (gnome) is needed for significant decisions
4. Mutual Dependence:
•	Other bishops do nothing significant without the protos
•	BUT the protos does nothing without consensus of all
This is true synodality: Neither the one nor the many act alone!
The Purpose:
“The action of a Synod in concord (ὁμονοία - homonoia) brought into being in this way by the Church is for the glorification of God the Father through Christ in the Holy Spirit.”
The Protos’s Role:
•	Convoke the Synod
•	Preside at the Synod
•	At appropriate level (provincial, metropolitan, eventually patriarchal)
•	To deal with common questions
•	Issue required solutions
•	By virtue of the authority (ἐξουσία - exousia) of the Lord
•	Expressed by Bishops gathered in Synod
C. Local, Provincial, and Ecumenical Levels
The Document Distinguishes Three Levels:
5. Diocesan/Local Synods
From 3rd century onwards:
•	Dealt with disciplinary matters
•	Dealt with cultic (liturgical) matters
•	Dealt with doctrinal matters
•	That had arisen locally
6. Provincial Synods
Regular gatherings at regional level:
•	Bishops of various dioceses in a province
•	Presbyters and monks could be invited to contribute
•	Dealt with matters affecting the whole region
7. Ecumenical Councils
First Millennium - Seven Ecumenical Councils:
•	Only Bishops took part (as voting members)
•	Represented the universal Church
•	Dealt with matters of universal significance
Key Point about Decisions:
“Although the Synods celebrated periodically from the third century onwards at diocesan and provincial levels dealt with disciplinary, cultic and doctrinal matters that had arisen locally, there is a firm conviction that the decisions taken were an expression of communion with all the Churches.”
This Ecclesial Sense Shows:
•	Each local Church is an expression of the one Catholic Church
•	Shown by: 
o	Sharing synodal letters
o	Sending lists of canons to other Churches
o	Requesting mutual recognition between sees
o	Exchange of delegations (often dangerous journeys)
D. The Church of Rome’s Special Role
The Document States Clearly:
“From the beginning, the Church of Rome has enjoyed particular respect, by virtue of the fact that the Apostles Peter - the Bishop of Rome is recognised as his successor - and Paul were martyred there.”
Why Rome Was Consulted:
Several Factors:
1.	Apostolic Faith: The apostolic faith firmly held there
2.	Ministry of Authority: The ministry of authority exercised by her Bishop in service of communion between Churches
3.	Rich History: Rich history of organizing synods
Result: “These all make the Church of Rome a reference-point for all Churches, which all consult her to resolve controversies, so that she functions as a See of appeal.”
Administrative and Canonical Model:
“Furthermore, in the West the See of Rome has become the organisational prototype of other Churches both administratively and canonically.”

PART 3: THE FIRST ECUMENICAL COUNCIL - NICAEA 325 
A. The Historical Context
The Situation:
•	Christianity now legal in Roman Empire (313 AD - Edict of Milan)
•	Major theological controversy: Arianism
•	Arius taught Christ was a creature, not fully divine
•	Threatened unity of Church and Empire
The Convocation:
325 AD - Emperor Constantine convokes the Council:
•	Held at Nicaea (near Constantinople)
•	First time bishops from across the empire could gather freely
Who Attended:
The document states: “Those present included bishops from various regions of the East and Legates of the Bishop of Rome.”
Key Point: Rome participated through representatives (legates), showing its primacy.
B. What Nicaea Did
Two Main Outcomes:
1. Profession of Faith - The Nicene Creed
•	Defined Christ as “consubstantial” (homoousios) with the Father
•	Against Arian heresy
•	This creed still used today
2. Canonical Decisions - Church discipline and organization
•	Canon 6: Recognized primacy of Rome, Alexandria, Antioch
•	Other canons on church order
Recognition as Normative:
“The Council’s profession of faith and its canonical decisions are recognised as having a normative value for the whole Church.”
The Document Notes: “Despite adverse reactions, which has been the case on other occasions throughout history, too.”
Not everyone accepted immediately - there was resistance and controversy for decades. But eventually the whole Church received Nicaea.
C. Theological Significance of Nicaea
The Document’s Assessment:
“With the Bishops exercising their ministry synodally, the Council of Nicaea was the first institutional expression, on the universal level, of the ἐξουσία (exousia) of the risen Lord guiding and directing the way forward for the People of God in the Holy Spirit.”
What This Means:
•	Not just human assembly
•	Christ’s authority (exousia) exercised
•	Through bishops gathered synodally
•	Guided by the Holy Spirit
•	For the whole People of God
Pattern for Future Councils:
Similar experiences in successive Councils in the First Millennium gave authentic shape to the identity of the one Catholic Church.
D. Requirements for Ecumenical Authority
The Document States:
In the Councils of the First Millennium, there was gradual clarification of the attitude required for exercising authority of an Ecumenical Council:
Four Requirements (All Greek Terms):
1.	Συμφωνία (symphonia) - Symphony/harmony of the heads of the various Churches
2.	Συνεργεία (synergia) - Synergy/cooperation of the Bishop of Rome
3.	Συμφρόνησις (symphronesis) - Common mind/thinking together of the other Patriarchs
4.	Agreement with Previous Councils - Its teaching must accord with preceding Councils
All Four Are Necessary for True Ecumenical Authority!
This shows synodality requires:
•	Unity among bishops (symphonia)
•	Participation of Rome (synergia)
•	Common discernment (symphronesis)
•	Continuity with Tradition (agreement)

PART 4: SYNODAL PROCEDURES IN THE FIRST MILLENNIUM
A. Modus Procedendi - How Synods Were Actually Conducted
The Document Notes:
“Local Synods in the First Millennium, on the one hand, followed the Apostolic Tradition and, on the other hand, as far as practical procedures were concerned, were influenced by the culture of the place where they were held.”
Key Insight: Synodal procedures adapted to local cultures while maintaining apostolic principles.
B. Who Participated at Different Levels?
1. Local Church (Diocesan) Synods:
The Principle: “In principle the whole community took part, each grouping in accordance with its respective role.”
This Means:
•	Not just clergy
•	The whole community participated
•	But in ordered ways according to roles
Evidence from Church Fathers:
Origen (3rd century) - “Dialogus cum Heraclio” IV, 24: Shows lay people present at local synods
Cyprian’s Practice in North Africa:
From his letters:
Epistula 17, 3: Shows consultation with laity
Epistula 19, 2: Includes people in decisions
Epistula 30, 5: Seeks consent of people
About the Synod of Carthage (256 AD): The record states it took place “praesente etiam plebis maxima parte” - “with also a very large part of the people present.”
From Sententiae episcoporum numero LXXXVII (Sentences of 87 Bishops): Shows the people were present.
Epistula 17, 3 Shows Cyprian’s Method:
Cyprian intended to make his decision:
•	In agreement with the whole plebs (people)
•	While acknowledging special value of his coepiscopi (fellow bishops)
This Shows the Pattern:
•	People present and consulted
•	Bishops have special role in decision
•	But decisions made in communion with people
2. Provincial Synods:
Participants:
•	Bishops of the various Churches (primary)
•	Presbyters could be invited to contribute
•	Monks could be invited to contribute
But: Only bishops had decision-making authority
3. Ecumenical Councils (First Millennium):
Only Bishops took part (as voting members)
The Pattern for First Millennium:
“It was mainly diocesan and provincial Synods that shaped the synodal procedure adopted in the First Millennium.”
C. Cultural Influences on Procedure
The Document Gives Specific Examples:
4. Africa:
Evidence of procedure of:
•	Roman Senate
•	Concilia municipalia (municipal councils)
Example: Council of Carthage (256)
5. Italy:
Used procedural methods known through imperial government
Example: Council of Aquileia (381)
6. Kingdom of Visigoths and Franks:
Synods reflected political procedures known there
Example: 7th century Ordo de celebrando Concilio (Order for Celebrating a Council)
Key Point: While maintaining apostolic tradition, synods adapted procedures to local cultural contexts. This is inculturation of synodal practice.

PART 5: ROLE OF DIFFERENT GROUPS
A. Role of Bishops
Primary Responsibility:
Bishops had the primary role in synods, especially at provincial and ecumenical levels.
Their Authority:
Based on:
•	Apostolic succession
•	Sacramental ordination
•	Teaching authority
•	Pastoral responsibility for their local Church
How They Exercised It Synodally:
Apostolic Canon 34 Pattern:
•	The protos (first) convokes and presides
•	But does nothing without consensus of all bishops
•	Other bishops do nothing without agreement of protos
At Ecumenical Councils:
From the Document on Nicaea and Later Councils:
“With the Bishops exercising their ministry synodally...”
This Means:
•	Not individually but together
•	In communion with each other
•	Under the Holy Spirit’s guidance
•	For the good of the whole Church
B. Role of Presbyters and Deacons
The “Council” of the Bishop:
Cyprian’s Formula: “nihil sine consilio vestro”
•	The Bishop’s council (consilium)
•	Composed of Presbyters and Deacons
At Diocesan Synods:
Presbyters and Deacons:
•	Present and participating
•	Offering counsel to Bishop
•	Helping implement decisions
At Provincial Synods:
•	Could be invited to contribute
•	Offered expertise and local knowledge
•	Did not have voting authority (that belonged to bishops)
The Principle:
The presbyterium (body of priests) worked collegially with the Bishop in governance of the local Church.
C. Role of Local Communities/Laity
The Surprising Reality:
The document makes clear that laity participated more than we often think!
Evidence Already Cited:
1. Cyprian’s Principle: “sine consensu plebis” - without consent of the people
2. Synod of Carthage (256): “with a very large part of the people present”
3. Various Letters of Cyprian: Show consultation with and consent of people
The Pattern:
At Diocesan/Local Level:
•	Whole community gathered
•	People present throughout
•	Gave consent to decisions
•	Helped in selection of clergy
Document Quote:
“In the case of a Synod in a local Church, in principle the whole community took part, each grouping in accordance with its respective role.”
Important Qualification:
Roles were distinct:
•	Laity present and consulted
•	Gave consent
•	But bishops made final decisions
•	This is ordered participation, not democracy
The Theology Behind It:
From Ignatius and Cyprian:
•	ALL baptized are companions on journey (synodoi)
•	ALL have dignity from baptism
•	Church is communion of all
•	Therefore all participate according to their role
D. Summary of Roles
The Three Levels Working Together:
In Cyprian’s Formula:
1.	Bishop (one) - “nihil sine episcopo” 
o	Presides
o	Makes final decision
o	Ensures apostolic fidelity
2.	Presbyters/Deacons (some) - “nihil sine consilio vestro” 
o	Form bishop’s council
o	Offer expertise
o	Help implement
3.	People (all) - “sine consensu plebis” 
o	Give consent
o	Participate in discernment
o	Receive and live the decisions
Each Level is Necessary!
This is the early Church’s answer to how “all, some, and one” work together.

PART 6: MONASTIC AND COMMUNAL DISCERNMENT TRADITIONS
A. Monastic Communities as Synodal Models
The Document’s Reference:
“Throughout the Middle Ages, there were examples of revitalisation of Synodal procedure in the widest sense of the term. The Monks of Cluny are one case.”
Why Monasteries Matter for Synodality:
1. They Maintained Communal Life
When broader Church structures weakened, monasteries preserved:
•	Common life
•	Communal prayer
•	Shared discernment
•	Ordered participation
2. Cathedral Chapters
The document notes: “Cathedral Chapters helped to keep synodal procedure alive.”
What They Were:
•	Community of clergy serving cathedral
•	Lived in common
•	Advised the bishop
•	Elected bishops in many places
3. The Mendicant Orders
“As did the new communities of religious life, particularly the Mendicant Orders.”
Who They Were:
•	Franciscans (founded 1209)
•	Dominicans (founded 1216)
•	Later: Augustinians, Carmelites
Their Synodal Structure:
The document describes their organization:
“Their convents were arranged in provinces and were subject to a Superior General, whose jurisdiction extended to all members of the Order.”
Three Key Synodal Features:
4. Election of Superiors:
“The Superiors of the Order – the general, the provincial and those of individual convents – were elected by representatives of the members of the Order for a determined period.”
Not hereditary or appointed from above - elected by community members!
5. Limited Terms:
“For a determined period” - not for life
This prevented concentration of power.
6. Councils/Chapters:
Superiors “were assisted in the exercise of their authority by a Chapter or Council.”
What This Means:
Even in religious orders with vowed obedience:
•	Communities elected leaders
•	Leaders had councils
•	Decisions made communally
•	Limited terms prevented abuse
This Kept Synodal Spirit Alive during periods when broader Church structures became more hierarchical.
B. The Principle of Communal Discernment
Though Not Explicitly Detailed in the Document:
The monastic tradition preserved practices that informed synodality:
From Monastic Rules:
7. Rule of St. Benedict (6th century):
Though not quoted in this document, Benedict’s Rule was influential and taught:
•	Abbot must consult community on important matters
•	Even youngest members may have wisdom
•	Decision made after listening to all
8. Common Prayer and Lectio Divina:
Monasteries practiced:
•	Praying together (Divine Office)
•	Reading Scripture together (lectio divina)
•	Discerning God’s will as community
9. Chapters (Community Meetings):
Regular gatherings where:
•	Community business discussed
•	Corrections given and received
•	Decisions made together
The Contribution to Synodality:
These practices showed:
•	Listening to Holy Spirit together
•	Communal discernment possible
•	Authority exercised in communion
•	Balance of personal leadership and communal participation
C. Why the Document Mentions These
The Context in the Document:
After describing how synodal procedure weakened in the Middle Ages, the document notes these communities as exceptions - places where synodal life continued.
The Lesson:
Even in difficult periods:

These monastic and mendicant models influenced:

SYNTHESIS AND SUMMARY
The Pattern of Synodality in the Early Church
1. CLEAR THEOLOGICAL FOUNDATIONS
From Ignatius:
•	All baptized are synodoi (companions)
•	Church is communion centered on Eucharist
•	Ordered structure: bishop, presbyters, people
From Cyprian:
•	Triple formula: bishop, council, consent of people
•	Episcopate is one, shared by all bishops
•	Unity in solidarity (in solidum)
2. DEVELOPED STRUCTURES
Three Levels:
Local (Diocesan):
•	Whole community participated
•	Bishop presided
•	Presbyters/deacons as council
•	People gave consent
Regional (Provincial):
•	Bishops of province
•	Metropolitan as protos (first)
•	Regular synods
•	Presbyters/monks invited
Universal (Ecumenical):
•	All bishops (represented universal Church)
•	Bishop of Rome’s synergy essential
•	Dealt with universal questions
•	Required: symphonia, synergia, symphronesis
3. CLEAR PROCEDURES
Apostolic Canon 34:
•	Protos convokes and presides
•	Nothing significant without his agreement
•	But he does nothing without consensus of all
Cultural Adaptation:
•	Procedures adapted to local cultures
•	While maintaining apostolic principles
•	Examples: Roman, Italian, Visigothic influences
4. ROLES WERE DISTINCT BUT COMPLEMENTARY
Bishops:
•	Apostolic authority
•	Final decisions
•	Teaching role
•	But always synodally (together)
Presbyters/Deacons:
•	Bishop’s council
•	Offered expertise
•	Helped implementation
Laity:
•	Present at local synods
•	Gave consent
•	Participated in discernment
•	Especially in selection of clergy
5. COMMUNION EXPRESSED AT ALL LEVELS
Local Churches:
•	Sent letters to other churches
•	Shared canonical decisions
•	Requested mutual recognition
•	Exchanged delegations
Sign of Recognition:
•	Each local Church is expression of one Catholic Church
•	Decisions made locally affect universal Church
•	Universal communion maintained through synodal communication
6. SPECIAL ROLE OF ROME
From the Beginning:
•	Particular respect
•	Reference-point for resolving controversies
•	See of appeal
•	Organizational model for West
•	But this primacy exercised within synodal framework
7. MONASTIC PRESERVATION
When Broader Structures Weakened:
•	Monasteries kept communal life
•	Mendicant orders maintained elections
•	Cathedral chapters preserved consultation
•	These became sources of renewal

KEY PRINCIPLES FOR TEACHING
What Students Must Understand:
1. THIS WAS NORMAL PRACTICE
Synodality wasn’t theory - it was how the Church actually functioned in first centuries.
2. EVERYONE PARTICIPATED - BUT DIFFERENTLY
•	Not everyone had same role
•	But everyone had a role
•	Ordered participation, not chaos or democracy
3. CYPRIAN’S FORMULA IS KEY
“Nothing without the bishop, nothing without your council, nothing without the consent of the people”
This is the clearest summary of early Church synodality.
4. STRUCTURES WERE CLEAR
•	Diocesan synods
•	Provincial synods
•	Ecumenical councils
•	Each with defined procedures
5. CULTURAL ADAPTATION HAPPENED
•	Procedures adapted to local contexts
•	But apostolic principles maintained
•	This is legitimate inculturation
6. COMMUNION WAS THE GOAL
All synodal structures aimed at:
•	Maintaining communion between Churches
•	Expressing unity of faith
•	Building up the Body of Christ
•	Glorifying the Trinity
7. ROME’S ROLE WAS RECOGNIZED
From the beginning, Rome had special role:
•	But exercised within communion
•	Not apart from synodal structures
•	As reference point and appeal

CONCLUSION
The early Church (1st-8th centuries) shows us that synodality is not a modern invention. It was the normal way the Church lived and made decisions.
Key Features:
•	Clear structures at every level
•	Everyone participated according to their role
•	Bishops exercised authority synodally (together)
•	Laity gave consent and participated
•	Procedures adapted to cultures
•	Communion maintained between Churches
•	Rome had special role within communion
This Pattern:
•	Rooted in Scripture (Acts 15)
•	Developed in Tradition (Fathers)
•	Expressed in Structures (Synods/Councils)
•	Preserved in Communities (Monasteries)
The Challenge Today: Can we recover this early Church practice for our time?
